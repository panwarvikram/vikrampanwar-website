Sanjeev Arora v. Enforcement Directorate: P&H High Court Says the ED Can Report a Cognizable Offence Found in a FEMA Search to the Police, and the Resulting FIR Can Anchor a PMLA Case

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab and Haryana High Court — Sanjeev Arora v. Directorate of Enforcement
BenchJustice Kuldeep Tiwari (single judge)
Date16 October 2026 (reported 17 October 2026)
CitationCRM-M-34651-2026 (no official citation yet)
Key SectionsPMLA Sections 19, 45, 50, 66; FEMA Section 37; IPC 420 (now BNS 318(4))

What the case was about

Sanjeev Arora, a Punjab minister and former chairman and managing director of Hampton Sky Realty Limited (HSRL), was arrested by the Enforcement Directorate on 9 May 2026 in a money-laundering case. The ED’s allegation is that HSRL showed fictitious exports of iPhones worth about ₹102.50 crore to two UAE entities and, on the strength of those exports, drew an IGST refund of roughly ₹15.63 crore.

The sequence of events is what made the case legally interesting. On 17 April 2026 the ED searched HSRL under Section 37 of FEMA, read with Section 132 of the Income Tax Act. During that search an ED Assistant Director complained to the Gurugram Police, and an FIR for cheating, forgery and conspiracy was registered at Police Station Udyog Vihar in the early hours of 18 April. Only on 5 May did the ED record its own ECIR, treating that FIR as the scheduled (predicate) offence. Arora argued that no ECIR existed when the complaint was made, that FEMA has no equivalent of Section 66 PMLA allowing such information-sharing, and that the FIR, registered within about 82 minutes of the general diary entry, was manufactured to give the ED jurisdiction under PMLA.

What the High Court held

Justice Kuldeep Tiwari of the Punjab and Haryana High Court rejected the bail plea on 16 October 2026. The reasoning, as reported, runs along these lines.

  • Reporting a cognizable offence needs no special provision. An officer who comes across a cognizable offence during a FEMA search may inform the local police. FEMA does not forbid this, and once the information discloses a cognizable offence the police are obliged to register an FIR. That FIR can validly be the predicate offence for a PMLA case. The Court leaned on the Supreme Court’s decision in Anil Tuteja v. Union of India on how different agencies’ investigations run on separate tracks.
  • Mala fides not made out at this stage. Given the material already collected, it was premature to hold that the FIR was registered only to create a predicate offence.
  • Cheating the revenue can be a scheduled offence. Relying on Ishwarlal Girdharilal Parekh v. State of Maharashtra, the Court held that inducing a tax officer to pass a refund order can amount to delivering a “valuable security” for cheating, so the alleged IGST refund prima facie made out a scheduled offence.
  • Section 45 twin conditions apply. Following Vijay Madanlal Choudhary, the Court held the twin conditions bind even a constitutional court deciding bail, and Arora did not satisfy them.
  • Arrest powers. A Section 50 summons is not a precondition for arrest under Section 19, the two being separate powers; the officer’s “reason to believe” rested on evasive answers during the search and banking and GST material.
  • Evidence of “trade-based money laundering.” The Court pointed to 123 identical IMEI numbers appearing in two export consignments, handsets activated in India before and after their supposed export, 1,249 handsets traced to 862 retail buyers with no link to HSRL, and tier-2 suppliers tied to a single mobile number. Staff were reported to have admitted that no phones were physically received at the office.
  • Medical grounds. Bail was also refused on health grounds, the Supreme Court having earlier dismissed his SLP after the ED assured proper medical care, and his condition being stable.

Reports differ slightly on how the proceeding is described: some outlets call it a regular bail application, while one reports it as a challenge to the arrest itself. The reasoning above comes from the more detailed report, and the order should be read before it is cited.

Why this matters for Criminal practitioners in Haryana

Many PMLA matters in this region begin with a local FIR for cheating or forgery that a central agency later adopts as its predicate offence. The common defence has been that the FIR was engineered by the agency. This order makes that argument harder: the High Court treats inter-agency information-sharing as routine, and demands proof of actual mala fides rather than inference from timing alone.

For defence counsel, the practical takeaways are to challenge the substance of the scheduled offence rather than the sequence of paperwork, to engage with the electronic and banking material on which the ED relies, and to remember that the Section 45 conditions are not diluted by approaching a High Court. For anyone advising on FEMA or customs searches, the ruling is a reminder that evidence found in one regime can feed a criminal case in another.

District-court lawyers will also see the downstream effect: once an FIR is registered on an agency’s complaint, the local police and the magistrate cannot treat it as suspect merely because the complainant is a central officer.

Statutory cross-references

The IPC offence of cheating (Section 420) corresponds to Section 318(4) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, forgery provisions now sit in Chapter XIV BNS, and criminal conspiracy is Section 61 BNS. The reports do not name the exact BNS sections in the Gurugram FIR, so these are indicative mappings only. Registration of an FIR on information of a cognizable offence, earlier Section 154 CrPC, is now Section 173 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. The PMLA provisions discussed (Sections 19, 45, 50 and 66) are unchanged by the new codes.
