Arrest Memo Cannot Be Equated With Grounds Of Arrest: P&H High Court Grants NDPS Bail In a 10-Kilogram Drone-Heroin Case

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseP&H High Court — NDPS bail petition, woman accused in a cross-border drone-heroin recovery (case title as reported by LiveLaw; full cause title not accessible)
BenchJustice Virinder Aggarwal
DateReported 24 July 2026
CitationNeutral/LiveLaw citation number not independently confirmed at the time of writing (report behind LiveLaw's premium paywall) — court, bench, date and holding verified against LiveLaw's published report
Key SectionsArt. 22(1), Constitution of India; Ss. 37 & 50, NDPS Act, 1985; S. 47, BNSS, 2023

What the case was about

The case arose out of one of the more serious categories of NDPS prosecution now reaching Punjab and Haryana's courts: contraband dropped by drone across the international border with Pakistan. The accused, a woman, was booked after the recovery of over 10 kilograms of heroin allegedly delivered this way — a commercial quantity many multiples over the threshold that attracts the NDPS Act's most stringent bail bar under Section 37. In ordinary circumstances, an accused facing a recovery of this scale would have an uphill battle for bail, since Section 37 requires the court to be satisfied that there are reasonable grounds to believe the accused is not guilty and is unlikely to commit an offence while on bail — a threshold courts apply cautiously in large-quantity cases.

The petitioner's bail plea did not contest the quantity or the recovery on merits. Instead, it attacked the legality of the arrest itself, arguing that the police had never actually communicated to her, or to a relative nominated by her, the specific grounds on which she was being arrested — as opposed to the fact of arrest and the offence she was booked under, which is typically all an arrest memo records. This is a live and recurring issue in NDPS practice: an arrest memo tells an accused that they have been arrested and under which sections; "grounds of arrest" is a distinct, more substantive disclosure of the actual reasons and material justifying the arrest, and the Supreme Court has increasingly treated the latter as a separate constitutional requirement rather than a formality satisfied by the former.

What the court held

Justice Virinder Aggarwal accepted the petitioner's argument and granted bail, holding that, prima facie, the arrest appeared to have been effected without the grounds of arrest being communicated to her or to her nominated relative, as required under Article 22(1) of the Constitution and the corresponding statutory safeguards. The Court's reasoning rests on the now well-established distinction between an arrest memo — a standard-form document recording that an arrest has occurred, the time, and the offence — and grounds of arrest, which must set out the specific basis for the arrest in a manner that allows the arrested person to effectively consult a lawyer and challenge the arrest or seek bail. Simply producing an arrest memo at the time of arrest, without more, does not discharge the constitutional obligation to communicate grounds.

This is significant because it shows the doctrine being applied even in a high-stakes commercial-quantity NDPS case involving cross-border narco-terrorism concerns, where courts have historically been reluctant to grant bail on anything but the strongest merits-based grounds. By granting bail purely on the constitutional infirmity in the arrest process — without needing to weigh the Section 37 "reasonable grounds" threshold on the merits of the recovery itself — the order reinforces that a defective arrest can unravel even a strong prosecution case on the papers, because the safeguard is treated as going to the legality of custody itself, not merely a procedural irregularity that can be cured after the fact.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

This blog covered the Supreme Court's broader "written grounds of arrest" doctrine in an earlier piece tracing its evolution from Pankaj Bansal's PMLA ruling through Vihaan Kumar's case to a universal mandate for every arrest. This P&H High Court order is a useful, very recent illustration of that doctrine being applied at the ground level in Haryana and Punjab's own courts, in exactly the kind of serious NDPS matter where the stakes for both prosecution and defence are highest. For defence counsel, the lesson is procedural discipline at the earliest possible stage: on taking up any NDPS or other serious matter, the first document to scrutinise is not just the arrest memo but whether a separate, substantive communication of grounds was made to the accused and a nominated relative, and whether that communication is verifiable on the record — because its absence can be argued as a freestanding ground for bail, independent of the strength of the recovery evidence.

For prosecutors and investigating agencies, the order is a pointed reminder that in the post-Pankaj Bansal, post-Vihaan Kumar legal landscape, an arrest memo is simply not a substitute for a written and communicated grounds-of-arrest document, however routine that substitution may have been in past practice. Given how consistently High Courts and the Supreme Court have now enforced this distinction, investigating officers in Haryana's own NDPS, UAPA and other serious cases should treat separate, documented communication of grounds as a non-negotiable step, not paperwork to be reconstructed after a bail challenge is filed.

Statutory cross-references

The order turns on Article 22(1) of the Constitution of India, Section 37 of the NDPS Act, 1985 (the bail bar for commercial quantities), and the arrest and remand safeguards under the erstwhile Section 50A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, now Section 47 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which governs the obligation to inform a friend or relative of the arrest and the place of custody. The doctrine on written, communicated grounds of arrest as a distinct constitutional requirement traces through Pankaj Bansal v. Union of India, 2023 INSC 866, and Vihaan Kumar v. State of Haryana, 2025 INSC 162, both discussed in this blog's earlier article on the subject.
