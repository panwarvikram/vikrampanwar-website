Amritpal Kaur @ Amrit Kaur v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Grants NDPS Bail In a 10-Kilogram Drone-Heroin Case, Holding an Arrest Memo Is Not Grounds Of Arrest

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseAmritpal Kaur @ Amrit Kaur v. State of Punjab, CRM-M-7299-2026 (O&M)
BenchJustice Virinder Aggarwal
Date22 July 2026
Citation2026:PHHC:100294
Key SectionsArt. 22(1), Constitution of India; S. 52(1), Ss. 21(c), 27-A & 29, NDPS Act, 1985; S. 483, BNSS, 2023

What the case was about

On the night of 21 September 2025, a police party conducting a naka near a railway crossing on Ramdas Road, Dera Baba Nanak, acted on secret information that smugglers were expected to collect a consignment of heroin allegedly dropped by drone from across the border in Village Thetharke, Gurdaspur. Around 2 a.m., two motorcycles carrying four men were intercepted, and a total of 10.02 kilograms of heroin was allegedly recovered from them — 2.06 kg each from three of the men and 4.02 kg from the fourth. FIR No. 143 was registered under Sections 21(c), 27-A and 29 of the NDPS Act, 1985, Sections 10 to 12 of the Aircraft Act, 1934, and Section 317(2) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

The petitioner, Amritpal Kaur, was not named in the FIR itself. She was nominated during investigation on the strength of disclosure statements allegedly made by two of the arrested men, and was arrested on 23 September 2025. No contraband was recovered from her. The sole allegation against her was that she had paid Rs. 40,000 to a co-accused at the instance of her husband, Bachittar Singh, who resides abroad. By the time her bail petition — her first, filed under Section 483 of the BNSS (corresponding to Section 439 CrPC) — came up for hearing, she had spent roughly nine months and twenty-six days in judicial custody, while the State opposed bail citing the commercial quantity involved and the twin-condition bar under Section 37 of the NDPS Act.

What the court held

Rather than wading into the Section 37 merits battle, Justice Virinder Aggarwal focused on the legality of the arrest itself. On examining the arrest memo (Annexure P-6) and the intimation memo sent to her nominated relative (Annexure P-7), the Court found that neither document disclosed that the specific grounds of arrest — as opposed to the general "consequences of the offence" and her right to have a relative informed — had actually been communicated to her or to her father-in-law, Gurdeep Singh, in a language they understood. That, the Court held, fell short of Article 22(1) of the Constitution read with Section 52(1) of the NDPS Act.

The judgment traces this doctrine in detail: Prabir Purkayastha v. State (NCT of Delhi), 2024 INSC 414, first held that grounds of arrest must be communicated in writing with reasonable promptitude; Vihaan Kumar v. State of Haryana clarified that merely informing a relative that an arrest has occurred is not the same as communicating grounds, and that the grounds must go to both the arrestee and the nominated person; the Punjab & Haryana High Court's own decision in Ashish Kakkar v. U.T. Chandigarh, 2025:PHHC:013798, held that an arrest memo cannot, by any interpretation, be equated with grounds of arrest; and Radhika Agarwal v. Union of India, 2025 INSC 272, a three-judge bench, reaffirmed that denial of written grounds violates Article 22(1) regardless of whether prejudice can be shown, following the "prejudice per se" rule from Kishan Chand v. State of Haryana, (2013) 2 SCC 502. Most significantly, the Court relied on Mihir Rajesh Shah v. State of Maharashtra, 2026(1) SCC 500, where the Supreme Court laid down that grounds of arrest must be communicated in writing, in a language the arrestee understands, and — if not possible immediately — at least two hours before the arrestee is produced for remand; non-compliance renders the arrest and remand illegal. A subsequent order in Dr. Rajinder Rajan v. Union of India applied this two-hour rule to strike down an arrest memo prepared in "template format," which is essentially what the Court found here.

Weighing this constitutional infirmity alongside the facts that no recovery was made from the petitioner, that she is a young woman with a two-and-a-half-year-old child in her care, that the trial was at a nascent stage with no realistic prospect of early conclusion, and that she had already spent nearly ten months in custody, the Court granted regular bail, subject to the standard conditions on evidence-tampering, travel, address verification, and disclosure of financial particulars. The Court also noted the line of cases — Priti Kumari v. State of Punjab, Jaya Karmarkar v. State of West Bengal, Soni Devi v. State of West Bengal, and Basanti Mondal v. State of West Bengal — in which the Supreme Court has treated the accused being a woman as a factor warranting distinct consideration in bail.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

This blog's earlier article traced the written-grounds-of-arrest doctrine from Pankaj Bansal's PMLA ruling through Vihaan Kumar. Amritpal Kaur adds the sharpest operational detail yet: Mihir Rajesh Shah's rule that grounds must be furnished in writing at least two hours before the arrestee is produced for remand. That is a concrete, verifiable compliance failure defence counsel in Haryana's own NDPS, UAPA and BNS matters can plead on the record — not by arguing that grounds were never explained at all, but by simply checking whether a distinct written document, separate from the arrest memo, was served within that window. The judgment's ten-point summary of the law (paragraph 7.1 of the order) is a ready checklist for any bail application built on this ground.

The order is also a reminder that a defective arrest can unravel a case that looks strong on the merits — here, a 10-kilogram commercial-quantity drone-heroin seizure with Section 37's stringent bail bar in play — because the Court did not need to reach the "reasonable grounds to believe not guilty" threshold at all once the constitutional infirmity in the arrest was established. For prosecutors and investigating agencies handling NDPS cases along Punjab's and Haryana's border districts, where drone-trafficking prosecutions are becoming routine, the lesson is equally direct: a template arrest memo reciting that "consequences of the offence were explained" is not a substitute for a specific, written, and timely-communicated grounds-of-arrest document, however large or well-evidenced the underlying seizure.

Statutory cross-references

The order turns on Article 22(1) of the Constitution of India and Section 52(1) of the NDPS Act, 1985 (communication of grounds of arrest), read against Section 37 of the NDPS Act (the twin-condition bail bar for commercial quantities) and Section 483 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which replaces Section 439 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 for regular bail applications. The FIR itself invoked Section 317(2) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, alongside Sections 10 to 12 of the Aircraft Act, 1934, reflecting how drone-delivery NDPS cases now routinely combine narcotics, aviation and BNS charges in a single FIR.
