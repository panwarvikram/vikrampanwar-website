Chavan Dharmendar v. State of Telangana (2026 INSC 1133): Supreme Court Says Breach of RBI Guidelines Cannot Be Prosecuted as an SC/ST Act Offence Without Specific Caste-Based Allegations

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Chavan Dharmendar & Ors. v. State of Telangana & Ors., Criminal Appeal No. 4930 of 2026
BenchJustice J.B. Pardiwala and Justice K. Vinod Chandran
Date12 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1133 (non-reportable)
Key SectionsSection 3(1)(q), SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989; RBI MSME revival framework

What the case was about

A small enterprise took credit facilities from the State Bank of India from 2010. By its own account it suffered a capital loss in 2012–13, and its accounts were classified as non-performing assets in 2016, although it said it had repaid a term loan and paid ₹45 lakh to regularise the account. In February 2019 the company lodged FIR No. 1 of 2019 at Police Station CID, Hyderabad against several bank officials.

The allegations were broad: caste-based discrimination against promoters belonging to Scheduled Castes and Tribes, forged documents, false information fed to superiors, an economic boycott of SC/ST-run enterprises, and failure to follow RBI circulars on reviving stressed small units, including denial of a rehabilitation package given to others. The complainant said this attracted Section 3(1)(q) of the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act. The officers answered that the documents in question had been furnished by the company’s directors, who were also guarantors. The Telangana High Court refused to stop the prosecution, reasoning that the officers had a duty to follow the Central Government and RBI revival framework for MSMEs.

What the Court held

A Bench of Justice J.B. Pardiwala and Justice K. Vinod Chandran allowed the officers’ appeal on 12 October 2026 and quashed the FIR and all proceedings arising from it. The order is marked non-reportable. Its reasoning is short and clear.

  • A regulatory duty is not a criminal charge. That bank officers are expected to follow the MSME revival framework does not, without more, justify criminal proceedings against them when they are alleged not to have done so.
  • Breach of RBI guidelines cannot by itself lead to prosecution. Non-compliance with RBI guidelines and circulars cannot result in criminal prosecution merely because the borrower’s directors belong to SC/ST communities.
  • No caste-specific allegation. The complaint did not say that any particular officer denigrated the directors because of their caste, which is the ingredient the Atrocities Act is aimed at.
  • The accounts were NPAs anyway. The Court noted that the accounts had admittedly turned non-performing, so recovery action would have followed regardless of anyone’s caste.
  • A factual slip in the complaint. The company had been described as a small enterprise mainly managed by SC/ST women, yet the Managing Director is not a woman.

Importantly, the Court preserved the company’s civil remedies. Its grievances about RBI circular violations, the denied rehabilitation package and allegedly false information used to trigger coercive recovery can still be raised before the Debts Recovery Tribunal, which will decide whether they are legally sound and whether they affect the bank’s recovery.

Why this matters for criminal practitioners in Haryana

Borrower-versus-bank disputes in our district courts frequently arrive with a criminal case attached, because an FIR is seen as leverage against recovery. This order is a useful authority against that tactic. Defending a bank officer, a recovery agent or a public servant in such a case, you can point out that a complaint which only lists regulatory lapses and then adds the complainant’s caste does not disclose the specific, caste-based act the Atrocities Act requires.

The ruling is equally a caution for complainants. A genuine caste-discrimination complaint needs particulars: who said or did what, when, and why it was because of the complainant’s identity. Where the real grievance is about credit, recovery or rehabilitation, the correct forums are the DRT, the civil court or the banking regulator’s grievance route. Since the order is non-reportable and decided on its own facts, it should be cited with care and read alongside the full text and the settled quashing jurisprudence, not as a rule that SC/ST borrowers lose protection against genuine discrimination.

Statutory cross-references

The special provision invoked was Section 3(1)(q) of the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989, which continues to apply unchanged. The order does not list the Indian Penal Code sections in the FIR; if allegations of forgery or cheating are pleaded in a similar case today, forgery under IPC Sections 463 to 471 now falls under BNS Sections 336 to 340 and cheating under IPC Section 420 under BNS Section 318. A petition to quash that earlier lay under Section 482 CrPC is now filed under Section 528 BNSS.
