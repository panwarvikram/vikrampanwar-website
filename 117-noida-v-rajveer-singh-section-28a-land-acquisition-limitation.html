NOIDA & Anr. v. Rajveer Singh & Ors.: Supreme Court Settles When the Section 28A Limitation Clock Actually Starts

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseNew Okhla Industrial Development Authority & Anr. v. Rajveer Singh & Ors.
BenchJustices J.B. Pardiwala & K. Vinod Chandran
Date21 September 2026
CitationCivil Appeal arising out of SLP (C) No. 18801 of 2025
Key SectionsS. 28A, S. 18 & S. 11, Land Acquisition Act, 1894

What the case was about

In 1977, the government acquired land in Ghaziabad for what would eventually become NOIDA, and paid compensation under an award of the Land Acquisition Officer dated 15 February 1977. The predecessors of Rajveer Singh and the other respondents accepted that compensation without seeking a reference under Section 18 of the Land Acquisition Act, 1894 to have it enhanced. Other landowners covered by the very same Section 4(1) notification did seek a reference; theirs was rejected by the Additional District Judge, Ghaziabad on 25 November 1981. Those landowners appealed, and their First Appeal No. 593 of 1982 was decided by the Allahabad High Court on 8 July 2019, granting the first enhancement of compensation under that notification. A connected First Appeal No. 458 of 1982, involving different landowners under the same notification, was disposed of separately by the High Court on 18 April 2022 — but that 2022 order did nothing more than follow the reasoning already laid down in the 2019 judgment; it enhanced nothing new. Believing the 2022 order gave them a fresh right, Rajveer Singh and the other respondents filed an application under Section 28A of the 1894 Act on 23 May 2022, within three months of that order. The Collector rejected it as not maintainable. A writ petition forced reconsideration, and the matter travelled up through the High Court, which sided with the respondents, and then to the Supreme Court on NOIDA's appeal.

What the court held

Section 28A is a beneficial provision. It lets a landowner who never sought a reference under Section 18 still ride on the higher compensation the Reference Court later awarded to a neighbour covered by the very same acquisition notification, as long as the application is made within three months of that award. Whether that three-month clock can also be restarted by a later appellate order has divided Supreme Court benches for three decades — Union of India v. Pradeep Kumari (1995) read it broadly to include successive Reference Court awards, while Jose Antonio Cruz v. Land Acquisition Collector (1996) confined it to the first award of the Reference Court itself, not an appellate order. Banwari v. HSIIDC (2024) later carved out a narrow exception: where a landowner's own reference was rejected outright with no enhancement at all, the first enhancement granted later in a First Appeal can itself start the clock. Justices Pardiwala and Vinod Chandran held that this exception did not help the respondents here, because the real first enhancement under this notification had already occurred on 8 July 2019, in the connected appeal. That gave the respondents until 7 October 2019 to apply under Section 28A. The 2022 order they actually relied on merely followed the 2019 judgment and created no fresh cause of action. Their application, filed in May 2022, was therefore roughly two-and-a-half years too late — even though it was well within three months of the order they had been tracking. The Court allowed NOIDA's appeal, set aside the High Court's judgment, and rejected the Section 28A application as time-barred, while also disapproving a 2026 two-judge bench ruling that had permitted a second Section 28A application on a "doctrine of merger" theory, calling it inconsistent with the three-judge bench precedents in Pradeep Kumari and Jose Antonio Cruz.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Haryana's district courts see a steady stream of land acquisition disputes tied to HSIIDC industrial estates, HSVP sectors, and national highway and rail corridor projects passing through Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, and Section 28A applications are a routine feature of that practice whenever a notification covers a cluster of landowners with uneven litigation histories. This ruling is a hard warning for that practice: a landowner who skipped their own Section 18 reference cannot simply track the appeal they happen to be following and assume its outcome will start their own limitation clock. They must independently track every award passed on any reference relating to the same Section 4(1) notification, because the three-month period can start — and expire — years before the case they are watching is even decided. For counsel advising clients who accepted a Collector's award without seeking a reference of their own, the practical lesson is to actively monitor parallel litigation under the same notification rather than wait passively for "their" appeal to conclude, since a later, more visible order offers no second chance once an earlier enhancement has already started the clock running.

Statutory cross-references

This case turns entirely on civil land acquisition law, not on any provision carried over into the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita or Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, so no BNS/BNSS mapping applies here. It is worth noting, however, that the Land Acquisition Act, 1894 has been substantially replaced for fresh acquisitions by the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, which uses its own reference mechanism under Section 64 before a dedicated Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority rather than a civil court. Acquisitions initiated before the 2013 Act's commencement, like the 1977 notification in this case, continue to be governed by the savings provisions of the 1894 Act, including Section 28A, which is why this decades-old dispute was still being fought under the old law in 2026.
