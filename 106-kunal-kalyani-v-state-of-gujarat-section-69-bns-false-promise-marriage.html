Kunal Rameshbhai Kalyani v. State of Gujarat: Supreme Court Says a Mother's Objection to a Marriage Doesn't Turn a Genuine Promise Into Section 69 BNS Deceit

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / CaseSupreme Court of India · Kunal Rameshbhai Kalyani v. State of Gujarat & Anr.
BenchJustices J.B. Pardiwala & K. Vinod Chandran
Date7 September 2026
Citation2026 SCC OnLine SC 1818 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 925
Key SectionsS. 69, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 · Ss. 90 & 375, IPC, 1860 (Ss. 28 & 63, BNS, 2023)

What the case was about

The complainant and the appellant met on a digital platform. What began as friendship turned into a relationship, and at their very first physical meeting the appellant told her he wanted to marry her. On that footing, she permitted a physical relationship — the first occasion said to be in February 2024 — and the two later spent two days together at a hotel in April 2024. The relationship eventually broke down when the appellant's mother refused to accept the match, and he backed out of the marriage. More than a year after the first physical relationship, an FIR was registered against him on 20 May 2025 at Sayajiganj Police Station, Vadodara, alleging an offence under Section 69 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

Section 69 BNS is a provision with no direct predecessor in the old Penal Code. It criminalises sexual intercourse obtained through "deceitful means," which the section itself defines to include "a promise to marry a woman without any intention of fulfilling the same." Before the BNS came into force, this fact pattern — sex obtained on a promise of marriage that is later broken — was prosecuted as rape under Section 375 IPC, read with Section 90 IPC's rule that consent given under a misconception of fact is no consent at all. Parliament's decision to carve this conduct out into its own, separately punishable offence under the BNS was the backdrop against which the Supreme Court had to decide whether this FIR could stand.

The appellant approached the Gujarat High Court seeking to have the FIR quashed. The High Court, relying on the Supreme Court's own ruling in Deepak Gulati v. State of Haryana, (2013) 7 SCC 675, declined to interfere. He then appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran began by tracing how the legal test survives the shift from the IPC to the BNS. Under the old law, the inquiry under Section 375 read with Section 90 IPC turned on whether the woman's consent was vitiated by a misconception of fact — whether she had agreed to sexual relations because she genuinely believed a promise of marriage would be honoured. Deepak Gulati had already drawn the crucial line within that inquiry: a promise made with no intention of ever being kept is deceit; a promise made in good faith that later cannot be kept because of circumstances is not. As the Court had put it in that earlier case, "misconception of fact cannot be alleged if the promise is made with good intention and later for unavoidable circumstances, the person is forced to resile from the aforesaid promise."

The Court held that Section 69 BNS, though textually a new and separate offence, imports the very same essential inquiry. The statutory phrase "deceitful means or by making a promise to marry to a woman without any intention of fulfilling the same" still turns on the state of mind of the person making the promise at the time he made it. If it was made with no genuine intention of ever marrying her, Section 69 is attracted. If it was made honestly, and only later abandoned because of intervening circumstances, mere failure to marry — however painful for the woman left behind — does not by itself amount to the deceit the section punishes.

Applying that test to the FIR itself, the Court found nothing in the complainant's own account to suggest deception at the inception. The relationship had developed gradually from an online friendship into a love affair; the appellant expressed his intention to marry her at their first meeting, well before any physical relationship began; and, tellingly, the complaint's own language recorded that she had "succumbed to the appellant's entreaties" on the first occasion — without stating that the physical relationship was conditioned on the promise of marriage at all. The eventual refusal to marry was attributed squarely to the appellant's mother's disapproval, a circumstance arising after the promise was made, not a fact suggesting the promise was hollow from the start. On this reading, the complaint disclosed, at worst, a consensual relationship that did not lead to marriage — not the deceitful inducement Section 69 BNS is aimed at. The Court quashed the FIR and allowed the appeal.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Section 69 BNS complaints are now a routine feature of criminal practice, and this ruling gives defence counsel a clear, evidence-based checklist for a quashing petition under Section 528 BNSS (the successor to Section 482 CrPC). The question a court will now ask is not simply whether a promise was made and later broken, but whether the complaint itself — on its own averments, taken at face value — discloses that the promise was hollow from day one. Relevant indicators the Court treated as significant include: whether the physical relationship began at, or only some time after, the promise; whether the complaint expressly ties consent to the promise, or instead uses language suggesting a voluntary, consensual liaison; whether the eventual refusal to marry is explained by an intervening, external cause (family opposition, financial ruin, ill health, a change of religion requirement that could not be met) rather than by circumstances that existed, but were concealed, at the time the promise was made; and the overall trajectory of the relationship, including cohabitation or repeated voluntary physical intimacy over time, which tends to undercut a claim of deception at the threshold.

For practitioners representing complainants, the ruling is equally instructive in the other direction: an FIR that wants to survive a quashing challenge needs to plead, with some specificity, facts pointing to deceit at inception — for instance, that the accused was already married, had no genuine intention from the start, made the promise only to secure sexual access, or actively concealed a fact that would have changed the complainant's decision to consent. A bare narrative of "he promised to marry me and didn't" will no longer, on its own, survive scrutiny at the threshold stage in light of this ruling.

Haryana's district courts see a steady volume of Section 69 BNS (and, for pre-2024 offences, Section 375/376 IPC on the same ground) complaints, often arising out of relationships that sour after family opposition surfaces — a fact pattern strikingly similar to the one in this very case. This ruling will be squarely relevant both at the bail stage before the Sessions Court and in quashing petitions before the Punjab & Haryana High Court, and it reinforces that courts must look past the emotional aftermath of a broken relationship to the narrower legal question of intent at inception.

Statutory cross-references

Section 69, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 has no direct one-to-one predecessor in the IPC; the same fact pattern was previously addressed through Section 375 IPC (definition of rape, now Section 63, BNS, 2023) read with Section 90, IPC (consent under misconception of fact, now Section 28, BNS, 2023). Quashing petitions that were filed under Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 are now filed under the corresponding Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.
