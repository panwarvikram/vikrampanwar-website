Saksham Rathore v. State of Haryana: P&H High Court Says a "Battle Casualty" Cannot Be Denied Compassionate Appointment by Calling Him Not a "Martyr"

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSaksham Rathore v. State of Haryana & Ors. — Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Nidhi Gupta
DateOrder dated 16 July 2026
CitationNeutral citation not independently confirmed at the time of writing — reported by The Tribune (22 July 2026) and ThePrint (26 July 2026)
Key SectionsArt. 226, Constitution of India · Haryana Compassionate Appointment (for the Family Member of Armed Forces and Central Armed Forces Killed in Battle Casualty) Policy, 2023 · Policy letters dated 30.05.2014 & 28.09.2018

What the case was about

Brigadier Abhimanyu Singh Rathore of the Indian Army died on 30 July 2023 at Command Hospital, Chandigarh, from Cerebral Venous Thrombosis — a blood clot in the brain whose onset had occurred nearly two years earlier, in January 2021, while he was serving in Leh, Ladakh, during "Operation Snow Leopard," the Army's response to Chinese incursions along the Line of Actual Control in the Galwan sector. The Army authorities formally certified his death as a "Battle Casualty" in January 2024.

His son, Saksham Rathore, a B.Com and MBA graduate, applied for a government job under Haryana's compassionate appointment scheme for dependents of battle casualties. The State's response was to reject his claim — not once, but three times, in orders dated 16 February 2024, 24 May 2024 and 4 December 2025. The Haryana government's stated position was that the Brigadier had died of "disease," not in action, and that its welfare policy extended only to the dependents of "martyrs," a category it argued did not include "battle casualties."

Saksham approached the Punjab & Haryana High Court by way of a writ petition under Article 226, seeking to quash all three rejection orders and to compel the State to process his claim on its merits.

What the court held

Justice Nidhi Gupta allowed the petition in an order passed on 16 July 2026, quashing all three rejection orders and directing the State to treat Saksham as eligible and grant him compassionate appointment within four months.

The Court dismantled the State's "not a martyr" argument by tracing the history of its own policy. Haryana's original compassionate appointment scheme, dated 30 May 2014, used the word "martyr" — a term the Court noted the Armed Forces do not officially recognise, since it carries political and religious connotations unsuited to describing personnel who die in the line of duty. The Army's own vocabulary is "battle casualty." Recognising this gap, Haryana amended its policy on 28 September 2018 to explicitly extend benefits to dependents of "battle casualties," and reaffirmed this in a fresh 2023 policy that defined the term broadly — covering deaths in war, border skirmishes, terrorist or militant attacks, UN peacekeeping operations, and even deaths from motor transport accidents, cardiac arrest, air crashes or natural calamities occurring in the course of duties demanding exceptional courage. The Court held Brigadier Rathore's case fell squarely within this definition, and that the State's continued reliance on the word "martyr" against its own superseded and amended policy language was, in the Court's words, "incomprehensible."

On the State's second argument — that the Brigadier died of "disease" rather than in action — the Court was equally unsparing. It noted that Cerebral Venous Thrombosis at high altitude is medically linked to hypoxia, dehydration and hyper-coagulation of the blood, conditions directly attributable to active service at extreme elevation during a border stand-off. Calling the State's characterisation "preposterous" and "crass and ignorant," the Court held: "If anything, it was a 'disease' suffered by a soldier while in service at an active battlefront." The Bench recorded that it was "deeply anguished and appalled" by the State's "unsympathetic and uncooperative stance," which it said sought to "unnecessarily complicate and embroil simple and clear policy instructions into semantics and legalese."

Justice Gupta grounded the ruling in a consistent line of the High Court's own precedents — Jai Dev v. State of Haryana (2025, later upheld by a Division Bench), Rita Saikia (2017), Major Arvind Kumar Suhag (2010) and Pushplata (2024) — all holding that once the Army has certified a death as a battle casualty, the State cannot reopen or dispute that classification to deny welfare benefits, and that beneficial welfare policies of this kind must be construed liberally, not narrowly. "It does not lie in the mouth of the State to contend otherwise," the Court observed, adding that a battle casualty "need not be a death arising out of active hostilities or bomb blasts."

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Compassionate appointment disputes are a recurring feature of writ practice in Haryana, and Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, like much of the state, send a disproportionate share of young men into the Armed Forces. Families of deceased or disabled defence personnel frequently run into exactly the kind of bureaucratic hair-splitting this judgment condemns — a technically worded rejection that quietly narrows a welfare policy far beyond what its own text permits.

This ruling gives practitioners drafting such petitions a clear template: pull the full chronological history of the policy in question, show how later amendments expanded or clarified terms the State is now trying to read narrowly, and lean on the settled principle that a service-specific classification like "battle casualty," once certified by the competent military authority, is not open to collateral dispute by a civil administration. The direction to complete the exercise within four months is also a useful precedent to cite when seeking time-bound compliance orders in similar writ petitions, given how routinely such claims otherwise languish in departmental limbo for years after a favourable order.

More broadly, the judgment is a reminder that Article 226 writ jurisdiction remains the primary and most effective route for challenging administrative policy misapplication of this kind — there is no alternate civil suit remedy that would move nearly as fast, and tribunal or departmental appeal mechanisms rarely engage with the kind of policy-history analysis the High Court undertook here.

Statutory cross-references

This dispute arises entirely under executive welfare policy and Article 226 of the Constitution, not under the penal statutes, so there is no BNS/BNSS equivalent mapping applicable here. Practitioners handling similar claims should track Haryana's Compassionate Appointment (for the Family Member of Armed Forces and Central Armed Forces Killed in Battle Casualty) Policy, 2023, which is now the operative instrument superseding the 2014 and 2018 policy letters, and should build their pleadings around the definitional chain this judgment relies upon.
