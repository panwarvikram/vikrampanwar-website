P&H High Court: A Husband's Offer to Pay Travel Costs Doesn't Automatically Defeat a Wife's Plea to Transfer a Matrimonial Case Closer to Home

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court, Chandigarh
BenchJustice Harkesh Manuja
Date9 September 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 322
Key SectionsS. 13, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 · S. 125, CrPC, 1973 (S. 144, BNSS, 2023)

What the case was about

A husband filed a divorce petition under Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 before the Family Court at Hisar. The couple had married in 2013 and have a minor son born in 2015. Amid the marital discord that followed, the wife moved out and began living with her widowed mother at Sonepat, where she also cared for the minor child full-time. She had no independent source of income and depended on her mother and younger brother for support.

Once the husband's divorce petition was pending at Hisar, the wife applied to have it transferred to the Family Court at Sonepat, arguing that travelling repeatedly to Hisar for hearings, without a job or independent means, while also being solely responsible for a young child, imposed real hardship on her. The husband opposed the transfer. His counsel did not dispute her circumstances outright, but argued that since the husband was willing to bear her travel and stay expenses for every hearing that required her presence, there was no need to disturb the forum at all.

The matter also had a procedural wrinkle worth noting for practitioners: the wife had earlier filed a maintenance petition under Section 125 CrPC before the Family Court at Sonepat itself, which had since been decided, with cross-challenges from both sides pending before the High Court. That gave Sonepat an independent, concrete connection to the parties' litigation history, beyond simply being the wife's chosen residence.

What the court held

Justice Harkesh Manuja rejected the argument that an offer to fund travel settles the question of transfer. The Court held that a willingness to bear expenses is, at best, one relevant fact among several — not a rule that trumps every other consideration. As the order put it, an offer of that kind "cannot be read as laying down an inflexible rule that an offer by the husband to bear travelling expenses would, in every case, defeat a wife's prayer for transfer," and that "the question of transfer has to be considered on the facts and circumstances of each case."

The husband's side leaned on two precedents to resist the transfer. The Court distinguished both on facts. In one, an earlier decision where a transfer plea had failed because grandparents were available to look after the child and the husband had offered to bear costs, the Court noted that the present case was materially different: here it was the wife herself, not any grandparent on the husband's side, who was providing full-time care for the child while living with her own widowed mother, with no income of her own. In the second case relied upon by the husband, the Supreme Court had, on its own specific facts, found no independent ground for transfer and had directed the husband to cover travel costs instead — but the Court held that decision was never meant to be read as a general principle applicable to every transfer petition regardless of circumstance.

Applying the settled line of authority that Indian courts follow in transfer applications between spouses — including Sumita Singh v. Kumar Sanjay (2002) and Rajani Kishor Pardeshi v. Kishor Babulal Pardeshi (2005) — the Court reiterated that the convenience of the wife, particularly a wife without independent income who is caring for a minor child, deserves real weightage in deciding where a matrimonial case should be heard, and that transfer should ordinarily be allowed to spare her undue hardship. On the facts before it, factoring in her lack of income, her dependence on her mother, her sole responsibility for the child, and the connected Section 125 CrPC proceedings already rooted in Sonepat, the Court allowed the transfer. The divorce petition now moves from the Family Court at Hisar to the Family Court at Sonepat, with the parties directed to appear there on 18 September 2026.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Transfer petitions are a routine but consequential part of matrimonial practice in Haryana's district courts, where spouses frequently end up living in different towns once a marriage breaks down. This order is a useful reminder that a monetary offer from the opposing side is not a shortcut past the merits. Advocates representing a wife seeking transfer should plead the full factual picture — income status, custody responsibilities, dependence on natal family, and any independently pending proceedings at the forum sought — rather than relying on the general principle alone, since the Court's analysis here turned heavily on exactly these specifics.

Equally, advocates representing a husband resisting transfer should be cautious about over-relying on an expense-bearing offer as a stand-alone defence; this order confirms that such an offer will be weighed alongside, not instead of, the wife's actual circumstances. The judgment also illustrates how a connected Section 125 CrPC (now Section 144 BNSS) proceeding at a particular forum can independently support a transfer plea, which is a tactical point worth flagging early when maintenance and divorce proceedings between the same parties are pending in different towns.

Statutory cross-references

Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, under which the wife's earlier maintenance petition was filed, now corresponds to Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, under which the husband's divorce petition was filed, remains unchanged, as personal-law statutes are outside the scope of the BNS/BNSS 2023 recodification of criminal law.
