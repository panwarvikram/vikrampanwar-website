Shamsher Singh & Anr. v. State of Haryana: P&H High Court Says a Parole Surety Can't Be Forfeited in Full Just Because the Parolee Reoffended

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Manisha Batra
Date29 August 2026
CitationCase Title: Shamsher Singh & Anr. v. State of Haryana · Reported by LiveLaw, 29 August 2026 (formal neutral citation not yet assigned at time of writing)
Key SectionsS. 528, BNSS, 2023 · S. 10(2)(c), Haryana Good Conduct Prisoners (Temporary Release) Act, 1988

What the case was about

Two men in Rohtak did what thousands of families across Haryana do every year: they stood surety of ₹2,00,000 each so that a relative, a convict named Sandeep serving a sentence for offences under Sections 506, 376-D and 366 IPC, could avail three weeks of parole in April 2020. Standing surety for a parolee is routine — it is the mechanism by which the Haryana Good Conduct Prisoners (Temporary Release) Act, 1988 lets a prisoner step outside jail walls for a defined period, on the strength of someone else's undertaking that he will return and behave.

Sandeep did not behave. During the parole period a fresh FIR was registered against him for offences including murder under the IPC and the Arms Act. On learning of this, the Jail Superintendent moved against the two sureties under Section 10(2)(c) of the 1988 Act. The District Magistrate, Rohtak, issued show cause notices and then forfeited the entire ₹2,00,000 bond furnished by each petitioner, triggering recovery proceedings against them personally.

The two sureties challenged this before the High Court under Section 528 BNSS, the renumbered provision that lets the High Court quash proceedings or orders to prevent abuse of process or to secure the ends of justice. Their case was straightforward: they had never been accused of facilitating, knowing about, or conniving in whatever Sandeep did during his parole. A surety bond, they argued, is not a promise to police an adult relative's every movement — it is an undertaking that can be enforced for breach, but not treated as an automatic penalty triggered by anything the parolee does.

What the court held

Justice Manisha Batra agreed with the sureties, though not by discharging them entirely. The Court read the District Magistrate's order closely and found it wanting on one specific point: it never recorded any finding that the petitioners had facilitated Sandeep's alleged criminal conduct, had knowledge of it, or had connived with him in violating his parole conditions. There was equally no finding that the petitioners had themselves failed to discharge any obligation they had personally undertaken under the bond. The order, in other words, treated the mere registration of a fresh FIR against the parolee as sufficient, by itself, to wipe out the sureties' full deposit.

The Court was careful not to let the sureties off the hook altogether. Relying on two Supreme Court decisions — Mohammed Kunju v. State of Karnataka, (1999) 8 SCC 660, and Ram Lal v. State of U.P., AIR 1979 SC 1498 — Justice Batra reiterated that a surety's undertaking is an independent, enforceable obligation, and that a breach can attract forfeiture. But the same line of authority makes forfeiture a matter of judicial discretion, not a mechanical formality. The authority ordering forfeiture must apply its mind to the quantum: whether the full amount is warranted, or whether the facts call for a reduced, proportionate sum.

Two considerations shaped the Court's proportionality analysis. First, a surety is not expected to keep continuous surveillance over an adult parolee once the very authority that granted the release has let him go — policing every waking hour of a released prisoner's conduct is not what the bond promises. Second, the object of the surety bond had, in a practical sense, still been substantially served: Sandeep was taken back into custody during the parole period itself, so the sureties had not, for instance, helped him abscond or evade recapture. Weighing this against the complete absence of any finding of connivance or wilful default, the Court held that forfeiting the entire ₹2,00,000 from each petitioner was unduly harsh and disproportionate. It modified the order, capping the recoverable amount at ₹50,000 per petitioner and directing that any excess already recovered be refunded.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Furlough and parole are everyday business in district court practice here — Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani see a steady stream of applications under the very 1988 Act this judgment interprets, and every one of them needs a surety willing to sign a bond running into lakhs of rupees. Until now, the risk calculus for a prospective surety, and the advice a lawyer gives a client asked to stand surety for a relative, has been genuinely uncertain: what actually happens if the parolee misbehaves? This judgment supplies a concrete, citable answer. Forfeiture is not an all-or-nothing switch flipped by the parolee's own misconduct; it requires the forfeiting authority to record a finding on the surety's own culpability, or at minimum to justify why the maximum amount, rather than something less, is being taken.

That has two practical consequences worth flagging to clients. First, anyone asked to stand surety for parole or furlough should understand that their bond can still be enforced — this is not a "sureties are never liable" ruling — but a forfeiture order that simply recites the parolee's fresh FIR without engaging with the surety's own conduct is now vulnerable to a Section 528 BNSS challenge. Second, for practitioners defending sureties before District Magistrates in forfeiture proceedings, the order itself demands a response: press the authority to record specific findings on connivance, facilitation, or wilful default, and if it doesn't, this judgment is the ready precedent to invoke in the High Court. Given how routinely parole and furlough sureties are drawn from a convict's immediate and extended family in this belt, a rule that keeps forfeiture proportionate rather than punitive protects ordinary relatives from a magnitude-scale financial hit for someone else's conduct they had no real power to control.

Statutory cross-references

The petition itself was filed under Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 — the renumbered successor to Section 482 of the old Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, preserving the High Court's inherent power to prevent abuse of process and secure the ends of justice. The forfeiture power under challenge, Section 10(2)(c) of the Haryana Good Conduct Prisoners (Temporary Release) Act, 1988, is a state enactment and has no BNSS/BNS equivalent; it continues to operate independently as the governing law for furlough and parole release and surety enforcement in Haryana. The underlying conviction offences cited in the case — Sections 506, 376-D and 366 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 — correspond broadly to Sections 351, 70(2) and 84 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 for offences committed after 1 July 2024, though Sandeep's original conviction predates the new codes and remains governed by the IPC.
