Furlough, Parole, and the Haryana Good Conduct Prisoners (Temporary Release) Act, 2022: Who Grants What, and on What Yardstick

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing StatuteHaryana Good Conduct Prisoners (Temporary Release) Act, 2022 (Haryana Act No. 15 of 2022)
In Force From11 April 2022 — repeals the 1988 Act of the same name
Key SectionsSs. 3–6 (grant of parole/furlough) · Ss. 9–10 (overstay/violation) · Ss. 11–12 (procedure)
Granting AuthoritiesCompetent Authority notified by State Government · Superintendent Jail (emergency & custody parole)

What the Act is about

Every convict lodged in a Haryana jail eventually faces the question of temporary release — whether to attend a family emergency, a wedding, a funeral, or simply as a structured incentive for years of good behaviour behind bars. Until 2022, this was governed by the Haryana Good Conduct Prisoners (Temporary Release) Act, 1988. That Act was repealed and replaced by the present Act, which received the Governor's assent on 31 March 2022 and came into force on 11 April 2022. The 2022 Act extends to the whole of Haryana and applies to every convicted prisoner confined under the orders of a court (including a court-martial) having jurisdiction in Haryana, regardless of which jail in India the prisoner is actually held in under reciprocal arrangements.

The Act is built around a single organising idea that is easy to lose sight of amid its procedural detail: temporary release is a privilege earned through conduct, and the entire statutory architecture — eligibility bars, hardcore-convict restrictions, bonds, and penalties for overstay — exists to make that privilege administrable, verifiable, and revocable, rather than to create a right enforceable on demand.

Furlough versus parole: the core distinction

The single most common confusion among clients — and, occasionally, even among junior counsel — is treating "parole" and "furlough" as interchangeable. The Act is explicit that they are conceptually different, and the difference has real consequences for how each is sought and what it does to a sentence.

Furlough (Section 4) is defined as a temporary release granted purely as an incentive for good behaviour and conduct sustained over a period of time. It requires no particular reason or emergency — a prisoner earns it simply by accumulating enough good conduct and time served. Critically, the period spent on furlough counts towards the prisoner's sentence (subject to forfeiture on overstay, discussed below). The standard furlough period is three weeks, which cannot be availed in instalments; a prisoner who has completed three-fourths of a term sentence, or ten years of a life sentence, becomes eligible for a longer four-week furlough, again to be availed in one continuous stretch. A prisoner is not eligible for furlough at all until three years of sentence have been served after conviction, and a range of grave offences — NDPS Act convictions, sedition, rape with murder, robbery or dacoity with murder, murder for ransom or extortion, sexual offences against a child below twelve, and life imprisonment till natural life — are altogether barred from furlough by statute, irrespective of conduct or time served.

Parole, by contrast, is fundamentally need-based rather than an automatic incentive, and the Act splits it into three distinct categories. Regular parole (Section 3) is available for personal, family, agricultural, or similar reasons, for up to ten weeks in a calendar year (which may be split into two spells), with a special six-month parole window for a female prisoner around childbirth. A prisoner must have served at least one year of sentence after conviction to qualify (a restriction lifted entirely for male convicts aged 70+ and female convicts aged 65+). Unlike furlough, the period spent on regular parole does not count towards the sentence, and no ordinary remission accrues during it. Emergency parole (Section 5) is available at any time, irrespective of how much sentence has been served, where a family member of the prisoner has died or is seriously ill, or the prisoner himself is seriously ill — verified within 24 hours through the police or a jail officer and certified by a Medical Officer, countersigned by the Civil Surgeon. It runs for up to four weeks in a calendar year (which may be split), and, like regular parole, does not count towards the sentence. Custody parole (Section 6) is the narrowest category: a prisoner is escorted under armed police custody to a specific place within India and back, for a specific purpose, for a maximum of six hours per event excluding travel time — and this period is treated as time actually spent in jail, making it the one form of temporary release that carries no sentence-computation consequence at all.

Who grants what: the layered authority structure

The Act deliberately assigns different releasing authorities to different categories and different levels of offence severity, so that graver cases are decided at a higher, more accountable level. Regular parole and furlough are granted by the "competent authority" — an officer notified for this purpose by the State Government. In practice, and consistent with the delegation the State Government has notified under the Act, more serious categories of case (murder, dacoity, rape with murder, dowry death, and NDPS Act offences) are routed to the Divisional Commissioner of the concerned division, while the District Magistrate handles the generality of other cases as the competent authority. Emergency parole and custody parole, being time-sensitive and narrower in scope, are exercised at the Superintendent Jail level, subject to the verification safeguards described above.

The process for regular parole and furlough is a structured chain, not a single-officer decision: the prisoner (or an adult family member) applies to the Superintendent Jail on the prescribed form; the Superintendent Jail forwards an eligible case to the District Magistrate with a copy to the Deputy Commissioner of Police or Superintendent of Police for a conduct/verification report; the police report goes to the District Magistrate, who records a recommendation; and the competent authority then takes the final call and issues a release warrant, or a speaking order of rejection. The Act fixes tight outer time limits for each stage — one week for the Superintendent Jail, two weeks each for the police report, the District Magistrate's recommendation, and the competent authority's decision — and if a report or recommendation does not arrive within its window, the competent authority is entitled to presume there is nothing adverse on record and decide the application accordingly, so that the process cannot be indefinitely stalled by an unresponsive intermediate authority.

The yardsticks that decide eligibility

Beyond the category-specific conditions above, three cross-cutting yardsticks govern every application. First, a prisoner must be out on bail in every other pending case against him before any court to even be considered for furlough, emergency parole, or custody parole — an unresolved case elsewhere is an absolute bar. Second, the Act creates a distinct, more restrictive regime for "hardcore convicted prisoners" — a defined category covering serious repeat offenders and those convicted of specified grave offences (robbery, dacoity, kidnapping for ransom, murder for extortion, rape or gang rape with aggravating features, sexual offences against a child below sixteen, serial or contract killing, and offences under laws like the NSA, UAPA-equivalent State-era statutes, or organised crime legislation, among others). A hardcore convict is ordinarily barred from emergency parole, regular parole, and furlough altogether, and can access only custody parole, and that too solely to attend a family member's funeral or a child's or sibling's wedding. This bar lifts only if the hardcore convict — not sentenced to death or life till natural life — has completed five years of sentence (including up to two years of undertrial detention) without a major jail offence or cognizable offence in that period, at which point he becomes eligible at par with an ordinary convict; a hardcore convict sentenced to life till natural life must complete seven years before even reaching that parity, and even then remains excluded from furlough by the separate offence-based bar discussed earlier. Third, conduct discipline is continuously enforced: a minor jail-offence punishment bars any parole for six months, a major jail-offence punishment or a violation of temporary-release conditions bars all parole for a full year, and no parole or furlough is ever extended once granted — a prisoner who needs more time must apply afresh, subject to the usual eligibility gates.

Why this matters for practitioners in Haryana

For counsel advising convicted clients or their families, the practical value of the Act lies in front-loading the eligibility check before any application is drafted: confirm there is no case pending anywhere without bail, confirm whether the client falls within the hardcore-convict definition (and if so, whether the five- or seven-year threshold has actually been crossed), and confirm the offence itself is not one of the categorical bars to furlough. Overstay carries serious consequences that clients often underestimate — failure to surrender within ten days of the due date is a cognizable, non-bailable offence carrying two to three years' imprisonment and a fine of up to Rs. 1 lakh, over and above whatever remission or bond forfeiture penalty is separately imposed, and even a timely surrender without "sufficient cause" for the delay in leaving custody can cost the prisoner remission days, canteen and interview privileges, or a downgrade in jail classification. Counsel should also flag the bond requirements early, since they can be substantial: a minimum Rs. 1 lakh personal bond (Rs. 2 lakh for hardcore convicts) with at least two sureties, though this reduces for prisoners with a clean track record of two peacefully availed regular paroles and one furlough.

Statutory cross-references

The hardcore-convict definition in Section 2(g) of the Act cross-refers extensively to Indian Penal Code, 1860 provisions — Sections 392/394 (robbery), 395/396/397 (dacoity), 364-A (kidnapping for ransom), 376/377 read with 302 (sexual offences with murder), 458/459/460 (lurking house trespass), and others — which now correspond to their renumbered equivalents under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 for offences committed on or after 1 July 2024. Practitioners should verify the applicable code (IPC or BNS) based on the date of the offence when assessing whether a client falls within the hardcore-convict bar, since the Haryana Act's text itself has not been formally amended to reflect the BNS renumbering.
