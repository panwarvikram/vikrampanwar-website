Amit Kumar v. State of Punjab (CRWP-8138-2024): P&H High Court Says Being the Biological Father Alone Does Not Justify Habeas Corpus for a Child’s Custody While a Guardianship Case Is Pending

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — Amit Kumar v. State of Punjab & Ors.
BenchJustice Sumeet Goel (single judge)
DateReported 6 October 2026 (exact date of pronouncement not confirmed from the sources available)
CitationCRWP-8138-2024 · 2026 LiveLaw (PH) 360
Key SectionsArticle 226 of the Constitution; Section 97 CrPC (now Section 100 BNSS); Section 25, Guardians and Wards Act, 1890

What the case was about

A widower from Sonipat filed a habeas corpus petition in the Punjab & Haryana High Court for the return of his young son. He had married in March 2019, the child was born in March 2020, and the mother died in May 2022. After that the boy lived with his father and went to school in Sonipat.

According to the petition, on 30 June 2023 the child’s maternal grandfather, uncle and grandmother took the boy to Patiala for a stay of five to seven days and then refused to send him back. In April 2024 the Sub-Divisional Magistrate, Sonipat, acting under Section 97 CrPC, told the father to take responsibility for the child but also directed him to approach the competent court on custody and welfare. In July 2024 the father went to Patiala with police to enforce that order, yet the child stayed on with his maternal family. Meanwhile the maternal grandmother had already moved a petition under Section 25 of the Guardians and Wards Act at Patiala, the maintainability of which the father disputed.

The father argued that, as the biological father and natural guardian, his right to custody was preferential and the retention of the child by the maternal family was illegal. The maternal family answered that the boy had been taken with the father’s consent, that they had been looking after him because the father, a truck driver, could not, and that the High Court should not step in when a guardianship proceeding was already pending.

What the court held

Justice Sumeet Goel dismissed the petition. Relying on Veerpal Kaur v. State of Punjab, the Court reiterated that habeas corpus in a custody dispute works only where the child’s present custody is demonstrably illegal. It is a writ against unlawful detention, not a shortcut to a custody decree.

The reasoning turns on the nature of the inquiry. Deciding where a child will be better off needs evidence, an interaction with the child and, in some cases, expert input. That is the work of a court of first instance conducting a full guardianship inquiry, not of a High Court exercising summary writ jurisdiction. Where a competent court is already seized of the custody question, the Court said, Article 226 jurisdiction is very limited: it can be used in a grave and immediate emergency, and even then ordinarily only to grant interim protection until the statutory forum decides.

Applying this, the bench noted that the competing claims — consent to the child’s stay, and who can care for him better — were disputed questions of fact. The SDM’s order was not a final adjudication of permanent custody; it expressly sent the parties to the proper court. And there was no imminent or extraordinary threat to the child that would justify interference. The father’s status as the biological parent, by itself, was not enough. The guardianship court must now decide the rival claims independently, with the welfare of the child as the paramount consideration.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Habeas corpus is the first instinct of many clients in custody fights, and the Punjab & Haryana High Court sees a steady stream of such petitions from district courts such as Sonipat, Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri. This ruling is a useful reality check when advising a parent who wants a quick order. The writ remedy is strongest where a child is being held by a stranger, or by someone with no legal claim, in circumstances that suggest real danger. It is weak where the child is with close relatives, the facts are contested, and a guardianship petition is pending or can be filed.

A practical point for the other side as well: if you act for grandparents or relatives who have the child, the existence of a properly filed Guardians and Wards Act petition is a strong shield against a writ. Conversely, a parent who has been told by a magistrate to approach the competent court, as happened here, should do so promptly rather than rely on the order to recover the child. Delay in filing the proper petition works against the client, because courts weigh the status quo and the child’s settled routine when deciding welfare.

Counsel should also keep in view the distinction with cases involving very young children. For a child below five, Section 6 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act says custody ordinarily lies with the mother, and the Court has treated that as relevant in habeas corpus petitions (see our earlier note on mother’s custody of a child below five). Here the father’s petition failed not because the mother’s side had superior rights, but because the writ forum was the wrong place to resolve the dispute.

When a writ is still worth filing, plead the emergency specifically: danger to the child’s safety, removal from the jurisdiction, or deliberate concealment. Seek interim access or a direction to produce the child, and ask for expedited disposal of the pending guardianship case rather than a final custody order.

Statutory cross-references

Section 97 CrPC (search for persons wrongfully confined), under which the SDM acted, is now Section 100 BNSS, 2023. The writ power invoked is Article 226 of the Constitution. The substantive custody law remains the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 (Section 25 deals with the return of a ward who leaves or is removed from the guardian’s custody, and Section 17 with the welfare of the minor) and, for Hindus, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956.
