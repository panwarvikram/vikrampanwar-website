MACT Compensation Is Not Just for Dependents: P&H High Court Reaffirms Legal Heirs' Right to Claim

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — appeal against a Motor Accident Claims Tribunal award
BenchJustice Virinder Aggarwal
DateReported 4 May 2026 (the MACT award under challenge was dated 23 February 2005)
CitationNeutral citation not confirmable — underlying LiveLaw report is paywalled; see disclaimer
Key SectionsSs. 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

The owner of a vehicle involved in a fatal road accident appealed against a Motor Accident Claims Tribunal award that had granted compensation to the legal heirs of the man who died. The appeal did not dispute the accident itself or the vehicle's involvement — the challenge was narrower and more technical: the owner argued that the claimants who had been awarded compensation were not financial dependents of the deceased, and therefore should not have been entitled to claim under the Motor Vehicles Act at all.

This is a recurring line of defence in MACT litigation. Insurers and vehicle owners frequently try to narrow the pool of eligible claimants by arguing that only those who were actually dependent on the deceased's income — typically a spouse, minor children, or elderly parents being supported by the deceased — can bring a compensation claim. Anyone else, on this argument, falls outside the statutory scheme and must be turned away regardless of their relationship to the deceased.

What the court held

Justice Virinder Aggarwal rejected that argument and dismissed the vehicle owner's appeal, upholding the Tribunal's award in full. The High Court reiterated a settled but frequently contested principle: the right to claim compensation under the Motor Vehicles Act is not confined to persons who were financially dependent on the deceased. Legal heirs — a broader class defined by personal law and succession, not by proof of economic reliance — are entitled to maintain and succeed in a compensation claim.

The distinction the Court drew is an important one for practitioners. "Dependency" is a concept that matters when a Tribunal computes the quantum of compensation — for instance, in applying the multiplier method and assessing loss of dependency for heirs who relied on the deceased's income. But it is not a threshold requirement for locus standi. The class of persons entitled to apply and be compensated under the Act's compensation provisions is the wider category of "legal representatives," which includes heirs who inherit under personal law even if they were not being financially supported by the deceased at the time of death. The vehicle owner's attempt to use a dependency test as a gatekeeping device to defeat the claim altogether was accordingly rejected.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

Motor accident claims make up a substantial share of the civil docket in Haryana's district courts, and objections to a claimant's eligibility are raised routinely by insurance companies and vehicle owners as a first line of defence — often before the merits of negligence or quantum are even reached. This ruling gives claimant-side counsel a clean, citable answer to the "you weren't dependent, so you can't claim" objection: dependency goes to quantum, not to entitlement.

It is particularly useful in cases involving married daughters, adult sons with independent income, or parents who were not financially reliant on the deceased — categories that insurers frequently try to exclude altogether rather than simply argue for a lower share of compensation. Practitioners filing claim petitions before the MACT at Bhiwani or Charkhi Dadri should plead the full class of legal heirs as claimants regardless of dependency status, and be prepared to argue quantum and dependency as a separate, later-stage question rather than conceding eligibility upfront.

The case is also a reminder of how long motor accident litigation can run in practice — the original award here was passed in February 2005, and the High Court did not finally dispose of the owner's appeal until roughly two decades later. That should inform realistic expectations set with clients about timelines, and underscores the value of pressing for interim awards and timely execution wherever the Tribunal's award is not under a stay.

Statutory cross-references

Section 166 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 governs applications for compensation and, through its Explanation, defines "legal representative" broadly enough to cover all persons entitled to succeed to the estate of the deceased, not merely dependents; Section 168 empowers the Tribunal to determine the amount of "just compensation" having regard to relevant heads, including loss of dependency, loss of consortium, and loss of estate. These provisions remain part of a special civil statute and are unaffected by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 — both of which recodify only the general penal law and criminal procedure. Where the same accident also gives rise to a criminal prosecution for causing death by rash or negligent driving — now Section 106, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (replacing Section 304A of the erstwhile Indian Penal Code) — that criminal case proceeds independently of the MACT claim; an acquittal in the criminal trial does not by itself defeat compensation before the Tribunal, since the standard of proof in accident claims is the civil standard of preponderance of probabilities rather than proof beyond reasonable doubt.
