V. Sumitra Reddy v. K. Ranganadha Reddy: Supreme Court Says a Dissolved Partnership's Land Must Be Valued at Today's Price, Not the 1983 Date of Dissolution

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseV. Sumitra Reddy & Anr. v. K. Ranganadha Reddy & Ors.
BenchJustices Ujjal Bhuyan & Vipul M. Pancholi
Date9 September 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (SC) 918
Key SectionsSs. 46 & 48, Indian Partnership Act, 1932

What the case was about

M/s Viraj Constructions was a partnership at will formed in 1964 to carry out construction works for the Railways. One of its partners, Kasireddy Lakshmi Narayana Reddy, held a 25% share, and the firm's principal asset was roughly 3.27 acres of land at Begumpet in Hyderabad — the kind of urban plot whose value has moved in an entirely different universe since the 1960s. In October 1983, the partnership was dissolved. What should have followed, under a straightforward reading of partnership law, was a winding up: the firm's assets sold or otherwise liquidated, its debts cleared, and the surplus divided among the partners according to their shares.

That did not happen. Decades passed with the dissolution unresolved on the ground, while the value of the Begumpet land climbed steadily. By the time the dispute reached litigation, the outgoing partner's legal representative — his son, K. Ranganadha Reddy — was pressing for his 25% share, and the remaining partners' side argued that whatever was owed had to be measured as it stood in 1983, the year the firm technically ceased to exist. Freezing the valuation at 1983 prices, on land that has since become part of one of Hyderabad's costlier commercial corridors, would have reduced a substantial entitlement to a token sum.

The High Court had ruled in favour of settlement and public auction at current values. The matter reached the Supreme Court by way of a civil appeal challenging that direction.

What the court held

The Supreme Court, dismissing the appeal, held that the correct valuation date for a dissolved firm's assets is not the date of dissolution but the date closer to actual settlement, sale or realisation of those assets. The reasoning rests on the basic architecture of Sections 46 and 48 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932: dissolution does not by itself convert a partner's interest into a fixed monetary claim frozen at that moment. Instead, the statute contemplates liquidation — the assets are meant to be actually sold or otherwise converted, the firm's debts paid off, and only the surplus divided among partners in their agreed proportions, unless the partners have agreed otherwise.

Because liquidation itself never took place in this case, the underlying asset — the Begumpet land — remained, in substance, unconverted partnership property. The Court held that a partner (or the estate of a deceased partner) cannot be made to accept a valuation pegged to a historical date when the liquidation contemplated by law simply did not happen on that date. Where the firm's assets are eventually sold or auctioned decades later, the outgoing partner's share has to be calculated on the value realised at that later point, not artificially rolled back to the value the land carried when the partnership dissolved.

The Court accordingly upheld the direction for settlement of accounts and, failing agreement, a public auction of the property, with the 25% share payable out of the price actually realised. Any interim stays that had kept the process frozen were vacated, clearing the way for the liquidation the 1983 dissolution should have triggered decades earlier.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Partnership disputes are a routine part of civil practice at the district level — family-run firms, agricultural equipment dealerships, transport businesses and small construction outfits in Haryana are very often structured as partnerships at will, and disputes over an exiting or deceased partner's share are common. This judgment closes off a defence that outgoing-partner disputes see fairly often: the remaining partners arguing that the exiting partner (or his legal heirs) must accept a value frozen at the date the partnership technically ended, especially where that date is old and the underlying property — commonly land — has appreciated substantially since.

The ruling gives plaintiffs' counsel a clean, Supreme Court-backed argument in any suit for dissolution, rendition of accounts or partition of partnership assets: if the assets were never actually liquidated at the time of dissolution, the valuation exercise has to track the property's worth as of the date of actual sale or settlement, not the paper date of dissolution. This matters enormously in Haryana, where land values in and around Gurugram, Panipat and even smaller towns like Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri have risen sharply over the past two decades, making the choice of valuation date the difference between a token payout and a fair one.

For defence counsel acting for continuing partners or a reconstituted firm, the case is also a caution: continuing to use a dissolved firm's assets without first settling accounts with the outgoing partner, or treating dissolution as if it alone extinguishes the exiting partner's economic interest, invites exactly the kind of long-delayed reckoning this case represents. The safer course, and the one this judgment implicitly endorses, is to actually carry out the liquidation contemplated by Sections 46 and 48 promptly after dissolution, rather than let an unresolved partnership interest sit and appreciate for decades before a court is asked to fix its value.

Statutory cross-references

Sections 46 and 48 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 are civil-law provisions unaffected by the 2023 recodification of the criminal statutes, so no BNS/BNSS cross-reference applies here. Practitioners handling partnership disputes in Haryana should read this ruling alongside the general law on partition and rendition-of-accounts suits under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, since a suit to enforce this kind of valuation typically proceeds as a suit for dissolution, accounts and, where a specific asset like land is involved, sale or partition of that asset.
