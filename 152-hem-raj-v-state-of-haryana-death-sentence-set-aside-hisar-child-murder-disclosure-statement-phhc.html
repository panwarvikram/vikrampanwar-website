Hem Raj v. State of Haryana: P&H High Court Sets Aside a Death Sentence in a 2018 Hisar Child-Murder Case, Holding a Resiled Section 164 Statement and Doubtful Recoveries Cannot Sustain a Conviction

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — State of Haryana v. Hem Raj (Murder Reference) with Hem Raj v. State of Haryana (Criminal Appeal)
BenchJustice Anoop Chitkara and Justice Harmeet Singh Deol (Division Bench)
Date7 October 2026 (as reported by LiveLaw and ThePrint)
CitationMRC-2-2022 with CRA-D-250-2022 (case numbers as reported by LiveLaw; neutral citation not confirmed)
Key SectionsSections 302, 201 IPC; Sections 164, 313 CrPC; Sections 26, 27, 106 Indian Evidence Act; Section 481 BNSS

What the case was about

On 16 June 2018 the headless lower body of a child of about two and a half years was found in a fallow field in village Gaibipur, District Hisar, and an FIR was registered at Police Station Barwala. Three days later the child’s mother told a Judicial Magistrate, in a statement under Section 164 CrPC, that the man she had been living with since April 2018, Hem Raj, had killed the boy on the night of 13 June. Hem Raj was arrested on 20 June, and police said his disclosure statements led to the recovery of the child’s clothes and a hoe (kassi).

In March 2022 the trial court convicted him under Sections 302 and 201 IPC and sentenced him to death. The reference for confirmation and his appeal were heard together by a Division Bench of the High Court, which had the benefit of a senior advocate as amicus curiae.

What the court held

The Bench acquitted Hem Raj, dismissed the murder reference as infructuous and ordered his release on a personal bond under Section 481 BNSS, without sureties. Its reasoning can be taken in four strands.

Identity of the body. Only the lower body was found, and DNA profiling failed. The court said the body was “most probably” the child’s, but probability is not proof. The clothes were never shown to the mother at trial, and the trial judge did not use his power to put them to her. The court also preferred the original Hindi deposition to its English translation, because the two differed on whether the mother had identified the child or only his clothes.

The Section 164 statement. The mother turned hostile in court and denied the accusation. The court reiterated that a Section 164 statement is not substantive evidence; it can only be used to corroborate or contradict, and it cannot carry a conviction on its own once the witness resiles. Her sworn, cross-examined trial testimony could not be brushed aside merely because it helped the accused.

Disclosure statements and recoveries. An accused’s identification of clothes while in police custody was treated as a confession barred by Section 26 of the Evidence Act. A second disclosure memo, said to have been signed at Bahadurgarh, was a computer-typed document and the court found no explanation of how it was printed on the roadside, calling it apparently fabricated. Pointing out a crime scene that police and villagers already knew about discovered nothing new, so Section 27 was not attracted. The blood on the hoe could not be traced to anyone, the hoe was not shown to the doctor, and the field owner was not associated with the recovery. Applying a six-fold test for Section 27 disclosures, the court held the recovery failed.

The Childline record and the Sarpanch. The village Sarpanch had phoned Childline at about 9:32 pm on 13 June. That call was admissible as part of the same transaction, and the Childline record named a different person, “Mahabir”, as the one beating the woman and child. Police never investigated who Mahabir was, or whether the name was an alias. The Sarpanch’s later drowning story was also contradicted by the medical evidence, which attributed death to skull fractures. The court added that the Section 313 examination was defective because the incriminating material was put to the accused in two omnibus questions.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

This is a useful checklist for anyone defending, or prosecuting, a circumstantial murder case in the Haryana sessions courts. First, a recovery memo is only as good as its paper trail: independent witnesses, a sensible account of where and how documents were prepared, and a recovered article actually linked to the victim. Second, a Section 164 statement that the witness disowns at trial is a weak foundation, and counsel should confront the Magistrate and the witness with it carefully. Third, the Section 313 point is worth raising in every appeal. Dumping the whole evidence into a couple of omnibus questions is a recurring defect in trial records, and courts treat it seriously in capital cases.

It also fits a growing line of P&H decisions, including the six-fold test for disclosure statements already discussed in our note on State of Haryana v. Arun, that insist on admissible, well-documented evidence before a death sentence can stand. For trial advocates in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, the practical lesson is to build the cross-examination around contemporaneous records, such as helpline logs and call details, rather than later oral accounts.

Statutory cross-references

Sections 302 and 201 IPC correspond to Sections 103 and 238 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. Sections 164 and 313 CrPC correspond to Sections 183 and 351 BNSS, and Section 437A CrPC (bail bond on acquittal) to Section 481 BNSS. The Evidence Act provisions discussed (Sections 6, 26, 27 and 106) now sit in the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, broadly as Sections 4, 23, 23(2) and 109; readers should confirm the exact numbering against the Act.
