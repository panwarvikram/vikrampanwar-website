Maintenance Under Section 144 BNSS: Who Can Claim, What You Need to Prove, and How to File

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseRajnesh v. Neha (procedural guidelines); Bhuwan Mohan Singh v. Meena (purpose of the provision)
BenchGoverned by the Judicial Magistrate First Class / Family Court, as applicable
DateProvision in force since 1 July 2024; guidelines from 2020/2021
Citation(2021) 2 SCC 324 · (2015) 6 SCC 353
Key SectionsS. 144, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (replacing S. 125, CrPC, 1973)

What this remedy is about

Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 — the direct successor to the long-familiar Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 — is a summary, fast-moving remedy meant to stop a spouse, child, or parent from sliding into destitution while a more elaborate civil claim, if any, works its way through the courts. It is deliberately housed in the criminal procedure code rather than a personal-law statute, so it applies across religions: a Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, or Parsi wife (or husband, in narrow circumstances), a minor or disabled adult child, or an indigent parent can all invoke it regardless of which personal law governs their family. The wording carried over into the BNSS almost unchanged, so five decades of Section 125 case law continues to apply with equal force.

People usually search for this provision at a stressful moment: a wife has been thrown out of the matrimonial home or has left it because of cruelty, a husband has stopped sending money, elderly parents are being ignored by an earning son, or a father has walked away from a child's schooling and medical costs. The law gives an interim, relatively quick answer to "how do I eat and pay rent until the bigger case is decided," rather than waiting years for a civil suit or contested divorce petition to conclude.

Who can claim, and what has to be shown

Four categories of people can apply: a wife (including one divorced and not remarried) unable to maintain herself; a legitimate or illegitimate minor child; a child who has attained majority but cannot maintain itself due to a physical or mental abnormality; and a father or mother unable to maintain themselves. In every case, the applicant must show two things — that the person against whom the claim is made has sufficient means, and that the applicant cannot maintain themselves. A wife loses the right to claim if she is living in adultery, has refused to live with her husband without sufficient reason, or the couple live separately by mutual consent.

Courts have held that "unable to maintain herself" does not mean utter destitution; it means the applicant cannot sustain a standard of living reasonably comparable to the matrimonial home, and some independent income does not disentitle her if it is insufficient to maintain that standard. Allegations against the wife's character — adultery, for instance — must be proved with cogent, admissible evidence and cannot simply be asserted to defeat an otherwise valid claim; courts routinely reject bare, unproven allegations as a ground to deny interim maintenance.

Which court to approach, and the procedure

An application under Section 144, BNSS is filed before the Judicial Magistrate of the First Class of the district where the applicant resides, where the respondent resides, or where they last resided together — a wife-friendly jurisdiction rule that lets an applicant sue close to her own current residence rather than having to chase the respondent to his town. In many States, including Haryana, such applications are also routed through the Family Court where one has been constituted, under the Family Courts Act, 1984.

The application sets out the marriage or relationship, the applicant's income and expenses, the respondent's income and assets to the extent known, and the amount claimed. It should be accompanied by proof of marriage (certificate, photographs, or an affidavit), proof of the applicant's own income or lack of it, and material bearing on the respondent's earning capacity — salary slips, income tax returns, property records, or evidence of lifestyle, business, or landholding where income is not straightforwardly documented. Notice is issued to the respondent, who files a reply, and the Magistrate can grant interim maintenance at an early stage, pending final disposal — this interim relief is often the practical difference between a workable outcome and months of hardship.

How the amount is fixed, and enforcement

The Supreme Court's guidelines in Rajnesh v. Neha require both parties to file a standardised affidavit of assets, income, and expenditure at the outset, cutting down the delay that used to plague these proceedings when income details were disputed for months. Courts weigh the respondent's income and financial capacity, the applicant's own means, the reasonable needs of the applicant and any children in her care, and the standard of living the parties were accustomed to. There is no rigid formula as in MACT compensation; the Magistrate exercises a broad, fact-sensitive discretion, though courts increasingly reference a rough one-fifth to one-fourth share of net income as a starting benchmark before adjusting up or down.

If maintenance goes unpaid, the applicant can seek its recovery in the manner provided for fines, and the Magistrate can, for good cause, sentence the defaulter to up to one month's imprisonment for each month's allowance unpaid, unless it is paid or a warrant executed within that time. This remedy remains available even after divorce; the wife's claim does not automatically end with dissolution of the marriage so long as she has not remarried.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Maintenance applications are among the most frequently filed matrimonial proceedings in the district courts at Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, often running parallel to a Section 12 Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act complaint, a divorce petition, or a Section 9 restitution of conjugal rights case. Getting the initial affidavit of assets and income right, backed by real documents rather than round-number assertions, materially speeds up the grant of interim maintenance and avoids the drawn-out disputes over income that Rajnesh v. Neha was specifically designed to prevent. Where the respondent is agricultural or self-employed — common in this belt — practitioners should be ready to establish income through landholding records, mandi receipts, or bank statements rather than relying solely on an unhelpful or non-existent salary slip.

Statutory cross-references

Section 144, BNSS, 2023 corresponds to Section 125, CrPC, 1973; Sections 145 to 147, BNSS correspond to the old Sections 126 to 128, CrPC, covering procedure, alteration of allowance, and enforcement of maintenance orders. This criminal-law remedy operates independently of, and in addition to, a wife's civil rights to maintenance and permanent alimony under Section 24 and Section 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, or under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 — a claimant is not required to choose only one route, though courts will account for any amount already being paid under another order while fixing the figure under Section 144.
