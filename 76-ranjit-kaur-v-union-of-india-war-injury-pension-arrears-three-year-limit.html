Ranjit Kaur v. Union of India & Ors.: P&H High Court Says a 1971 War-Injury Widow's Pension Arrears Cannot Be Capped at Three Years

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court · Ranjit Kaur v. Union of India & Ors.
BenchJustice Harsimran Singh Sethi & Justice Amarinder Singh Grewal
DateOrder dated 27 August 2026; reported 28 August 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 297
Key SectionsArt. 300-A, Constitution of India · Armed Forces Tribunal Act, 2007 · Entitlement Rules for Casualty Pensionary Awards, 1982

What the case was about

The petitioner's husband was wounded during the 1971 Indo-Pak war, suffering a shell injury to the left side of his face that left him with hypermetropic astigmatism in the left eye. The injury was formally held attributable to military service. Yet the war injury pension that should have followed automatically from that finding never actually reached him at the time — it was granted only years later, after a claim was pursued. The real fight before the High Court was not about entitlement itself, which nobody disputed, but about how far back the arrears could be paid.

The Armed Forces Tribunal, Regional Bench, Chandigarh, in its order dated 21 August 2019, accepted that the pension was due but confined the arrears to the three years preceding the date the original application was filed — a standard limitation-style restriction that Tribunals and pension authorities often apply by default, treating a pension claim much like an ordinary civil claim subject to a limitation period. The widow challenged that restriction before the High Court, arguing that since the benefit was available to her husband even at the relevant time in the past and the State never disputed the underlying facts, the State ought to have granted it on its own rather than waiting to be asked, and certainly should not now be allowed to ration how far back it pays once it concedes the claim was always valid. Counsel for the Union of India did not contest the underlying facts, but argued that the soldier or his family should have "remained vigilant" and claimed the benefit sooner.

What the court held

The Division Bench of Justice Harsimran Singh Sethi and Justice Amarinder Singh Grewal rejected the Union's position in language that left little room for ambiguity: "the person who fought for the nation and suffered a shell injury on his face, resulting in damage to his eye, deserves appreciation and recognition rather than looking for the reasons to deny the claim," and that "entitled benefits should have been extended by the Union of India to such a brave soldier." Framing the real question as whether pension arrears could be time-capped at all when there is no limitation period for claiming a pension in the first place — pension being a recurring cause of action that refreshes with each missed payment — the Court held that the settled position in Balbir Singh and the more recent SGT Girish Kumar ruling required the three-year restriction to be struck down.

Drawing extensively on SGT Girish Kumar, the Bench reiterated that a pension "is neither a bounty nor an ex gratia payment" but a vested, enforceable right that partakes the character of property protected under Article 300-A of the Constitution, incapable of being withheld or reduced except by authority of law — a principle the Court held applies with full force to disability and war injury pension, since that category of pension recognises sacrifice in service rather than mere length of service. Having already accepted, through its own Tribunal, that the entitlement existed and was never in dispute, the Court held the Union could not now "resile and contend that such arrears ought to be confined to a period of three years preceding the claim," since doing so would amount to "acknowledging the right in principle while denying its substantive content in effect." The Court also rejected any limitation-based objection, noting that the broader legal question of broad-banding of disability pension attained finality only in December 2014, so the widow could not fairly be faulted for not having agitated the claim earlier. The Tribunal's three-year cap was set aside, and the petitioner was held entitled to arrears running from the date her husband's entitlement first arose, not merely from three years before the claim was filed.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Haryana sends a disproportionately large number of soldiers into the armed forces, and disability and war-injury pension disputes involving veterans and their widows are a recurring category of writ and Armed Forces Tribunal work that regularly touches district-level practice through connected proceedings, affidavits, and local verification. This ruling gives practitioners a clean, recent precedent to cite whenever a pension sanctioning authority or Tribunal tries to apply an ordinary three-year arrears cap to a disability or war-injury pension case where entitlement itself is not disputed. The core takeaway is that once the State concedes eligibility, it cannot simultaneously ration the financial consequence of that concession by treating the claim as though it were time-barred like an ordinary money suit; pension is a continuing, property-like right, and the limitation clock effectively does not run against it in the way it would against a civil debt. For advocates drafting a writ petition or an appeal from an Armed Forces Tribunal order, this case is also a useful illustration of how the December 2014 broad-banding finality date can be used to defeat a limitation objection where the underlying dispute traces back to an earlier, unsettled area of law.

Statutory cross-references

The case turns on Article 300-A of the Constitution, which has not been altered by any of the 2023 criminal law recodifications and continues to protect pension as a property right that cannot be taken away except by authority of law. Proceedings before the Armed Forces Tribunal are governed by the Armed Forces Tribunal Act, 2007, a standalone civil special statute unaffected by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, both of which apply to criminal matters and have no bearing on pension entitlement litigation of this kind. The pensionary entitlement itself flows from the Entitlement Rules for Casualty Pensionary Awards, 1982, and the broad-banding policy whose finality date the Court relied on to reject the limitation plea.
