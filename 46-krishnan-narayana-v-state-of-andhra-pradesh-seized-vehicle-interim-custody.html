Krishnan Narayana v. State of Andhra Pradesh: Supreme Court Says a Registration Certificate Alone Cannot Decide Who Gets Interim Custody of a Seized Vehicle

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseKrishnan Narayana v. State of Andhra Pradesh & Ors., Supreme Court of India
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & Augustine George Masih
Date27 July 2026
Citation2026 INSC 748 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 724
Key SectionsSs. 451 & 457, CrPC, 1973 (now Ss. 497 & 503, BNSS, 2023)

What the case was about

Two companies, one dispute, and a fleet of earthmoving machinery stuck in the middle. The appellant was a director of M/s Pure Minerals, a granite quarrying outfit, and also held an 80% shareholding in the respondent company, M/s Earth Stein Private Limited. Between 2014 and 2022, a Bolero City pick-up, three excavators, and an Ashok Leyland tipper were purchased and registered in the name of Pure Minerals.

The appellant's version was that he was intimidated at the premises of a third company to which he was owed money, coerced into signing blank papers that were later used to forge his resignation from Earth Stein, and that the respondents then trespassed into the Pure Minerals factory and forcibly drove away the vehicles. An FIR followed on his complaint. The respondent company told a different story: that the appellant had siphoned off ₹1.73 crore from Earth Stein's accounts and used that money to buy the very same vehicles, registering them in his own company's name to keep them out of Earth Stein's reach.

With criminal proceedings pending over the seizure, both sides moved for interim custody of the machinery — first before the Trial Court, then before the Andhra Pradesh High Court after the Trial Court's orders went against each of them in different rounds. The High Court eventually handed interim possession to Earth Stein, subject to an undertaking. The appellant, leaning almost entirely on the fact that the registration certificates stood in his company's name, appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The Bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and Augustine George Masih was unambiguous: a registration certificate is a relevant circumstance, but it is evidentiary, not conclusive, of who is entitled to interim possession. Sections 451 and 457 of the CrPC use the words "possession" and "custody" deliberately — the Court exercising this jurisdiction is not adjudicating title to the property at all. It is only trying to prevent the property from decaying or losing value while the underlying criminal case runs its course. That is a fundamentally narrower inquiry than a civil suit for ownership, and the Court cautioned against treating an interim custody order as if it settles who actually owns the vehicles.

Applying that lens to the facts, the Bench found several circumstances that outweighed the bare registration certificates: the loan EMIs for the vehicles, payable to the financier, had admittedly been paid from Earth Stein's own account rather than the appellant's personal funds; the vehicles were seized from Earth Stein's operational site, where they had continued to be used; and the appellant himself had executed a written undertaking connected to that continued use. Layered on top of this was the fact that the registration certificates themselves were under a cloud — the appellant faced pending allegations of having misappropriated Earth Stein's money specifically to acquire these vehicles in his own company's name. A document procured through the very fraud alleged in the FIR, the Court reasoned, could hardly be treated as decisive proof of entitlement.

On a cumulative assessment of all these circumstances, the Supreme Court agreed with the High Court's view and dismissed the appeals, leaving interim possession with Earth Stein Private Limited pending trial.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Interim custody applications for seized vehicles are a routine, almost weekly, feature of practice before Magistrates and Sessions Courts in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani — tractors and trolleys seized in illegal mining or sand-lifting cases, tippers and JCBs held in theft or forgery disputes between partners, and vehicles recovered in NDPS or excise cases where ownership itself is contested. The instinctive move for most litigants has always been to walk in with the RC book and treat the matter as settled. Krishnan Narayana forecloses that shortcut.

The judgment gives defence counsel and complainants alike a clearer checklist for what a Section 451/457 application should actually plead and prove: who has been in continuous physical possession and use of the vehicle, who has been servicing the loan or hypothecation instalments, whether any undertaking or admission exists on record, and whether the registration itself is tainted by the very allegations under investigation. For advocates representing financiers, partnership disputes, or family-run transport and quarrying businesses — all common in this belt of Haryana — this is now the operative framework to build interim custody applications around, rather than resting the case on paperwork alone. It is equally useful on the other side: a party opposing an interim custody claim can no longer be defeated merely by an opponent producing an RC, if possession, financing, and use point the other way.

It is also a reminder that interim custody orders carry no finality on title. A party who loses at the interim stage retains every right to establish ownership in appropriate civil proceedings or at the conclusion of the criminal trial under the disposal provisions that follow Section 451/497.

Statutory cross-references

Section 451, CrPC, 1973 (order for custody and disposal of property pending trial) now corresponds to Section 497, BNSS, 2023. Section 457, CrPC, 1973 (procedure by police upon seizure of property) now corresponds to Section 503, BNSS, 2023. The underlying scheme — interim custody without adjudication of title, followed by final disposal under the successor to Section 452 CrPC (now Section 498, BNSS) at the conclusion of the case — remains materially unchanged under the new code, so this ruling applies with equal force to seizures and applications now governed by the BNSS.
