Paramjit Kaur v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Says Visa Fraud Accused Must First Publish Her FIRs in Newspapers Before Quashing Is Even Considered

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseParamjit Kaur v. State of Punjab and Others, Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Alok Jain
DateOrder passed on or about 22 July 2026; reported 22–23 July 2026
CitationNeutral citation not yet available in open reporting at the time of writing — see disclaimer
Key SectionsSs. 420 & 406, IPC (cheating & criminal breach of trust) · Punjab Travel Professionals (Regulation) Act · S. 528, BNSS (quashing power)

What the case was about

An FIR had been registered against Paramjit Kaur alleging overseas placement fraud — the kind of case that is depressingly common across rural Punjab and Haryana, where families sell land or pledge jewellery to raise lakhs of rupees for a "sure-shot" Canada, UK or Gulf visa, only to find the agent has taken the money and produced nothing but forged documents, a fake stamp, or a visa application that was never actually filed. The FIR accused her of cheating and criminal breach of trust under the Indian Penal Code, along with violations of the Punjab Travel Professionals (Regulation) Act, which specifically governs licensing and conduct for travel and immigration agents operating in the state.

Kaur approached the Punjab & Haryana High Court under the High Court's inherent quashing jurisdiction, seeking to have the FIR quashed on the ground that she had reached a compromise with the complainant. Quashing on the basis of a private settlement is a well-established route in non-heinous offences, particularly where the dispute is essentially civil or commercial in character and continuing the prosecution would serve no purpose once the parties have settled. The difficulty in this case was that this was not Kaur's only brush with the law — the record showed a pattern of similar allegations against her.

What the court held

Justice Alok Jain did not outright refuse to entertain the quashing plea, but before even reaching the merits of the compromise, the Court held that the nature of the allegations — fraud relating to overseas placement, involving fabricated documents and a fraudulent visa process — are serious enough, and have affected enough "innocent lives" wishing to settle abroad, that the public has a right to know who they are dealing with. The Court noted the petitioner's criminal antecedents on record and observed that before her prayer for quashing could even be considered, the public needed to be made aware of those antecedents.

Accordingly, the Court directed Kaur to publish a detailed public notice in at least five widely circulated newspapers in Punjab, of which at least two must be Punjabi-language papers. That notice must disclose her name, address and contact details, along with particulars of every FIR ever registered against her — not just the present one, and not just the pending ones. The direction expressly covers FIRs that are pending, already decided, or even previously quashed. Only after this public notice requirement is satisfied will the Court proceed to examine whether the quashing plea itself should succeed.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

This order is a genuinely novel use of the High Court's inherent powers, and it deserves attention from every advocate in Haryana who handles visa-fraud, "kabootarbaazi" or immigration-agent cheating complaints — a category of crime that is a significant share of the criminal docket in border districts and NRI-heavy belts of the state. Courts have traditionally treated compromise-based quashing as a fairly mechanical exercise once the complainant confirms settlement and the offence is not one that is barred from compounding. This order signals that repeat offenders in cheating cases involving public trust — especially where large numbers of victims and public money are involved — may face an additional, non-monetary condition before the Court will even engage with the settlement.

For defence counsel, the practical takeaway is that a client's criminal history is no longer just background information the Court might consider while exercising discretion; in cases like this it can become an affirmative condition precedent, imposed proactively, that must be complied with before the quashing application is even taken up on merits. For complainants' counsel and for victims still pursuing separate cases, this order is also useful as a precedent to cite where an accused with a track record of similar fraud seeks quashing purely on the strength of one settled complaint. It builds public accountability into the quashing process itself, rather than leaving it to police records or word of mouth in the local community.

There is also a consumer-protection dimension here that Haryana practitioners should not overlook. Immigration and travel-agent fraud is chronically under-reported because victims are often ashamed of having been cheated, or fear that going to the police will further delay any chance of recovering their money. A public notice naming a repeat offender, printed in Punjabi-language papers with wide rural circulation, functions as an informal warning system for prospective clients — something that licensing authorities under the Punjab Travel Professionals (Regulation) Act and its equivalent framework have not always achieved through registration cancellations alone.

Statutory cross-references

The FIR sections cited — cheating and criminal breach of trust under the Indian Penal Code, 1860 — now correspond to Section 318 (cheating) and Section 316 (criminal breach of trust) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, for offences registered on or after 1 July 2024. The High Court's inherent power to quash FIRs, earlier exercised under Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, is now exercised under the materially similar Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS). State-specific regulatory statutes such as the Punjab Travel Professionals (Regulation) Act continue to operate independently of these central codes and are unaffected by the BNS/BNSS transition.
