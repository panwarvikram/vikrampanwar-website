NDPS Act: The Procedural Lapses That Most Often Lead to Acquittal

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Leading CasesState of Himachal Pradesh v. Surat Singh (2026); State of Punjab v. Baldev Singh (1999, Constitution Bench); Karnail Singh v. State of Haryana (2009, Constitution Bench); Union of India v. Mohanlal (2016)
Citations2026 INSC 240 : 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 246 · (1999) 6 SCC 172 · (2009) 8 SCC 539 · (2016) 3 SCC 379

Key SectionsSections 42, 50 and 52A, NDPS Act, 1985

NDPS cases are won and lost on process, not just quantity

The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985 carries some of the harshest minimum sentences in Indian criminal law, and it builds in correspondingly rigorous procedural safeguards at the search, seizure and sampling stage. Indian courts, from the Supreme Court down, have repeatedly acquitted accused persons — even where a substantial quantity of contraband was recovered — because the investigating agency failed to follow one of these safeguards to the letter. Three provisions account for the overwhelming majority of such acquittals: Section 50 (the right to be searched before a Magistrate or Gazetted Officer), Section 42 (recording information and informing a superior officer before a search), and Section 52A (drawing and certifying samples before a Magistrate).

Section 50: no "third option" of a police officer

In State of Punjab v. Baldev Singh, a Constitution Bench held that before conducting a personal search, an empowered officer must inform the suspect of their right to be searched before a nearby Magistrate or Gazetted Officer — and that this requirement is mandatory, not directory. Failure to do so renders the recovery, and any conviction built on it, unsustainable.

This March, the Supreme Court applied that rule squarely in State of Himachal Pradesh v. Surat Singh, upholding an acquittal in a case involving 11.5 kg of charas recovered from a bag during a police nakabandi. The investigating officer had offered the accused three options — a Magistrate, a Gazetted Officer, or a police officer — where the statute recognises only the first two. Because the "consent" obtained was built on a legally non-existent third option, the Court held the search itself was not validly conducted, and — combined with doubts about the weighing process — dismissed the State's appeal against acquittal.

The lesson for defence counsel is direct: examine the exact wording used in the arrest memo or search memo recording the accused's option. An option that includes a police officer as an alternative to a Magistrate or Gazetted Officer is, by itself, a ground to challenge the search's legality — regardless of which option the accused ultimately picked.

Section 42: information in writing, superior officer informed

Where a search is conducted otherwise than in a public place — typically inside a house, building, or vehicle not stopped in the open — Section 42 requires the empowered officer to record the information received in writing, and to send a copy to a superior officer before undertaking the search. In Karnail Singh v. State of Haryana, a Constitution Bench resolved a long-standing conflict in the case law: total non-compliance with Section 42 is impermissible, but delay in recording or reporting may be excused where genuine urgency — such as risk of the evidence being destroyed or the accused escaping — is shown and explained on record. What will not be excused is silence: an investigating officer who simply never recorded the information or never informed a superior, with no explanation offered, cannot rely on the "substantial compliance" exception.

Section 52A: samples must be drawn before a Magistrate, not just a Gazetted Officer

Once contraband is seized, Section 52A governs how it is inventoried, sampled and eventually destroyed — because narcotics cannot practically be preserved as a case property for years pending trial. In Union of India v. Mohanlal, the Supreme Court held that only samples drawn in the presence of a Magistrate, following an inventory prepared under Section 52A(2), constitute valid primary evidence. Drawing samples merely in the presence of a Gazetted Officer at the time of seizure — a common shortcut in the field — does not meet this standard, and can cast serious doubt on whether the substance later tested by the FSL is even the same substance that was seized from the accused.

Why this matters for Criminal practitioners in Haryana

These three provisions form the front end of every NDPS defence: was the accused offered a legally valid choice under Section 50; was the Section 42 information properly recorded and reported where the search was not in a public place; and were the samples drawn and certified before a Magistrate under Section 52A. This is distinct from — and should be checked alongside — the back-end forensic safeguards discussed in our earlier piece on Satta Ram v. State of Punjab, which turned on broken FSL chain of custody and the absence of homogeneous mixing before sampling. A thorough NDPS defence audits both ends of the chain — search and sampling at the point of seizure, and custody and testing thereafter — since a lapse at either stage can be fatal to the prosecution's case, independent of the quantity recovered.

Statutory cross-references

Sections 42, 50 and 52A of the NDPS Act, 1985 are special-statute provisions and retain their original numbering under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023, since the NDPS Act is not itself renumbered by the BNSS. Where NDPS searches additionally invoke general search-witness safeguards under Section 100 of the CrPC, 1973, that provision now corresponds to Section 103 of the BNSS.
