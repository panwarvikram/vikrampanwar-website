Maharaj Saran v. State of Punjab: Supreme Court Says Section 66 IT Act Offences Are Bailable, Reversing the Punjab & Haryana High Court

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseMaharaj Saran v. State of Punjab & Ors.
BenchJustices M.M. Sundresh & Prasanna B. Varale
DateOrder dated 16 July 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (SC) 707
Key SectionsSs. 66, 72, 84-C & 77B, Information Technology Act, 2000 · S. 78(1)(ii), BNS, 2023

What the case was about

The case began not as a bail matter but as a habeas corpus petition. An FIR had been registered alleging offences under Sections 66 (computer-related offences), 72 (breach of confidentiality and privacy) and 84-C (abetment) of the Information Technology Act, 2000, along with Section 78(1)(ii) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. Acting on this FIR, a Look Out Circular (LOC) was issued, and the appellant's son was detained at an airport while attempting to travel — despite there being no formal order of arrest against him at that stage.

The father approached the Punjab & Haryana High Court with a habeas corpus petition, challenging the legality of the detention flowing from the LOC. While disposing of the writ petition, the High Court made a passing but consequential observation: that the offence under Section 66 of the IT Act was, prima facie, non-bailable. That single observation is what reached the Supreme Court on a limited appeal — not the merits of the FIR, not the propriety of the LOC, but simply the correct bail classification of Section 66.

What the court held

A Bench of Justices M.M. Sundresh and Prasanna B. Varale set aside the High Court's observation in unambiguous terms, holding that the offence under Section 66 of the IT Act is bailable. The reasoning was narrow but important: the High Court had looked at Section 66 in isolation and concluded that the IT Act nowhere expressly labels it "bailable," and had stopped there. What it failed to read was Section 77B of the IT Act, a general provision that deems any offence under the Act punishable with imprisonment of three years or more to be cognizable, and — critically — any offence punishable with imprisonment of three years or less to be bailable.

Section 66 of the IT Act carries a maximum sentence of three years' imprisonment, or a fine which may extend to five lakh rupees, or both. Read together with Section 77B, the Supreme Court held, the "true nature of the offence" makes it a bailable one. The Court's order records: "the view expressed by High Court that Section 66 of the IT Act has not been defined as a bailable offence under the IT Act, stands set aside" — and clarifies that Section 77B "has to be read to ascertain the nature of offence provided under Section 66 of the IT Act." With that clarification, the plea was disposed of.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Cyber-enabled complaints — financial fraud through UPI and net-banking, SIM-swap and OTP theft, unauthorised access to computer systems, morphed images and impersonation on social media — are now a routine part of the docket in every district in Haryana, Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani included. Section 66 of the IT Act is very frequently the lead or accompanying section in such FIRs, often clubbed with provisions of the BNS. It is not uncommon, in practice, for investigating officers, and occasionally even for courts at the first instance, to treat Section 66 as a serious, non-bailable offence simply because it deals with "hacking" or unauthorised computer access, without pausing to check the sentence prescribed and Section 77B's deeming provision.

This ruling gives defence counsel a clean, citable Supreme Court authority to insist that bail in a pure Section 66 case is a matter of right under Section 478 of the BNSS (the successor to Section 436 of the CrPC), to be granted by the police officer or the Magistrate as a matter of course, rather than something to be argued for on merits before a Sessions Court. Where Section 66 is clubbed with other, genuinely non-bailable sections — as is common — the classification of each section still has to be assessed independently, and this ruling supplies the correct starting point for that exercise.

The case also carries a quieter but equally important lesson about Look Out Circulars. An LOC was used here to detain a person at an airport in connection with an FIR where, at least as to one of the principal sections invoked, no custodial interrogation could lawfully have been insisted upon in the first place. Practitioners dealing with LOC-related habeas corpus petitions in Haryana — a state with high outbound travel for work and study — would do well to scrutinise the underlying FIR's bail classification as a first line of attack, exactly as was done here.

Statutory cross-references

The Information Technology Act, 2000 is a special enactment and was not amended by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023; Sections 66, 72, 84-C and 77B of the IT Act continue to apply exactly as before. What has changed is the procedural code around them: the erstwhile Section 436 CrPC, dealing with bail in bailable offences as a matter of right, now corresponds to Section 478 of the BNSS, while the general provision on grant of bail on default now sits at Section 187 BNSS. The FIR in this case also invoked Section 78(1)(ii) of the BNS — the provision on stalking, including its cyber-stalking limb, which corresponds to the erstwhile Section 354D of the Indian Penal Code, 1860. The remedy of habeas corpus itself, invoked here before the High Court, remains anchored in Article 226 of the Constitution and is unaffected by the 2023 criminal law overhaul.
