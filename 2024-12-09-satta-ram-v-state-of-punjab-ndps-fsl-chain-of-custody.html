Satta Ram v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Acquits Opium Accused Over Broken FSL Chain of Custody — NDPS Act Section 18(b)

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court (Division Bench)
BenchJustices Sureshwar Thakur and Sudeepti Sharma
Date of Judgment9 December 2024
Citation2024 NCPHHC 163565 : 2025(1) RCR(Criminal) 225
Key SectionsSection 18(b), NDPS Act, 1985; Section 173 CrPC; Section 52A, NDPS Act

What the case was about

In July 2002, a police party near Meerapur intercepted a Maruti car driven by Satta Ram. On search, conducted after he was offered — and opted for — a search before a gazetted officer, the police recovered 20 kg of opium wrapped in a glazed paper from the car's stepney cavity. Two 10-gram samples were drawn, sealed, and sent for forensic examination; the remaining bulk was split into two containers. The accused could not produce a licence, and a case under Section 18(b) of the NDPS Act, 1985 followed.

At trial, the Forensic Science Laboratory report confirmed the sample contained opium with 4.37% morphine, and the Special Court at Patiala convicted Satta Ram, sentencing him to 12 years' rigorous imprisonment and a fine of Rs. 1 lakh. He appealed to the Punjab & Haryana High Court, more than two decades after the alleged recovery.

What the court held

A Division Bench acquitted Satta Ram, setting aside both the conviction and sentence. The reasoning turned entirely on procedural and forensic integrity, not on any factual dispute about the recovery itself:

  • Broken chain of custody at the FSL: The Forensic Science Laboratory's report confirmed the seals on the sample parcel were intact on receipt, but the examining chemist's report was silent on whether the examined stuff was re-sealed and returned to the sender after testing. Without an endorsement recording re-enclosure and return, the parcels later produced in court could not be reliably linked back to the parcels actually tested at the FSL.
  • No homogeneous mixing of the seized bulk: Citing its earlier Full Bench ruling in Deepak Kumar v. State of Punjab and the Supreme Court's decision in Noor Aga v. State of Punjab, the Bench held that where samples are drawn from a bulk seizure without first homogeneously mixing the entire bulk, a positive lab finding on the sample does not prove the charge for the full weight of the seizure. Any charge based on the entire 20 kg, absent this mixing step, could not be sustained beyond reasonable doubt.
  • Doubt benefits the accused: The Court illustrated the principle with an analogy — if only some packets among several recovered contain contraband, mixing everything together before sampling makes it impossible to know what was actually tested. The same logic applied here: procedural shortcuts in sampling created reasonable doubt that had to be resolved in the accused's favour.
  • Result: The conviction and 12-year sentence were quashed, and Satta Ram was ordered released and his fine refunded — a stark illustration of how forensic and procedural lapses, even where a substantial recovery is not otherwise disputed, can unravel a matter decades after trial.

Why this matters for Criminal practitioners in Haryana

This judgment is essential reading for anyone handling NDPS matters before the District Courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, where recovery and sampling procedure is frequently the actual battleground rather than the fact of possession itself. Defence counsel should scrutinise every FSL report for an explicit endorsement confirming re-sealing and return of the examined sample — its absence, per this ruling, is not a technicality but a substantive break in the chain of custody capable of founding an acquittal.

Equally important is the homogeneous mixing requirement. In matters involving multi-packet or bulk seizures — a recurring fact pattern in Haryana's NDPS caseload along the national highways and border districts — counsel should examine the recovery memo and site plan for any indication that the entire bulk was mixed before sampling. Its absence is now a well-established ground to challenge the weight-based charge, even where the sample itself tests positive.

For prosecutors and investigating officers, the case is a pointed reminder that NDPS recoveries require meticulous documentation at the FSL stage — the Section 52A inventory and sampling procedure, not just the initial seizure, must be defence-proof if a conviction is to survive appellate scrutiny years down the line.

Statutory cross-references

The NDPS Act, 1985 remains a standalone special statute unaffected by the BNS/BNSS transition; Section 18(b) (punishment for possession, sale, or manufacture of opium) and Section 52A (disposal of seized narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances) continue to apply as before. However, the procedural framework surrounding investigation, recovery memos, and inquiry now operates under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023 in place of the CrPC — the Section 173 CrPC report referenced in this case corresponds to Section 193 BNSS today. Practitioners citing this judgment in matters investigated after 1 July 2024 should make this cross-reference explicit before the trial court.

This is a significant NDPS Act Section 18 Punjab Haryana High Court ruling on sampling procedure, and a useful precedent alongside Deepak Kumar v. State of Punjab and the Supreme Court's Noor Aga v. State of Punjab for any narcotics matter turning on FSL chain-of-custody or bulk-mixing defects.
