Reliance General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Om Parkash & Ors.: Supreme Court Says an Insurer Isn't Ultimately Liable Where the Driver's Licence Was Invalid, But Must Still Pay and Recover

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseReliance General Insurance Company Ltd. v. Om Parkash & Ors.
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & N. Kotiswar Singh
Date30 July 2026
CitationCivil Appeal Nos. of 2026, arising out of SLP(C) Nos. 6743–6744 of 2023
Key SectionsSs. 149, 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 · Ss. 61–65, Indian Evidence Act, 1872

What the case was about

The dispute traces back to a road accident on 14 October 2009 near Panipat, in which a vehicle driven by Om Parkash collided with a two-wheeler. Rash and negligent driving was never seriously contested; what was contested was whether Om Parkash held a valid driving licence on the date of the accident, and if not, who should ultimately bear the compensation.

The Motor Accident Claims Tribunal, Panipat, awarded the claimant Rs. 86,95,947 with 7.5% interest, but held only the driver and the vehicle owner, Rajinder Singh, liable — not the insurer, Reliance General Insurance. The Tribunal's finding was that the driver's licence had expired on 21 June 2007 and was renewed only on 25 August 2010, after payment of a penalty for the intervening gap, which placed the accident squarely within the unlicensed period.

On appeal, the Punjab & Haryana High Court took a different view. It relied on a letter from a Motor Licensing Officer stating that licence renewal records for 2007 to 2010 had been lost owing to a technical error during a data migration between two outsourced vendors, and on that basis held the insurer liable, also enhancing the total award to over Rs. 1.08 crore with 8% interest. The insurer then appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

A Bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and N. Kotiswar Singh reversed the High Court and restored the Tribunal's finding that the insurer was not ultimately liable. The Court's central holding turns on a point of evidence law that has wide application well beyond motor accident claims: official records must ordinarily be proved by primary evidence, and secondary evidence — such as an unverified letter asserting that records were lost — is admissible only where the absence of the primary source has itself been satisfactorily explained and proved.

Relying on the Constitution Bench decision in Neeraj Dutta v. State (NCT of Delhi) and on Tharammel Peethambaran v. T. Ushakrishnan, the Court held that no official record was produced to show either the actual loss of the licensing data or what recovery steps the transport department had taken. A bare oral assertion and an unverified letter could not substitute for that proof. The Court also invoked its earlier ruling in Telangana State Level Police Recruitment Board v. Penjarla Vijay Kumar to reject the argument that a late renewal operates retroactively — the fact that a penalty was paid for late renewal was itself, the Court said, an admission that the licence had lapsed.

Importantly, the Court did not leave the victim without a remedy. Applying the well-established "pay and recover" principle, it directed that compensation already released to the claimant be retained, while giving the insurer liberty to recover the amount from the vehicle owner and the driver. The Court also placed the burden squarely on owners: the owner here never entered the witness box to show reasonable care in verifying the driver's licence, and the Court held — citing Beli Ram v. Rajinder Kumar, approving National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Hem Raj — that owners owe a responsibility to the public to check this before handing over the wheel.

Beyond the parties, the Bench flagged a systemic concern, urging the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways and state transport departments to run awareness campaigns on licence renewal, streamline the renewal process, regulate driving schools, and make tests more accessible in regional languages — observing that an expired licence can expose an ordinary owner or driver to liability running into crores of rupees that insurance was never going to cover.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

"Pay and recover" is not a new doctrine, but this judgment sharpens two things that come up constantly in Haryana's MACT practice, where a large share of claims arise on national and state highways with heavy commercial and private vehicle traffic. First, insurers defending on the ground of an invalid or expired licence cannot simply plead the lapse and expect the Tribunal to exonerate them outright — the claimant must still be paid, and the insurer's remedy is a separate recovery against the owner and driver, not a defence that lets it walk away from the award altogether. Claimants' counsel should insist on this structure at the Tribunal stage, rather than letting an insurer's licence-validity defence be treated as full exoneration.

Second, this judgment cautions counsel defending vehicle owners against recovery claims: a licensing authority's letter asserting that records were "lost in migration," without more, will not survive scrutiny. If an owner's defence depends on showing a licence was in fact valid despite missing departmental records, that proof has to be built through primary evidence — original entries or certified extracts — not a bare covering letter. Given how often Haryana's own RTO offices report data-migration gaps for licences issued in the 2000s, local practitioners will keep meeting this fact pattern, and this judgment sets the evidentiary bar they will be tested against. For advocates advising vehicle owners generally, the case is also a useful client-counselling tool: a real award exceeding a crore makes concrete why checking a driver's licence before handing over the keys is not a formality but a direct personal financial exposure.

Statutory cross-references

The core claim provisions here — Sections 166 and 168 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, governing the claim and the Tribunal's award, and Section 149, governing the insurer's statutory defences and the pay-and-recover mechanism — remain in force unchanged and are not affected by the 2023 criminal law recodification, since the Motor Vehicles Act is a separate civil/regulatory statute. The evidentiary provisions relied on by the Court, Sections 61 to 65 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (governing proof of documents by primary and secondary evidence), broadly correspond to Sections 57 to 60 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 for matters arising after 1 July 2024; practitioners should verify the precise BSA section against the facts of a new matter rather than assume a mechanical one-to-one match, as the BSA reorganised and expanded some of these provisions.
