S. Sangeetha & Ors. v. Tmt. P. Ponni: Supreme Court Says Marking a Document as an Exhibit Is Not Proof of Its Contents

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — S. Sangeetha & Ors. v. Tmt. P. Ponni & Ors.
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & N. Kotiswar Singh
Date7 August 2026
Citation2026 INSC 813
Key SectionsOrder XIII Rule 3, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 · Ss. 61–65, Indian Evidence Act, 1872

What the case was about

The dispute began in 2019, when Tmt. P. Ponni filed a suit before the High Court of Judicature at Madras seeking probate of the will of one late C. Shyamalavalli. S. Sangeetha and the other appellants, who contested the will, filed a written statement opposing probate. As the case proceeded, the plaintiff filed a proof affidavit in support of her claim, exhibiting a set of documents along with it.

The appellants were unhappy with what had been let in through that affidavit. They moved an application asking the court to eschew — strike out and disregard — the proof affidavit, several of its averments, and the documents exhibited with it, arguing that certain documents and statements had nothing to do with the probate proceeding: some referred to other suits pending between family members, others to transfers of property within the same family structure. If those documents had no bearing on the will's genuineness, the appellants argued, they should not sit on the record at all.

A Single Judge of the Madras High Court rejected that application. The reasoning was straightforward: marking a will as an exhibit is different from proving it is genuine, and a will can be marked into evidence through the testimony of its beneficiary. Filing an affidavit that mentions certain facts is likewise not the same as proving those facts — but that goes to how much weight the material eventually carries, not to whether it can be looked at at all. The appellants' further appeal within the High Court failed on the same logic, and the matter reached the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The Bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and N. Kotiswar Singh dismissed the appeal and upheld the courts below, but with a careful, two-part answer rather than a blanket one.

On one narrow point, the Court agreed with the appellants: where the plaintiff had filed photocopies of certain documents in place of the originals, without any explanation for why the originals weren't produced, the Single Judge had rightly kept those out. That part of the exclusion stood.

But for the rest of the material — documents about other pending suits and property transfers within the family — the Court held that the objections raised did not show the documents to be prima facie inadmissible or irrelevant to the properties actually in dispute. Family litigation over a will rarely exists in isolation; other pending suits and property dealings within the same family can bear on motive, conduct, and competing claims to the estate. The Bench invoked the settled principle that mere marking of a document as an exhibit is not proof of the contents thereof — a document can be admitted onto the record at a low threshold, but its contents still have to be established through evidence led and tested under the law governing proof of documents.

Turning to Order XIII Rule 3 of the CPC, which empowers a court to reject documents that are irrelevant or legally inadmissible, the Bench held that this power is not meant to be exercised as a routine screening tool at the threshold stage. Since the documents were not shown to be inadmissible or irrelevant on their face, striking them out before trial was not warranted. The correct course was to let them remain on record and let their evidentiary value be tested when the suit is finally adjudicated — not shut the door on them at an interlocutory stage.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

This is not, at its core, a will case — it is a ruling about how civil trials in India are meant to be run, and it applies with equal force to a partition suit, a specific performance suit, or a money recovery suit heading to trial before a District or Civil Judge in Charkhi Dadri or Bhiwani. Two stages get conflated far too often in mofussil practice: the marking of a document as an exhibit, which is a formal, low-threshold act of bringing a document onto the record, and the proof of that document, which requires it to be established through primary or secondary evidence and to survive cross-examination on its contents.

Advocates on the receiving end of an inconvenient document should resist filing an Order XIII Rule 3 application hoping to have it thrown out before trial begins, unless the document is genuinely and facially inadmissible — say, an unstamped instrument the law bars from being looked at for a stated purpose. Where the objection is really about weight, genuineness, or relevance depending on surrounding facts, the sustainable strategy is to let the document go on record and attack its credibility during cross-examination and final arguments, where the trial court must actually reason through the point.

Equally, this is a reminder for the side relying on a document not to treat exhibiting it as the end of the job. A document marked "Ex. P-1" still needs a witness who can speak to its execution, custody, or contents, and that witness still has to survive cross-examination. Courts in Haryana handling the growing volume of succession and probate matters — a category expanding as agricultural land keeps changing hands across generations — will find this a useful, recent restatement of both halves of that rule.

Statutory cross-references

The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, including Order XIII Rule 3 discussed in this judgment, is unaffected by the 2023 criminal law reforms and continues to apply exactly as before. The Indian Evidence Act, 1872 provisions on proof of documents by primary and secondary evidence — Sections 61 to 65, which underlie the "marking is not proof" principle — have since been re-enacted, for evidence led in proceedings governed by the new law, as Sections 56 to 60 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. The substance of the proof requirement is carried forward unchanged; only the section numbers have shifted. Practitioners should check which enactment governs a given proceeding based on when it was instituted or when the relevant evidence is being led.
