Kulvir Singh & Rajvir Kaur v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Says Demanding Loan Repayment Isn't Abetment of Suicide Without a Proximate Overt Act

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseKulvir Singh & Rajvir Kaur v. State of Punjab, Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Manisha Batra
Date30 July 2026
CitationReported by LiveLaw and Law Trend, 30 July 2026 (formal neutral citation not yet available in open sources)
Key SectionsS. 306 & S. 107, IPC, 1860 · S. 482, CrPC, 1973

What the case was about

On 25 September 2016, a family of four in Jalandhar — Anil Aggarwal, his wife, and their two children — died by suicide, leaving behind a note that named eight people. The note alleged that the family had been under continuing pressure from lenders to repay money even though, according to the family, the principal amount along with interest had already been cleared. Police registered an FIR under Section 306 of the IPC against all eight named persons.

Investigation cleared five of the eight. Two of the remaining three — Kulvir Singh and Rajvir Kaur, a couple who the police alleged had been pressing the family for loan recovery — approached the High Court to have the FIR and all consequent proceedings against them quashed under Section 482 CrPC. Their counsel argued that the couple ran a home-based tiffin service and were not moneylenders in any commercial sense, questioned the genuineness of a suicide note apparently written and signed by a single hand despite four deaths, and pointed to a Special Investigation Team inquiry in which the complainant — the deceased's sister — herself said she did not know the real reason for the deaths and was no longer interested in pursuing the case. The State opposed quashing, arguing that the note named the accused specifically, by name and phone number, and that its evidentiary value should be tested at trial rather than cut off at the threshold.

What the court held

Justice Manisha Batra quashed the FIR and all proceedings against the two petitioners. The Court's reasoning rests on a distinction that recurs constantly in Section 306 jurisprudence but is easy to lose sight of at the FIR stage: a suicide note naming a person, and even genuine allegations of harassment connected to a money dispute, do not by themselves make out the offence. What Section 306 requires, read with the definition of abetment in Section 107 IPC, is proof of instigation, conspiracy, or intentional aid — a positive act, not merely persistent unpleasantness.

The Court held that demands for repayment of a loan, even if repeated and even if accompanied by allegations of harassment or intimidation, do not satisfy the ingredients of abetment in the absence of any overt act that is proximate in time to the suicide and that can be shown to have left the deceased with no option but to end their life. The single-page note recovered from the scene was, in the Court's assessment, an expression of the victim's personal anguish and desperation rather than evidence of a deliberate design by the named individuals to drive the family to suicide. Continuing a criminal prosecution on that material, the Court held, would amount to an abuse of the process of law.

It is worth being precise about what the judgment does not say. It does not hold that lenders can never face liability for aggressive recovery tactics, and it does not disturb the well-settled line of authority — going back to decisions of the Supreme Court on Section 306 — that a proximate, positive act of instigation close in time to the suicide can still sustain a prosecution. What it reaffirms is the threshold requirement: general unhappiness, financial stress, or friction over money is not, by itself, abetment.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Suicide-note-driven FIRs are common in Haryana's district courts, whether they arise from loan disputes, matrimonial friction, or workplace pressure, and they are among the most emotionally charged cases a practitioner will handle. Investigating officers frequently register offences against every name that appears in a note without independently testing whether the material discloses a positive act of instigation proximate to the death. This judgment gives defence counsel a clean, recent, and directly applicable precedent to press at the quashing stage itself, rather than waiting for a full trial to establish the same point through cross-examination years later.

For complainants' counsel and prosecutors, the case is a reminder to build the FIR and subsequent investigation around specific, dated, proximate acts — a threat made shortly before the death, a specific ultimatum, conduct that can be shown to have been the final push — rather than relying on the cumulative weight of a note naming several people over a long-running dispute. A prosecution built only on "harassment in general" connected to money owed is now more vulnerable to quashing at the threshold, even where the underlying grief and loss are real and the note itself is not in dispute.

The ruling also illustrates how Section 482 CrPC quashing petitions function as an early check on prosecutions that, however sympathetic the underlying tragedy, do not disclose the ingredients of the offence charged. Advocates drafting such petitions should structure the argument the way the Court did here: first isolate what the FIR and the material collected during investigation actually allege, then test that material strictly against the statutory definition of abetment, rather than against the moral weight of the tragedy.

Statutory cross-references

The FIR in this matter was registered in 2016 and was therefore rightly dealt with under the old Indian Penal Code and Code of Criminal Procedure. For offences committed after 1 July 2024, the equivalent provisions are Section 108 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (abetment of suicide, in place of Section 306 IPC) and Section 45 of the BNS, 2023 (definition of abetment, in place of Section 107 IPC) — the substance of both provisions is carried over unchanged. The power to quash an FIR or proceedings, exercised here under Section 482 CrPC, now lies under Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.
