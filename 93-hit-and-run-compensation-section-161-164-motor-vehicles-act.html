Hit-and-Run Compensation Under Sections 161 & 164 of the Motor Vehicles Act: How a Victim Gets Paid When the Driver Is Never Traced

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing LawMotor Vehicles Act, 1988 (as amended 2019)
SchemeCompensation to Victims of Hit and Run Motor Accidents Scheme, 2022
In force from1 April 2022 (superseded the Solatium Scheme, 1989)
Key SectionsSs. 161, 162, 164 & 164B, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988
Compensation (hit-and-run)₹2,00,000 (death) · ₹50,000 (grievous hurt)

What the problem is

Most motor accident compensation claims in Haryana's district courts assume there is somebody to sue: a traceable driver, a registered vehicle, and an insurer standing behind that vehicle's policy. A hit-and-run accident breaks that assumption at the first step. The offending vehicle speeds off, no number plate is noted, no FIR names an accused, and the victim's family is left with a body, a hospital bill, and no defendant to claim against under the ordinary fault-based framework of Sections 165 and 166 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988.

Parliament built a separate, no-fault compensation route for such cases decades ago. What often confuses claimants, and sometimes their advocates, is that two distinct routes run in parallel under the Act: the hit-and-run Solatium Scheme under Section 161, and general no-fault liability under Section 164. They serve different situations, pay different amounts, and follow different procedures — conflating the two is one of the more common early mistakes in filing these claims.

Section 161: the hit-and-run route, when nobody can be identified

Section 161 exists for exactly one scenario: the vehicle that caused the accident cannot be identified. This is what "hit and run" means in the statute's technical sense — not merely that the driver fled, but that the offending vehicle's identity remains unknown even after investigation. Where the vehicle is identified but the driver simply absconds, the claim proceeds as an ordinary claim against the vehicle's insurer or owner, not under this special scheme.

The 2019 amendment substantially increased payouts under this head, and the Central Government notified the revised amounts through the Compensation to Victims of Hit and Run Motor Accidents Scheme, 2022, in force from 1 April 2022, superseding the earlier Solatium Scheme of 1989. A hit-and-run death now carries ₹2,00,000, up from ₹25,000, and grievous hurt carries ₹50,000, up from ₹12,500 — a fixed, statutory sum that does not depend on proving income, dependency, or any multiplier-based calculation.

Because there is no identified insurer to pay this sum, the money is drawn from a dedicated corpus. Section 164B of the Act establishes the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund, financed through contributions specified in the Act, and it is this Fund, administered through the General Insurance Council under the scheme, that actually disburses hit-and-run compensation once a claim is verified.

How to actually file a Section 161 claim

The claimant, ordinarily a legal heir in a death case or the injured person in a grievous hurt case, must file before the Claims Tribunal having jurisdiction over the area where the accident occurred, generally within six months, though delay can be condoned on sufficient cause. Two documents anchor the claim: the FIR, which must record that the offending vehicle could not be identified, and, in a death case, the post-mortem report or death certificate connecting the death to the accident; in a grievous hurt case, contemporaneous medical records and an injury or disability certificate serve the same purpose.

Because Section 161 compensation is a fixed statutory sum rather than a computed award, the Tribunal's inquiry is comparatively narrow: confirming the accident occurred, that it genuinely qualifies as hit-and-run, and that the claimant is the correct legal heir or injured party. This makes these claims procedurally faster than a full income-and-multiplier MACT claim, though claimants sometimes find the fixed amount modest relative to what a fault-based claim against an identified, insured vehicle might have yielded.

Section 164: no-fault liability, when the vehicle is known but fault is disputed

Section 164 covers a different situation entirely: the vehicle and its insurer are known, but establishing whose fault the accident was, the driver's, the victim's, or shared, would ordinarily take a full trial on evidence. Rather than making a grieving family wait years for that determination before receiving anything, Section 164 allows a claim on a no-fault basis, meaning the claimant does not have to prove negligence at all to receive this baseline sum. It compensates a fixed amount, currently ₹5,00,000 for death and ₹2,50,000 for permanent disability, payable by the insurer (or owner, where uninsured) regardless of who was actually at fault.

Critically, claiming under Section 164 does not shut the door on pursuing a larger, fault-based award under Sections 166 and 168. A claimant can, and often should, pursue both: take the no-fault sum quickly to cover immediate needs, funeral expenses, hospital bills, lost income, while the fuller compensation claim, computed on the Sarla Verma multiplier method this blog has covered separately, proceeds on its own timeline. Any amount received under Section 164 is adjusted against, not added on top of, the final award under Section 166, functioning as an interim advance rather than a separate windfall.

The golden hour: Section 162 and cashless treatment

A related but distinct protection sits in Section 162, mandating cashless treatment during the "golden hour" — the first hour after a traumatic injury, when prompt care has the highest chance of preventing death. Following Supreme Court directions, the Cashless Treatment Scheme, 2025 now provides cashless treatment up to ₹1.5 lakh for up to seven days at designated hospitals, regardless of who caused the accident or whether the vehicle is ever identified. This is a treatment benefit, not compensation, but it removes the immediate financial barrier to emergency care that hit-and-run victims, lacking anyone to bill, would otherwise face at the hospital door.

Why this matters for practitioners and litigants in Haryana

Haryana's highway corridors, particularly stretches of NH-9 and NH-152D running through Bhiwani, Charkhi Dadri and neighbouring districts, see a steady volume of hit-and-run accidents involving heavy vehicles that leave the scene before identification. For families in these cases, the practical first step is ensuring the FIR is drafted to accurately record that the vehicle could not be identified, since a vague FIR that never confirms investigation genuinely failed to trace it can create avoidable friction before the Tribunal later.

Advocates should also flag, at the outset, that a Section 161 claim and a full Section 166 claim are not mutually exclusive if the vehicle is later traced, say through CCTV footage or a chargesheet naming an accused; the claim then shifts to the ordinary fault-based track and the Section 161 route falls away. Conversely, where a driver is named in the FIR but simply cannot be located, that is not a Section 161 case at all — the vehicle is known, so the claim proceeds under Sections 165, 166 and 164 against the insurer regardless.

Statutory cross-references

The hit-and-run and no-fault liability provisions discussed here sit in the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, as amended by the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019: Section 161 (special provisions for hit-and-run compensation), Section 162 (golden hour cashless treatment scheme), Section 164 (no-fault liability compensation), and Section 164B (Motor Vehicle Accident Fund). These are read alongside the Compensation to Victims of Hit and Run Motor Accidents Scheme, 2022 and the Cashless Treatment Scheme, 2025, both notified under the Act's rule-making power. None of these provisions have renumbered equivalents under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita or Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, since the Motor Vehicles Act remains a separate, standalone statute outside the 2023 criminal law recodification; the applicable FIR for the underlying accident, however, would now typically invoke Sections 106 and 281 of the BNS, 2023 (causing death by negligence, and rash driving) in place of the earlier Sections 304A and 279 IPC.
