Mahinder & Others v. Puran Singh: Supreme Court Holds Section 22 HSA's Preferential Right Extends to Agricultural Land

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseMahinder & Others v. Puran Singh, Supreme Court of India
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & Nongmeikapam Kotiswar Singh
Date14 July 2026
Citation2026 INSC 698 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 675
Key SectionsS. 22, Hindu Succession Act, 1956; Entry 5, List III, Constitution of India

What the case was about

Section 22 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 gives Class-I heirs who have jointly inherited property from the same intestate a preferential right — if one of them wants to sell or transfer his inherited share to an outsider, he must first offer it to his co-heirs on the same terms. It is a familiar provision in Haryana and Punjab, where joint ancestral agricultural land passing to siblings on a father's death is the norm rather than the exception, and disputes over who gets first refusal on a share are common in our civil courts.

In this case, a father's Class-I heirs inherited his agricultural land. Several of the siblings — directly or through their own legal heirs — sold their combined shares to a third party by a sale deed dated 28 December 2011. Before that sale deed was executed, one sibling had already filed a petition under Section 22 on 8 December 2011, seeking to exercise his preferential right to buy out the shares being sold rather than let them pass to an outsider.

The Civil Court at Karnal dismissed that petition. It relied on the Constitution Bench ruling in Atam Prakash v. State of Haryana, (1986) 2 SCC 249, which had struck down Section 15 of the Punjab Pre-emption Act, 1913 as unconstitutional because the list of persons entitled to pre-empt under that Act bore no rational connection to any legitimate object — it favoured certain blood relations over others for no defensible reason. The trial court reasoned that Section 22 HSA was similarly structured and must fall too. The First Appellate Court disagreed, relying on the precedent in Babu Ram v. Santokh Singh (2019), which had already held Section 22 HSA applicable to agricultural land, and the High Court's second appeal affirmed that view. The original defendants then approached the Supreme Court, arguing that Babu Ram was wrongly decided because it had overlooked Parliament's and the state legislature's separate competences over agricultural land under Entries 18 and 14 of the State List.

What the court held

The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal and upheld Section 22 HSA's applicability to agricultural land. The Bench drew a sharp distinction between the Punjab Pre-emption Act's Section 15, which extended pre-emption rights to a wide, loosely connected class including tenants, co-sharers and even vicinal owners with no blood relationship to the seller, and Section 22 HSA, which is confined strictly to Class-I heirs who inherited together from the same person. As the Court put it, no tenant, no distant relation and no unrelated co-owner can invoke Section 22 the way they could invoke the old Punjab Act — the right "lives and dies with the succession relationship." That structural difference meant the constitutional infirmity identified in Atam Prakash could not simply be transplanted onto an entirely different, unrelated provision that was never under challenge in that earlier case.

On the competence question, the Court applied the doctrine of pith and substance. It held that Section 22's true character is that of a succession provision, an "incident of succession and nothing more," falling within Entry 5 of List III (the Concurrent List, covering intestacy and succession) — not Entry 18 of the State List, which governs transfer of agricultural land generally. The Court traced the doctrinal foundation of pre-emption as "a right of substitution but not of re-purchase" through the Constitution Bench in Audh Behari Singh v. Gajadhar Jaipuria, (1954) 1 SCC 836, and the four-judge bench in Bishan Singh v. Khazan Singh, 1958 SCC OnLine SC 88, describing it as a weak right that can be lost through acquiescence. It further noted that the pre-Constitution Government of India Act, 1935 had expressly excluded agricultural land from the succession entry, and Parliament's deliberate omission of that exclusion from the current Constitution's Entry 5 confirmed its unrestricted competence over succession to all property, agricultural or otherwise. The appeal was dismissed with no order as to costs, and the Court held that no occasion arose to refer Babu Ram to a larger Bench.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

This ruling closes a live and recurring argument in Haryana and Punjab civil litigation. Defendants in Section 22 suits involving farmland have, for years, tried the Atam Prakash route to argue the provision itself is constitutionally suspect, hoping to defeat a co-heir's pre-emption claim on a threshold point rather than on facts. That argument is now settled against them at the Supreme Court level. For practitioners advising clients on partition, family settlements or sale of ancestral agricultural land in joint names, the message is straightforward: a Class-I heir's preferential right under Section 22 is fully enforceable over agricultural holdings, and any attempted sale to an outsider without first offering the share to co-heirs remains vulnerable to a Section 22 suit, provided it is filed at the right stage — here, notably, before the impugned sale deed was even executed.

The judgment is also a useful reminder on drafting strategy: raising a constitutional challenge to a well-established statutory provision as a defence is a high-risk move that can consume years of litigation before failing at the highest court, when the underlying facts might have been contested on much narrower and more immediately effective grounds.

Statutory cross-references

This is a civil succession matter under a special Act, not the criminal codes, so there is no BNS/BNSS mapping to note. The operative provision, Section 22 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, remains unamended and unaffected by the 2023 criminal law re-codification. Practitioners should read it alongside Section 4 of the same Act (overriding effect of the Act over other Hindu law and custom) and, where relevant, the East Punjab Urban Rent Restriction Act and Punjab Pre-emption Act, 1913 — the latter now substantially hollowed out by Atam Prakash and subsequent legislative amendments, and expressly distinguished from Section 22 HSA in this judgment.
