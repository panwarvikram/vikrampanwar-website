Bhag Singh v. Basant Kaur: Supreme Court Says a Dera's Decades of Cultivation Proves Possession, Not Ownership

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Bhag Singh (D) Thr. Mahant Kashmir Singh v. Basant Kaur (D) Thr. LRs. & Ors.
BenchJustices Prashant Kumar Mishra & Shree Chandrashekhar
Date10 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 983 · Civil Appeal No. 1718 of 2016
Key SectionsS. 100, CPC, 1908 · Limitation Act, 1963 · Doctrine of Religious Dedication (Dharam-Arth)

What the case was about

A dispute over a modest 4 Kanals 18 Marlas of agricultural land in the revenue estate of Muktsar, Punjab, took forty-five years to reach a final answer — and along the way, it forced the Supreme Court to untangle two claims that a local Dera had tried to run side by side: that the land had been permanently dedicated to it for religious purposes, and that even if it hadn't, decades of cultivation by its Mahants had ripened into ownership by adverse possession.

The plaintiffs traced their title to a registered sale deed dated 13 May 1965, executed in their predecessor's favour by two vendors, Gajjan Singh and Baggu Singh. Their case was straightforward on paper: a valid registered conveyance, with possession recited as having passed at the time of execution. The complication was that the revenue records never caught up with the sale deed. Long after 1965, the possession column of the Jamabandi still carried the name of one Attar Singh, described as holding the land as "gair marusi bila lagan bawaja Dharam Arth" — a non-occupancy tenant, without payment of rent, on account of religious and charitable purposes. Attar Singh had in fact died nearly two decades before the suit was even filed in 1981.

The defendants, appearing on behalf of Dera Bhai Mastan Singh, built their resistance on that revenue entry. Their case was that the land had ceased to be private property well before 1965, having already been irrevocably dedicated to the Dera for Dharam-Arth. After Attar Singh, they said, successive Mahants — Kishan Singh, Santa Singh, and finally Bhag Singh — continued in open, unbroken cultivating possession for decades, which, even independently of any dedication, had matured into ownership by adverse possession. They also argued, almost as a fallback, that Gajjan Singh and Baggu Singh together owned only a half share in the suit land, with the remaining half belonging to a third party, so the 1965 sale deed could not in any event have conveyed the whole property.

What the court held

The trial court and the first appellate court had both ruled for the Dera, leaning heavily on the 1945-46 Jamabandi entry to conclude that a dedication had taken place and that Attar Singh's possession, and the Mahants' possession after him, was representative of the religious institution rather than personal. In 2011, the Punjab and Haryana High Court reversed both findings in second appeal under Section 100 CPC, holding that the revenue entries proved nothing more than who was recorded as being in possession, and that there was no evidence of a completed dedication or of any hostile assertion of title against the recorded owners. The Supreme Court has now affirmed that reversal and dismissed the Dera's appeal.

The judgment's central move is to separate, cleanly, the question of who was physically occupying the land from the question of who owned it. Revenue records such as Jamabandis and Khasra Girdawaris, the Court held, are maintained for fiscal and administrative purposes; they are admissible evidence of possession, but they neither create nor extinguish title, and ownership has to be established through independent, substantive evidence of the actual source of title. An entry describing cultivation as being "bawaja Dharam Arth" tells you why the entry-keeper believed someone was in occupation, not that the recorded owner had permanently and unequivocally divested himself of his rights in favour of a religious institution. The burden of proving a completed dedication rests squarely on the party asserting it, and a revenue entry by itself does not discharge that burden.

The Court then flagged something more fundamental in the Dera's own pleadings: it had run dedication and adverse possession as parallel, mutually reinforcing arguments, when the two are legally incompatible foundations. If the land had already vested in the Dera through a completed dedication, there was no occasion left for it to separately acquire the same title by adverse possession — adverse possession presumes that title vests in someone else and is subsequently extinguished by possession that is hostile to that person. A claimant cannot simultaneously say "the property was always ours by gift" and "we made it ours by taking it against the true owner's will." The Dera's own account of how its Mahants came into possession — through a religious dedication, not through any act of defiance against Attar Singh or the recorded proprietors — meant its possession was never hostile at its inception, which is fatal to an adverse possession plea regardless of how long that possession continued.

On adverse possession as a doctrine, the Court reiterated principles drawn from its own precedents, including T. Anjanappa v. Somalingappa: possession must be actual, open, continuous, and hostile, amounting to a clear and unequivocal denial of the true owner's title, and this hostility must be affirmatively pleaded and proved — specifically, the claimant must identify when the possession turned hostile, so that the limitation clock can even be said to have started running. Neither the pleadings nor the evidence here identified any such starting point. The Court also rejected the argument that failure of the Dera's claims automatically perfected the plaintiffs' title to the whole of the land; since the vendors may have held only a half share, a transferor cannot convey a better title than he possesses, but that possibility does not, by itself, hand ownership to the defendants either — each side must succeed on the strength of its own title, not the weakness of the other's.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Muktsar sits in Punjab, but the fact pattern here is one every district court lawyer in Haryana will recognise instantly: old agrarian land, a chain of oral or informal dedications to a Dera, Gurdwara, temple, or family deity, and revenue records that were never updated to match a later registered sale. Litigants routinely walk in believing that a decades-old Jamabandi entry showing a particular person's name in the possession column settles the ownership question. This judgment is a useful, citable reminder that it does not — and that a lawyer defending a client's registered sale deed against a rival claim rooted purely in revenue entries has strong ground to stand on, provided the opposing side cannot point to independent proof of a completed transfer of title.

It is equally a caution for lawyers representing the other side. A common defensive strategy in property suits is to plead every available theory at once: dedication, adverse possession, estoppel, and so on, hoping one sticks. This judgment shows that pleading dedication and adverse possession together can actively undermine both, because the two theories contradict each other's premise about how possession began. Practitioners drafting written statements in title suits pending before the Civil Judge or Additional District Judge in Bhiwani or Charkhi Dadri should treat this as a drafting discipline point: identify, and commit to, a single coherent narrative of how the client's possession originated and how, if at all, it turned hostile, complete with an approximate date or triggering event. A vague claim of "possession for the last thirty-five years" without a hostility narrative will not survive scrutiny after this ruling.

The judgment also has quiet relevance for suits challenging mutation entries and revenue records more broadly, an area that already came up in this blog's discussion of Jamnabai v. Vasudev. Read together, the two rulings reinforce a consistent Supreme Court position: a mutation or Jamabandi entry is administrative housekeeping, not a title document, and civil courts should not let revenue authorities' bookkeeping substitute for the evidentiary rigour that a declaration suit demands.

Statutory cross-references

This is a civil property dispute and does not attract the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita or Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita framework. The governing provisions remain Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (scope of second appeal, correctly invoked here since the lower courts had misapplied settled legal principles rather than merely arrived at a different reading of the same facts), and the relevant limitation provisions under the Limitation Act, 1963 governing suits for possession and declarations of title, read against the general law on adverse possession and religious/charitable dedications, which remains uncodified and continues to be developed through case law such as this judgment and the precedents it relies upon.
