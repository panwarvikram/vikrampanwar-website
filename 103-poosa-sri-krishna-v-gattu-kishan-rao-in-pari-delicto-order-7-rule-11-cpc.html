Poosa Sri Krishna & Ors. v. Gattu Kishan Rao & Anr.: Supreme Court Says a Plaint That Reveals Its Own Illegal Purpose Must Be Thrown Out at the Threshold

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePoosa Sri Krishna & Ors. v. Gattu Kishan Rao & Anr., Supreme Court of India
BenchJustice Ahsanuddin Amanullah & Justice Manmohan
DateReported 11 September 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (SC) 928
Key SectionsOrder VII Rule 11(d), CPC, 1908 · S. 23, Indian Contract Act, 1872

What the case was about

A money suit filed in a Telangana district court looked, on its face, like an ordinary recovery claim: the plaintiff alleged she had handed over a sum of money to the defendants to help arrange bank loans, supposedly to cover unspecified "overhead expenses," and wanted it back after the arrangement fell through. But the plaint's own averments told a more troubling story. Part of the money, it stated, was meant to satisfy demands made by bank officials in their personal capacity to sanction the loans in the first place. Separately, the plaint disclosed that demonetised currency notes had been collected and handed to the defendants for exchange — itself an object the law does not permit.

The defendants moved to have the plaint thrown out at the earliest possible stage under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, arguing that a court cannot even entertain a suit whose own pleadings admit an illegal and fraudulent purpose. Both the trial court and, on appeal, the Telangana High Court disagreed and allowed the suit to proceed to trial. The defendants then appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The Supreme Court reversed both courts below and ordered the plaint rejected. Reading the plaint on its own terms — which is all Order VII Rule 11 permits a court to do at this stage — the Bench of Justices Ahsanuddin Amanullah and Manmohan found two independent grounds on which the transaction was illegal from the outset: first, that a portion of the money was earmarked to gratify bank officials personally in exchange for sanctioning loans, and second, that demonetised notes had been procured and handed over for exchange as consideration under the same arrangement, which is itself impermissible. Either ground alone, the Court held, was enough to render the underlying Memorandum of Understanding void under Section 23 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, as an object forbidden by law, opposed to public policy, and fraudulent.

Having found the object void, the Court applied the doctrine of in pari delicto — the principle, as Black's Law Dictionary puts it, that "a plaintiff who has participated in wrongdoing may not recover damages resulting from the wrongdoing." Citing the American authority Bateman Eichler, Hill Richards, Inc. v. Berner, the Bench explained the doctrine rests on two ideas: courts should not referee disputes between wrongdoers, and refusing relief to a party who admits its own illegality is itself a deterrent against illegality. Drawing on its own precedents in G. Pankajakshi Amma v. Mathai Mathew and Vinod Popli v. Ragini Popli, the Court reaffirmed that where a transaction is illegal and unaccounted for, "the loss must be allowed to lie where it falls" — a court will not step in to redistribute the consequences of a bargain both sides knew was unlawful.

The plaintiff had tried to escape this result by invoking Sita Ram v. Radha Bai & Ors., which allows recovery of money paid under an illegal transaction where that transaction has not actually been carried into effect — the idea being that if nothing illegal actually happened, refusing recovery would only unjustly enrich the other side. The Supreme Court held this exception did not apply here. On the plaintiff's own case, she had already parted with the money, and the illegal object had been substantially carried out: the demonetised notes had in fact been collected and handed over for exchange. Because her side of the illegal bargain was complete, and only the defendants' side of procuring the loans had allegedly failed, this was not a case of an illegal scheme that fizzled out before anything illegal happened — it was one where the illegality had already been executed. The Sita Ram exception, confined to unexecuted illegal transactions, therefore gave her no shelter, and the suit was ordered rejected under Order VII Rule 11(d).

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Money-recovery suits arising out of informal arrangements — loan facilitation, "commission" payments to get a file moved, adjustments through demonetised currency, or payments to smooth some official process — are common in district court practice, and clients often assume that so long as they can prove the payment was made, a civil court will help them recover it if the other side doesn't deliver. This judgment is a sharp reminder that a court's assistance is not available to either side of a bargain that the plaint itself shows to be unlawful, however genuinely the money changed hands.

For advocates settling a plaint, the practical lesson is to scrutinise the client's own narrative before filing: language that inadvertently discloses payments meant to influence an official personally, or to achieve some prohibited object, can hand the defence a ready-made Order VII Rule 11 application, disposing of the suit before a single witness is examined. Conversely, defence counsel facing a recovery suit rooted in a shady arrangement should read the plaint closely for admissions of this kind — the illegality does not need to be proved through evidence at the interlocutory stage; if it appears from the plaint's own averments, that is sufficient to defeat the suit at the threshold.

The judgment also usefully clarifies the boundary of the Sita Ram exception, which litigants often invoke loosely to argue that "the deal fell through, so I should get my money back." That exception survives only where the illegal object was never actually executed. Once even part of the unlawful scheme has been carried out — as with the exchange of demonetised notes here — a plaintiff who parted with money as her share of that scheme cannot dress the claim up as an ordinary recovery suit.

Statutory cross-references

This is a civil procedure and contract-law ruling with no criminal-code provisions in play, so there is no BNS/BNSS/BSA correspondence to note. The operative provisions remain Order VII Rule 11(d) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, which has not been amended by the recent criminal-law recodification, and Section 23 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, governing agreements void for unlawful or fraudulent object.
