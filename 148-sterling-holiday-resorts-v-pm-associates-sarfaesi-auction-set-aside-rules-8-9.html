Sterling Holiday Resorts v. M/s P.M. Associates (2026 INSC 1071): Supreme Court Says a SARFAESI Sale Held in Breach of a Restraint Order and the 30-Day Notice Period Has “No Sanctity”

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Sterling Holiday Resorts Ltd. v. M/s P.M. Associates & Ors.
BenchJustice P.S. Narasimha and Justice Alok Aradhe (judgment by Justice Aradhe)
Date30 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1071
Key SectionsSARFAESI Act s. 13(8); Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002, Rules 8 & 9; Art. 300A

What the case was about

Sterling Holiday Resorts had mortgaged “The Fernhill,” its resort at Ooty, to secure loans from IFCI Limited and Tourism Finance Corporation of India. After default, IFCI invoked the SARFAESI Act, took symbolic possession and issued an auction notice dated 25 March 2010 with a ₹20 crore reserve price, fixing the auction for 28 April 2010.

On 7 April 2010 the Debts Recovery Appellate Tribunal (DRAT) restrained IFCI from proceeding further, on condition of a ₹1 crore deposit, which the borrower paid the next day. IFCI nevertheless received bids and earnest money during the restraint, though it did not open them. Many years later, once the restraint ended, it opened the old bids without telling the borrower, declared a bidder successful, and issued the sale certificate not to that bidder but to a partnership firm he had formed with another person — a firm that did not exist on the auction date.

The borrower later cleared IFCI’s entire dues. IFCI cancelled the sale certificate and refunded the price with interest, which the purchaser encashed. The Madras High Court nevertheless set the cancellation aside, upheld the certificate and ordered possession and registration in the purchaser’s favour. The borrower appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the Court held

A bench of Justices P.S. Narasimha and Alok Aradhe set aside the High Court judgment on 30 September 2026. The reasoning rests on one idea: statutory procedure is the source of an auction’s validity, and a confirmed sale cannot cure what was illegal at the start. As the Court put it, sanctity is the reward of legality, not a substitute for it.

First, Rules 8 and 9 of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002 are mandatory and demand strict compliance. The Court relied on its earlier rulings in Mardia Chemicals, Mathew Varghese and J. Rajiv Subramaniyan. The 30-day period under Rules 8(6) and 9(1) exists to give the borrower a real chance to redeem the property.

Second, that period stops running while a restraint order is in force. Thirteen of the 30 days had elapsed when the DRAT restrained IFCI, leaving 17 days. IFCI concluded the sale before those 17 days were over, cutting into the borrower’s right of redemption under Section 13(8) of the Act as it then stood.

Third, receiving bids during the restraint was itself a prohibited step. Leaving them unopened did not cure the illegality, and a later High Court ruling in IFCI’s favour could not retrospectively validate what was done while the restraint operated.

Fourth, the revival of the process after a long gap, without notice to the borrower, defeated the purpose of the safeguards. Neither the Rules nor the auction notice allowed a successful bidder to nominate someone else, and the entity that received the certificate had neither bid nor existed on the auction date. IFCI also described the transaction as a private treaty before the High Court and as a public tender elsewhere, and did not produce the original records or details of other bidders despite directions.

The Court invoked Article 300A: property can be taken away only by authority of law, and a sale that disregards statutory procedure does not meet that standard. The purchaser’s cross-appeals were dismissed, the contempt petitions were not pursued, and the purchaser’s challenge to the Thomas Cook amalgamation scheme fell away with its auction claim. The Court left open whether an Authorised Officer can cancel a sale certificate, whether the certificate needs registration, and when redemption rights are extinguished.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Borrowers and auction purchasers in our district courts and DRT-related litigation often assume that once an auction is confirmed and a certificate issued, the matter is closed. This ruling says otherwise. If you act for a borrower, check four things in the record: whether the 30-day notice ran in full, whether any stay or restraint interrupted the process, whether the bidder who paid is the bidder who was declared successful, and whether the borrower was heard before a stalled auction was revived.

If you act for a purchaser, the lesson is the reverse: diligence on the bank’s compliance file before bidding protects your money better than the sale certificate afterwards. Bids received under a restraint, nominations not permitted by the notice, and long-dormant auctions revived silently are all red flags that can unwind a sale years later, even after possession is claimed. For matters that reach the High Court under Article 226 or a civil court challenge, plead the specific Rule breached and the exact number of days that remained; the Court did its arithmetic, and so should the pleading.

Statutory cross-references

This is a civil and banking-recovery matter, so no IPC/CrPC to BNS/BNSS mapping arises. The relevant provisions are Section 13(8) of the SARFAESI Act, 2002 (right of redemption, as it then stood), Rules 8 and 9 of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002 (notice of sale and the 30-day period) and Article 300A of the Constitution (right to property).
