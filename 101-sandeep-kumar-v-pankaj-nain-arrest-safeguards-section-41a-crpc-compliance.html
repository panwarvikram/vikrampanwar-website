Sandeep Kumar v. Pankaj Nain, IPS & Ors.: P&H High Court Orders Punjab, Haryana & Chandigarh to Explain How They Comply With Arrest-Notice Safeguards Under Section 41-A CrPC

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court, Chandigarh
BenchJustice Sudeepti Sharma
Date11 September 2026 (matter adjourned to 16 September 2026)
Case TitleSandeep Kumar v. Pankaj Nain, IPS and others (contempt petitions, batch)
Key SectionsSs. 41 & 41-A, CrPC, 1973 (S. 35, BNSS, 2023) · Satender Kumar Antil v. CBI, 2022 AIR SC 3386

What the case was about

This order arises out of a batch of contempt of court petitions before the Punjab and Haryana High Court concerning alleged non-compliance with the safeguards against illegal or unnecessary arrest that the Supreme Court laid down in Satender Kumar Antil v. Central Bureau of Investigation, 2022 AIR SC 3386. That 2022 judgment remains one of the most cited in bail and arrest jurisprudence, and among its directions was a requirement that every State and Union Territory put in place standing orders governing how police officers use Sections 41 and 41-A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, before arresting a person in a case where arrest is not mandatory.

More than four years after that judgment, the High Court, hearing the pending contempt petitions with Senior Advocate Ankur Mittal assisting as Amicus Curiae, found the position on the ground still unclear. The Amicus drew the Court's attention to a 2018 Delhi High Court Division Bench ruling, Amandeep Singh Johar v. State of NCT of Delhi, which had itself flagged how poorly the Section 41-A notice machinery was functioning and had laid down a detailed protocol in response — a standard notice format, a serially numbered duplicate booklet for investigating officers to issue and retain proof of service, mandatory rescheduling safeguards for an accused who genuinely cannot appear on the date given, and contempt exposure for investigating officers who bypass the process, drawing on the Supreme Court's separate ruling in Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar. Delhi Police had translated that protocol into Standing Order No. 109 of 2020, and the Supreme Court in Satender Kumar Antil expressly endorsed both the Delhi High Court's framework and that standing order as a model, observing that such a system would "certainly take care of unwarranted arrests."

What the court held

Justice Sudeepti Sharma held that the Supreme Court's mandate in Paragraph 73(d) of Satender Kumar Antil was "unambiguously clear," and that it was now time to test actual compliance rather than assume it. The Court directed the Directors General of Police and the Chief Secretaries of Punjab, Haryana, and the Union Territory of Chandigarh to file specific affidavits explaining exactly how the Antil directions on Section 41/41-A standing orders have been complied with "in its true letter and spirit" in their respective jurisdictions. Each affidavit must attach the actual proforma currently used by police officers in that jurisdiction for issuing a Section 41-A notice, along with the acknowledgment format used to record that the notice was served and understood.

The Court fixed a firm procedural discipline around this: the affidavits are to be filed at least two days before the next hearing, with advance copies furnished to the Amicus Curiae, failing which the order records that "appropriate order shall follow." Copies of the order itself were directed to be transmitted to the respective DGPs and Chief Secretaries through the counsel appearing for the three governments, to ensure the direction reaches the right desks and is not lost in transmission. With arguments heard only in part, the matter now stands adjourned to 16 September 2026.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Section 41-A notices are a daily reality in district court criminal practice — they are frequently the first formal contact a person under investigation has with the police in a case where arrest is not automatic, and how faithfully that notice procedure is followed often determines whether an anticipatory bail application even becomes necessary. Advocates should treat this order as a reminder to actually demand and inspect the notice, the acknowledgment, and the reasons recorded when police do proceed to arrest despite the Antil safeguards, since a departure from a properly notified standing order can itself become a ground for relief, including under Arnesh Kumar-style contempt exposure for the investigating officer.

Once the affidavits from Punjab, Haryana, and Chandigarh are actually filed, Haryana's own standing order or proforma, if one exists, will become a matter of public record through this litigation — something worth tracking for anyone regularly filing anticipatory bail applications or challenging arrests in the district courts of Haryana. Until then, practitioners can still rely on the Antil directions themselves, which apply irrespective of whether a state has finished formalising its own standing order, as binding law on the Section 41/41-A procedure police are required to follow.

Statutory cross-references

Section 41 of the CrPC, 1973 (grounds and manner of arrest without warrant) now corresponds to Section 35 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, and the notice-of-appearance safeguard formerly under Section 41-A CrPC is incorporated into Section 35(3) BNSS, which requires a notice directing appearance, rather than arrest, in appropriate cases where the maximum punishment does not exceed seven years, subject to the exceptions recognised in that provision.
