Madan Mohan v. Thakar Dass: Supreme Court Says a Storage Shop "Lying Vacant" Doesn't Defeat a Landlord's Bona Fide Requirement, and Reins In Section 115 CPC Revisions

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseMadan Mohan (Dead) Through LRs v. Thakar Dass (Dead), Supreme Court of India (on appeal from Punjab & Haryana High Court)
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & Augustine George Masih
Date22 July 2026 (reported 23 July 2026)
Citation2026 INSC 730
Key SectionsS. 13, East Punjab Urban Rent Restriction Act, 1949 · S. 115, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908

What the case was about

Kamla Devi filed an eviction petition against her tenant in Nabha, Punjab, on two grounds: non-payment of rent, and bona fide requirement of the tenanted shop for herself and her son, Madan Mohan, who wanted to run a boutique. Both landlady and son have since died, and the appeal was carried forward by their legal heirs. Two courts below — the Rent Controller, Nabha, and the Appellate Authority, Patiala — found the eviction case fully proved and ordered the tenant out.

The tenant then took the matter in revision to the Punjab & Haryana High Court under Section 15(5) of the East Punjab Urban Rent Restriction Act, which is exercised on the same limited footing as Section 115 CPC. The High Court reversed both concurrent findings. Its reasoning: Kamla Devi had not come to court with clean hands, because she had denied — and only admitted in her evidence — that a third shop she owned was lying vacant, being used, on her own case, to store garbage. If an alternate shop was available, the High Court reasoned, the claim of genuine need collapsed. The landlady's heirs appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The Bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and Augustine George Masih restored the eviction order, and in doing so, laid down two distinct propositions that matter well beyond this one shop in Nabha.

First, on what "bona fide requirement" actually means: the Court explained that it translates to "in good faith," and while a landlord does have the right to enjoy his own property, that right is not a licence for arbitrary eviction — the requirement must genuinely exist and must be assessed on the facts, "not something that can be determined by a mathematical formula." Here, both Kamla Devi and Madan Mohan had pleaded the requirement from the very start, and two courts below had concurrently accepted it. The Supreme Court found nothing in the record to justify departing from those concurrent findings.

Second, and more sharply, on the "vacant shop" reasoning that had persuaded the High Court: the Bench pointed out that the third shop, by the landlady's own admission, was being used to store waste material. In a line that will likely be quoted often, the Court observed — "one man's trash is another man's treasure" — and held that even taking her evidence exactly as given, there was nothing wrong with continuing to use a garbage-storage space as a garbage-storage space while reclaiming the tenanted shop to run a boutique. A shop that is not fit, or not being used, for the very commercial purpose the landlord wants to pursue does not amount to an available "alternate accommodation" that defeats bona fide requirement.

Underlying both findings was the Court's reminder about the narrow scope of revisional jurisdiction. Section 115 CPC (and its equivalent under rent legislation) does not permit a High Court, sitting in revision, to reappreciate evidence and substitute its own view for concurrent findings of fact reached by two courts below, unless there is a jurisdictional error or a perversity in those findings. The Bench found no such infirmity here, set aside the High Court's judgment, and directed the tenant to hand over vacant and peaceful possession of the shop within two months.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Eviction litigation on the ground of bona fide requirement is a staple of civil practice in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, where small shopkeepers, tenant families, and landlords locked in decades-old tenancies routinely litigate up through the Rent Controller, the Appellate Authority, and then in revision before the High Court. Two practical lessons stand out from this ruling.

The first is for landlords and their counsel: a landlord who owns other property need not fear that mere ownership of another vacant space will automatically sink a bona fide requirement claim. What matters is whether that other space is genuinely usable for the purpose the landlord actually needs — a godown used for storage, a shop in disrepair, or premises unsuitable for the specific business in question does not count as a real alternative. This gives landlords a sturdier footing to resist the common tenant defence of "you already have another shop."

The second lesson is procedural, and arguably more important for appellate strategy: this judgment is a reminder that Section 115 CPC revisions (and the parallel revisional provisions under state rent Acts) are not a second appeal in disguise. Where the Rent Controller and Appellate Authority have concurrently found the facts a certain way, a party seeking to overturn that finding at the High Court revision stage faces a genuinely high bar. Advocates drafting revision petitions, and those defending eviction decrees at that stage, should calibrate expectations and arguments accordingly — and advocates for landlords, in particular, can now cite this ruling directly against attempts to relitigate settled factual findings through revisional jurisdiction.

Statutory cross-references

Section 115 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, and Section 13 read with Section 15(5) of the East Punjab Urban Rent Restriction Act, 1949, are both untouched by the 2023 criminal law reforms (BNS, BNSS, and BSA), since they are civil and state rent-control provisions rather than criminal procedure. They continue to apply in their existing form to eviction and revision proceedings across Punjab and Haryana, including before the Rent Controllers and Appellate Authorities at Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani.
