Inderjit Kaur v. State of Haryana: P&H High Court Says Taking Children Away by Deception Doesn't, By Itself, Make Out Cheating or Criminal Breach of Trust

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseInderjit Kaur v. State of Haryana & Anr.; Gurjit Singh & Ors. v. State of Haryana & Anr. (connected petitions), Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Manisha Batra
Date7 September 2026
CitationReported by LiveLaw & ApniLaw, 7 September 2026
Key SectionsSs. 406, 420, 120-B & 506, IPC, 1860 · S. 482, CrPC, 1973

What the case was about

The dispute began in November 2016, when Sita Rani lodged an FIR against her husband Gurjit Singh — an NRI settled in Italy — along with her mother-in-law Harjinder Kaur, sister-in-law Inderjeet Kaur, and Inderjeet Kaur's husband Jarnail Singh. Her allegation was straightforward on its face: her mother-in-law had taken her two children away in a car on the pretext of attending a Paath (a religious ceremony) at Inderjeet Kaur's house. When she tried to reach the family the next day, she says Inderjeet Kaur denied knowing where the children were, and the phones of both the mother-in-law and the children went unreachable. Her case was that the family had conspired to spirit the children out of the country to Italy without her consent, and that Inderjeet Kaur had, on top of it, threatened to kill her.

The FIR started life under Sections 365 (kidnapping) and 506 (criminal intimidation) of the IPC. As the investigation proceeded, the police added Sections 406 (criminal breach of trust), 420 (cheating) and 120-B (criminal conspiracy). Section 365 was eventually dropped, and one co-accused, Pardeep Kumar, was found to have no role at all. A charge-sheet followed, but charges under Sections 420 and 506 IPC were framed only against Inderjeet Kaur, in July 2017 — Gurjit Singh, Harjinder Kaur and Jarnail Singh, all in Italy, were never arrested or charge-sheeted, with the State keeping open the option of a supplementary charge-sheet once they were.

Both sets of accused approached the High Court under Section 482 CrPC to quash the FIR and everything that flowed from it. Their argument wasn't that the facts alleged were false — it was that even taking the FIR entirely at face value, none of the offences invoked were actually made out. The foundational allegation of kidnapping had already been dropped. What remained, they argued, was a custody dispute dressed up in the language of a criminal complaint.

What the court held

Justice Manisha Batra worked through each offence on its own statutory anatomy, rather than treating the FIR's characterisation of events as conclusive.

On Section 406 IPC, criminal breach of trust requires, as a starting point, that property be entrusted to the accused and then dishonestly misappropriated or converted. The Court found no allegation anywhere in the FIR that any property had been entrusted to the petitioners. Custody of children, the Court held, "however serious the dispute may be, cannot by itself constitute entrustment of property so as to attract Section 406 IPC." Children are not property in the sense the section requires, and a custody grievance cannot be repackaged as a breach-of-trust case merely by calling it one.

On Section 420 IPC, cheating requires a dishonest or fraudulent inducement that results in the delivery of property or a valuable security. The Court was prepared to assume, purely for the sake of argument, that Inderjeet Kaur had indeed induced the complainant to send her children over under the pretext of the religious ceremony. Even so, what was obtained through that deception was, at most, temporary custody of the children — not delivery of property within the meaning of Section 420. Justice Batra was direct about the drafting habit that often inflates such FIRs: "the mere use of the words 'cheated' or 'dishonestly induced' in the allegations cannot make out an offence under Section 420 IPC when the necessary ingredients of that provision are otherwise absent." Notably, the Court held that this reasoning applied even though a charge under Section 420 had already been framed against Inderjeet Kaur — framing of charge doesn't immunise a case from Section 482 scrutiny where the offence's basic ingredients are missing on a plain reading of the complaint.

On Section 120-B IPC (criminal conspiracy), the Court found the conspiracy allegation was "essentially a conclusion drawn by the complainant" from the bare fact that the children left with their grandmother and weren't returned. There was no material pointing to any actual meeting of minds between the accused. And since the substantive offences the conspiracy was meant to serve weren't themselves made out, the conspiracy charge had nothing left to attach to.

On Section 506 IPC, the death threat allegation against Inderjeet Kaur was found to be "completely general in nature" — no date, time, occasion, or actual words attributed to her. General, unparticularised allegations of this kind don't meet the threshold for criminal intimidation.

The Court expressly declined to resolve the underlying factual dispute over whether the complainant's affidavit consenting to the children's relocation to Italy was voluntary or not — that question, it said, didn't need answering, because even setting it aside entirely, the FIR's own allegations failed to disclose the ingredients of any of the offences charged. Allowing both connected petitions, the Court quashed FIR No. 279 dated 25 November 2016 along with the charge-sheet and framed charges against all the petitioners, holding that continuing the proceedings would amount to an abuse of the process of law, since the underlying dispute was, at bottom, a custody dispute and nothing more.

Why this matters for practitioners in Haryana

This judgment is a useful reminder of a recurring pattern in matrimonial fallout cases across Haryana's district courts: a genuine custody or access grievance gets filed as a criminal complaint, often loaded with Sections 406, 420 and 506 IPC (now Sections 316, 318 and 351 BNS) because criminal proceedings are seen as carrying more leverage than a slow-moving guardianship petition. Defence counsel handling quashing petitions under Section 482 CrPC (now Section 528 BNSS) should take from this ruling a clean, reusable template: test each invoked section against its statutory ingredients independently, rather than arguing generally that the FIR is "false" or "motivated." Here, the fatal gap for Section 406 was the complete absence of any entrustment of property; for Section 420, the absence of any delivery of property beyond mere custody; for Section 120-B, the absence of any specific material showing agreement; and for Section 506, the absence of particulars.

For complainants and their counsel, the lesson cuts the other way: a custody dispute, however painful, belongs first in a guardianship or custody proceeding under the Guardians and Wards Act or the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, or in a habeas corpus petition if the child's whereabouts are genuinely unknown. Reaching first for the criminal law risks exactly this outcome — a quashing that ends the case at the threshold, with the real custody question never decided on its merits at all.

Statutory cross-references

Section 406 IPC (criminal breach of trust) now corresponds to Section 316, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. Section 420 IPC (cheating) corresponds to Section 318, BNS. Section 120-B IPC (criminal conspiracy) corresponds to Section 61, BNS. Section 506 IPC (criminal intimidation) corresponds to Section 351, BNS. Section 482 CrPC, under which the quashing was sought, corresponds to Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. The FIR in this case predates the new codes and was rightly examined under the IPC and CrPC as they stood in 2016, but the underlying analysis of what each offence requires carries over unchanged to its BNS/BNSS equivalent.
