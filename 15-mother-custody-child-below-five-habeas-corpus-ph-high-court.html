"Grave Cruelty": P&H High Court Orders Return of Child to Mother, Rejects In-Laws' Plea That a Pregnant Woman Can't Parent Her Firstborn

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court, Habeas Corpus petition by mother for custody of minor son
BenchJustice Jasjit Singh Bedi
Date14 July 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 232
Key SectionsArt. 226, Constitution of India (Habeas Corpus); Guardians and Wards Act, 1890; Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956

What the case was about

A mother approached the Punjab & Haryana High Court through a writ of habeas corpus, seeking the return of her three-year-old son from her in-laws. The child had been kept back by the paternal grandparents, and the mother's case was that there was no lawful basis for them to retain custody against her wishes as the natural, and in this instance the sole caregiving, parent.

The in-laws resisted the petition on an unusual ground: they argued that the mother, being pregnant with her second child, was not in a position to look after her three-year-old firstborn, and that the child was therefore better looked after in their household. This is a fact pattern that comes up more often than one would expect in custody litigation in Haryana and Punjab — a mother's pregnancy, ill-health, remarriage, or simply her in-laws' preference is used to argue that custody should shift away from her, often without any real finding of unfitness.

What the court held

Justice Jasjit Singh Bedi allowed the habeas corpus petition and directed that custody of the three-year-old be restored to the mother. The Court was unwilling to accept the in-laws' argument that a pregnant woman is, by that fact alone, incapable of caring for an older child. As reported, the Bench found nothing on record to suggest the mother was an unsuitable or unfit custodian, and characterised the attempt to separate a pregnant mother from her firstborn on that basis as amounting to grave cruelty toward her.

The ruling reaffirms a settled and important principle of Indian family law: under Section 6 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, the custody of a child below the age of five years ordinarily rests with the mother. This is not treated as an absolute or mechanical rule, but the threshold to displace it is high — the person seeking to deny the mother custody must affirmatively show that she is wholly unsuitable to have the child, whether on grounds of neglect, incapacity, or conduct harmful to the child's welfare. A future pregnancy, without more, does not come close to meeting that threshold. If anything, as the Court's reasoning suggests, using a woman's pregnancy against her to strip her of custody of an existing child inverts the very protective purpose the "below five" rule was designed to serve.

Habeas corpus, in the child custody context, is not meant to conduct a full inquiry into welfare akin to guardianship proceedings, but it remains the fastest available remedy where a child is being held by someone without legal authority to do so, and where the delay of a full guardianship trial would itself cause harm. This case illustrates that use: the High Court moved directly to restore the child rather than relegating the mother to a fresh suit under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, given the absence of any credible basis to keep the child away from her.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

In our courts, custody disputes between a mother and her in-laws — as distinct from disputes between the two parents — are a recurring feature, particularly where the father is absent, working outside the state, or where the marriage has broken down and the child was living in the matrimonial home with grandparents at the time of separation. This ruling gives practitioners a clean, recent, and squarely on-point authority to resist arguments that a mother's pregnancy, remarriage prospects, or general life circumstances (short of demonstrated unfitness) justify denying her custody of a child under five.

It is also a useful precedent on drafting habeas corpus petitions in custody matters generally. Advocates should lead with the age of the child and the absence of any specific, evidence-backed unsuitability finding against the mother, since this is precisely the framework the High Court applied here. Conversely, respondents defending such a petition need more than a generalised apprehension about the mother's circumstances — they need concrete material showing risk to the child, not speculation about a parent's future capacity dressed up as present unfitness.

For advocates practising in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, where joint family living arrangements are common and disputes over a grandchild's custody following marital discord are frequent, this order is a timely reminder that the "below five" presumption in the mother's favour carries real weight against sentiment-driven or convenience-driven claims by in-laws, however well-meaning those claims may be framed to appear.

Statutory cross-references

This matter arises under civil and personal law provisions untouched by the 2023 criminal law re-codification, so there is no BNS/BNSS mapping applicable here. The relevant framework remains Section 6 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 (natural guardian of a Hindu minor, and the special rule for custody of children below five), read with the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 for full welfare-based guardianship proceedings, and Article 226 of the Constitution for the extraordinary writ jurisdiction invoked here.
