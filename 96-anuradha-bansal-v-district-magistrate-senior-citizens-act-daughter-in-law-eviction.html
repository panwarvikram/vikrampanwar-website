Anuradha Bansal v. District Magistrate: P&H High Court Says the Senior Citizens Act Can't Be Used to Evict an Estranged Daughter-in-Law

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseAnuradha Bansal v. District Magistrate and others
BenchJustices Suvir Sehgal & Rajesh Gaur, Punjab & Haryana High Court
Date8 September 2026
CitationLPA-2464-2026 · Reported by LiveLaw & The Tribune, 8 September 2026
Key SectionsMaintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 · S. 17, Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 · S. Vanitha v. Deputy Commissioner, Bengaluru Urban District, (2021) 15 SCC 730

What the case was about

A mother-in-law who owned a residential property went to the Maintenance Tribunal constituted under the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007, seeking to evict her daughter-in-law from the house. Her case was that after her husband's death, her two sons had lived in the property while paying her rent, a fact she said was reflected in their income tax returns. When her younger son moved out after allegedly stopping the rent payments, she claimed his wife stayed on and began harassing her, and asked the Tribunal to remove the daughter-in-law under the summary eviction machinery the 2007 Act provides for senior citizens.

The daughter-in-law's version, which found favour before the Single Judge when the mother-in-law's writ petition against the Tribunal's refusal came up, was rather different. The Single Judge found that the Senior Citizens Act proceedings had, in substance, been engineered at the instance of the appellant's own son, who was fighting separate and independent proceedings brought against him by his estranged wife under the very same Act. In other words, a statute meant to protect ageing parents from neglect by their children was, on this finding, being used as a tactical weapon in an ongoing matrimonial dispute between husband and wife. The writ petition was dismissed, and the mother-in-law carried the matter further in a Letters Patent Appeal before the Division Bench.

What the court held

The Division Bench of Justices Suvir Sehgal and Rajesh Gaur found no infirmity in the Single Judge's reasoning and dismissed the appeal. The Court's central holding was that the 2007 Act exists to enforce the obligation of children and relatives to maintain senior citizens who cannot support themselves, not to resolve matrimonial or property disputes dressed up as maintenance complaints. As the Bench put it, the statute "cannot be pressed into service for the adjudication of matrimonial or property disputes," and it certainly cannot be used to evict an estranged daughter-in-law who otherwise possesses an independent right to reside in the shared household — a right the Supreme Court had already recognised in S. Vanitha v. Deputy Commissioner, Bengaluru Urban District, (2021) 15 SCC 730.

Two evidentiary threads reinforced that conclusion. First, the appellant's claim that her son had stopped paying rent had never actually been raised before the Maintenance Tribunal, nor was it substantiated before the writ court — it surfaced only as an afterthought, consistent with the finding that the "welfare" complaint was really a proxy for the son's matrimonial fight. Second, and perhaps more tellingly, the property had devolved in equal shares on the appellant and both her sons after her husband's death, yet she chose to proceed only against her younger son and his estranged wife, taking no action whatsoever against her elder son. The Court treated this selective targeting as a strong indicator that the object of the litigation was to help one son prevail over his wife, not to secure the appellant's own maintenance or residential comfort. The Bench went so far as to observe that the appeal deserved to be dismissed with heavy costs, but stayed its hand only in view of the appellant's advanced age.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

This judgment addresses a pattern that shows up with some regularity in family law practice across Haryana's district courts: in-laws invoking the Senior Citizens Act's comparatively quick, tribunal-based eviction mechanism to sidestep the slower, more protective machinery of the Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and thereby oust a daughter-in-law from what is, in substance, her matrimonial home. Practitioners representing women in such situations now have a clear, recent P&H High Court statement, resting squarely on S. Vanitha, that the 2007 Act's summary eviction route cannot be used to defeat a residence right that exists independently of any title or ownership interest in the property.

The judgment is equally useful on the other side of the aisle, for family members who genuinely need to invoke the Senior Citizens Act. It signals that Maintenance Tribunals and reviewing courts will scrutinise the underlying purpose of an eviction application closely, and that inconsistencies such as selectively proceeding against one son's household while ignoring an equally entitled co-owner, or raising a ground for the first time only in appeal, will be read as evidence of a collusive or proxy filing. Advocates drafting Senior Citizens Act complaints should therefore ensure the pleaded facts are consistent from the first filing before the Tribunal onward, and should be candid with clients about the risk that a complaint perceived as matrimonial gamesmanship is likely to fail regardless of the senior citizen's genuine underlying grievances.

For advocates representing daughters-in-law, the practical takeaway is to plead the shared household right affirmatively and early, ideally through a parallel Section 17 application, rather than waiting to raise it only after a Senior Citizens Act eviction order has already been passed.

Statutory cross-references

This is a civil and family-law dispute rather than a criminal one, so no IPC or BNS mapping arises on the facts of this case; matrimonial cruelty or harassment allegations arising from the same family dispute would separately fall under Section 85, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (the successor to Section 498-A, IPC), but that provision was not in play here. The two enactments that do matter are the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007, which creates the Maintenance Tribunal and its summary eviction powers, and Section 17 of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, which secures a woman's right to reside in a shared household regardless of any title, right or interest she holds in it. Reading the two together, as the Division Bench did, prevents the 2007 Act's welfare-oriented machinery from being converted into a fast-track eviction tool against women who are independently protected under the 2005 Act.
