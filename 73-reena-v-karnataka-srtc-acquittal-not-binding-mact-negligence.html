Reena v. Managing Director, KSRTC: Supreme Court Says a Bus Driver's Criminal Acquittal Cannot Dictate the MACT's Negligence Finding

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtSupreme Court of India
BenchJustices S.V.N. Bhatti & N.V. Anjaria
DateJudgment dated 19 August 2026; reported 20–21 August 2026
Citation2026 INSC 889
Key SectionsSs. 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 · S. 304-A, IPC, 1860

What the case was about

In the early hours of 14 October 2005, a Hyundai Santro travelling on National Highway 48 near Kunigal, Karnataka, collided with a bus belonging to the Karnataka State Road Transport Corporation. Three people in the car — the driver's wife, his mother-in-law and a family friend — died at the spot or shortly after; the appellant, who was newly married and travelling with them, survived with injuries. She went on to file three separate claim petitions before the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal seeking compensation for the three deaths and for her own injuries.

The Tribunal examined the First Information Report, the chargesheet and, crucially, the eyewitness testimony of the bus conductor, and concluded that the KSRTC driver was 100% negligent. It awarded compensation on that basis, though the appellant felt the quantum was inadequate given her husband's actual earnings. On appeal, the Karnataka High Court enhanced the amount for the husband's death but simultaneously clawed back a chunk of it by attributing 50% contributory negligence to the deceased husband himself. It did so relying on the resting position of the vehicles shown in a post-accident topographical sketch, and by treating the bus driver's acquittal in the parallel criminal trial as an "honourable" one.

The appellant, arguing her own case in person before the Supreme Court, challenged both the negligence apportionment and the inadequate income assessment, and separately sought permission to place fresh documentary evidence about her husband's earnings on record — evidence she had not been able to gather in time for the original proceedings.

What the court held

The Bench of Justices S.V.N. Bhatti and N.V. Anjaria set aside the High Court's finding of contributory negligence and restored the Tribunal's conclusion that the bus driver bore full responsibility for the collision. The reasoning rests on a point that comes up constantly in MACT practice but is still frequently got wrong at the appellate stage: a criminal trial and a civil compensation claim are not run on the same evidentiary track, and an outcome in one cannot be imported wholesale into the other.

Relying on its earlier decision in Mathew Alexander v. Mohd. Shafi, (2023) 13 SCC 510, the Court restated the position that "culpable rashness" under Section 304-A IPC demands a considerably higher degree of proof than the ordinary law of negligence in tort that governs a MACT claim. An accused who walks free from a criminal court on the benefit of the doubt has not been found innocent in any affirmative sense — the prosecution has simply failed to establish guilt beyond reasonable doubt. That is a different thing entirely from an "honourable acquittal," and the High Court erred in treating the two as interchangeable. A benefit-of-doubt acquittal, the Court held, cannot be allowed to displace the prima facie evidentiary value that an FIR and a chargesheet already carry in the civil proceeding.

The judgment goes further and lays out, almost as a checklist for tribunals and appellate courts, the propositions that now govern this overlap: a chargesheet creates a prima facie case of negligence; a criminal acquittal does not automatically displace that prima facie civil liability; post-impact resting positions or drag marks on a scene sketch cannot, without more, be used to infer contributory negligence against a smaller vehicle that has clearly come off worse in a collision with a heavier one; and a High Court cannot summarily overturn a Tribunal's factual findings on negligence without actually meeting the civil standard of preponderance of probability. On facts, the Court noted that the High Court had preferred an inference from a sketch over the conductor's largely unchallenged eyewitness account, and had done so without the KSRTC even bothering to examine that same conductor to contest his version.

On the separate question of additional evidence, the Court allowed the appellant to place fresh income records on file at the appellate stage, holding that documentary evidence going to a deceased's earning capacity may be admitted late where it serves the object of awarding "just compensation" under the Motor Vehicles Act, particularly where the delay is explained by circumstances such as the sudden and recent nature of the marriage, the shock of the accident, and the appellant's own resulting unemployment. On this material, the Court revised the deceased's assessed monthly income upward from ₹20,000 to ₹70,000, reflecting his software qualifications and entrepreneurial invoices that had not been placed before the Tribunal. The appeals concerning the husband's death were allowed, full liability was restored on KSRTC, and enhanced compensation of ₹50,81,876 was awarded with 6% interest from the date of filing; compensation for the other claims was confirmed, with the entire liability directed to be borne exclusively by KSRTC.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

This is not an exotic ruling confined to Karnataka's highways — it addresses a fact pattern that recurs in nearly every Haryana MACT file where the offending vehicle's driver has also faced, or is facing, a parallel prosecution under Section 106 BNS (the successor to Section 304-A IPC) or under the old provision itself for accidents from before 1 July 2024. Insurers and owners routinely try to lean on a driver's criminal acquittal to argue there was no negligence at all, or to shift blame onto the deceased or the claimant. This judgment gives claimants' counsel a clean, Supreme-Court-endorsed answer: point to the chargesheet's prima facie value, insist that the criminal standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt has nothing to do with the civil standard of preponderance of probability, and resist any attempt to dress up a benefit-of-doubt acquittal as an honourable one.

It is equally useful on the evidentiary side. Tribunals in Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri routinely have to weigh scene sketches, site plans and eyewitness accounts against each other, especially in two-vehicle collisions involving a heavy commercial vehicle and a lighter one. The Court's specific caution against inferring contributory negligence purely from where vehicles ended up after impact — as opposed to where the actual point of contact occurred — is a useful precedent to cite whenever an insurer's counsel tries to build a contributory-negligence argument out of a post-accident photograph or a rough diagram rather than any real reconstruction evidence.

The ruling on belated income evidence also deserves attention. Claimants, particularly widows and young dependents thrown into sudden financial distress, often cannot marshal complete income documentation at the first opportunity. This judgment confirms that appellate courts retain the discretion to admit such evidence later in the interest of "just compensation," provided the delay is genuinely explained — a principle worth invoking whenever a claim has been under-assessed for want of income proof that surfaces only later.

Statutory cross-references

Section 304-A of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (causing death by a rash or negligent act not amounting to culpable homicide) — the provision under which the KSRTC driver was tried and acquitted — now corresponds to Section 106 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, for offences occurring on or after 1 July 2024. Sections 166 and 168 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, governing the filing of claim petitions and the Tribunal's power to award compensation, are unaffected by the BNS/BNSS transition and continue to apply as before.
