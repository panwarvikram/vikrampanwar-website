Sheetal Rani v. State of Haryana: P&H High Court Quashes Blanket Foreign Travel Ban on Government Employees as "Manifestly Arbitrary"

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSheetal Rani v. State of Haryana & Ors.
BenchJustice Harpreet Singh Brar
DateOrder reported 30 August 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 302
Key SectionsArts. 14 & 21, Constitution of India

What the case was about

A nursing officer posted at PGIMS Rohtak had qualified for an Objective Structured Clinical Examination in Australia, the kind of professional upgrade that could shape the rest of her career. Under an ordinary set of service rules, taking earned or study leave to sit such an exam abroad would have been a formality. But Haryana had, by an instruction dated 10 June 2026, barred every government employee, and every employee of state boards, corporations and local bodies, from travelling abroad for any purpose, official or personal, until September 2026. The only carve-out was for medical treatment.

The State defended the instruction as an austerity measure. Its counsel told the High Court that the Russia-Ukraine conflict and the West Asian crisis had strained global fuel supplies and supply chains, and that a temporary travel freeze was a reasonable, public-interest response to that pressure. On that logic, a nurse trying to clear a clinical exam in Melbourne was collateral damage in a resource-conservation drive.

The petitioner's case was narrower and more direct: the ban made no distinction between her situation and anyone else's, gave the competent authority no discretion to weigh individual circumstances, and effectively converted a fundamental liberty into something the government could switch off for an entire workforce with a single circular.

What the court held

Justice Harpreet Singh Brar framed the constitutional question at the outset: whether a blanket prohibition on foreign travel by government employees, issued purely as an executive instruction, could stand up to scrutiny under Articles 14 and 21. The starting point was settled law that the right to travel abroad is not a discretionary perk of government service but part of the right to personal liberty itself, so any restriction on it has to clear the fairness, reasonableness, non-arbitrariness and proportionality test that Article 21 demands, not merely the lower bar applicable to ordinary administrative circulars.

Measured against that standard, the instruction failed on its own terms. The Court noted there was no attempt in the instruction to distinguish between an employee travelling for leisure and one travelling to complete a professional qualification, no scope to weigh designation, nature of duties or purpose of travel, and no explanation of how keeping a nursing officer out of an examination hall in Australia would conserve fuel or ease supply-chain pressure back home. "No plausible explanation is forthcoming on behalf of the respondents," the Court observed, before concluding that applying an across-the-board ban to solve a narrowly defined resource problem was, in its words, "a sledgehammer being used to crack a nut" — impermissible under India's constitutional scheme.

The Court also rejected the implicit premise that being in government service diminishes an employee's constitutional entitlements. A prohibition that would be plainly unconstitutional if imposed on a private citizen does not become valid merely because the state happens to be the employer as well as the rule-maker. Since the instruction derived no authority from any statute and existed purely as executive fiat, it could not override the higher constitutional threshold that governs restrictions on personal liberty.

The writ petition was accordingly disposed of by setting aside the instruction to the extent it imposed a complete ban on foreign travel, with a direction to the competent authority to grant the petitioner permission to travel, and a corresponding direction to her to rejoin duty once her sanctioned leave expired.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Service law disputes rarely turn on dramatic facts, but this one is a useful template for a recurring problem in government practice: departments routinely issue blanket instructions — travel bans, transfer freezes, leave restrictions — dressed up as administrative housekeeping, without pausing to ask whether a rule aimed at one narrow objective needs to sweep in every employee regardless of circumstance. For counsel appearing before the Haryana government's various departments, boards and corporations, this ruling is a ready-made precedent to challenge any instruction that applies a uniform restriction to a diverse class of employees without a mechanism for individual consideration.

It is also a reminder that executive instructions, however well-intentioned, sit lower in the constitutional hierarchy than the rights they touch. An instruction that would need legislative backing to survive scrutiny cannot borrow legitimacy merely because it is framed as a temporary or emergency measure. Practitioners drafting representations against transfer orders, deputation bars, or similar administrative restraints in Haryana government service can draw directly on the proportionality framework this judgment applies: is there a rational nexus between the stated objective and the restriction, and does the restriction leave any room for the authority to consider the individual's circumstances before applying it. Where either answer is no, the instruction is vulnerable regardless of how it is labelled.

For clients who are themselves government employees, board staff or corporation employees caught by similar generalised bans — whether on travel, private practice, or property transactions — this case gives concrete language to invoke: a restriction that is "manifestly arbitrary" because it is "mechanically applied" without regard to "individual circumstances, nature of duties, designation... or purpose" is not merely bad policy, it is unconstitutional.

Statutory cross-references

This case turns entirely on Articles 14 and 21 of the Constitution of India, both of which are untouched by the 2023 recodification of the criminal statutes (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam), so no cross-reference to those enactments arises here. Practitioners relying on this judgment in service matters may also find it useful alongside the general body of Article 21 "right to travel abroad" precedent built up since Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, which the High Court expressly invoked as the foundation for its reasoning.
