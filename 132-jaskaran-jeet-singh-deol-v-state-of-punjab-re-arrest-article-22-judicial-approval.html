Jaskaran Jeet Singh Deol v. State of Punjab (2026 INSC 1022): Supreme Court Says Police Cannot Re-Arrest an Accused After an Article 22 Breach Without a Magistrate's Prior Approval

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Jaskaran Jeet Singh Deol v. State of Punjab (Reportable)
BenchJustice Ujjal Bhuyan and Justice Atul S. Chandurkar
Date21 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1022
Key SectionsArticle 22(1) and 22(2), Constitution; Sections 47 and 58 BNSS, 2023; BNS and POCSO Act, 2012

What the case was about

The appellant was arrested by the Punjab Police at 7:54 am on 17 May 2026 in a case under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita and the POCSO Act, 2012, alleging sexual offences against a minor. He was not given the grounds of arrest in writing, and he was produced before the Magistrate only around noon on 18 May 2026, that is, after more than 24 hours.

The Magistrate found the arrest illegal for want of written grounds. The real fight then began over what happens next. Can the police simply arrest the same person again, this time by the book, and carry on? The Supreme Court had to decide who gets to decide that question.

What the court held

The Bench held that once Article 22(1) has been breached, the power to re-arrest cannot be left to the discretion of the very same authority that violated the Constitution. Re-arrest needs what the Court called judicial imprimatur, meaning the prior approval of the jurisdictional Magistrate. The Court relied on its earlier line of authority: Pankaj Bansal v. Union of India (2023), Prabir Purkayastha v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2024), Vihaan Kumar v. State of Haryana (2025) and Mihir Rajesh Shah v. State of Maharashtra (2025), all of which treat written grounds of arrest as a constitutional requirement rather than a formality.

The Court laid down a procedure. The investigating agency must move the Magistrate with a written application that states the reasons for re-arrest, explains why the earlier requirement was not complied with, and carries the endorsement of a superior officer. The Magistrate must hear the accused, following natural justice, and should decide the application within one week. The same procedure applies where the breach is of Article 22(2), the 24-hour production rule.

Accountability was built in as well. The superior authority must transfer the investigation to another officer, and a departmental inquiry into the original violation is mandatory. Adverse findings should lead to disciplinary action, with a note in the officer's service record. The Court directed that the judgment be circulated to all Directors General of Police and to the Registrars of the High Courts. The summaries I reviewed do not spell out the final relief on the appellant's own plea, so that point should be checked in the full text.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

For the defence, the judgment adds a second layer to the arguments already run in remand hearings. If the arrest memo carries no written grounds, or the accused reached the Magistrate beyond 24 hours (excluding journey time), the illegality is not cured by the police arresting again next morning. Counsel should ask the remand Magistrate to record the breach in the order, because the finding is what triggers the re-arrest procedure. A fresh arrest with no prior application should be resisted at once, and a copy of the order should be kept ready for the High Court under Section 528 BNSS.

For prosecutors and investigating officers in Charkhi Dadri, Bhiwani and elsewhere, the message is practical: get the written grounds right the first time, in a language the accused understands, and log the time of arrest and production carefully. Where a lapse has already occurred, an application before the Magistrate, backed by a superior officer's endorsement, is now the only safe course. Remember that the judgment does not bar re-arrest altogether; it regulates who authorises it.

Magistrates carry the heaviest new duty. A one-week time limit and a hearing to the accused mean these applications cannot be disposed of in a routine way, and the written reasons will be scrutinised on revision.

Statutory cross-references

Article 22(1) and 22(2) of the Constitution are unchanged. Section 47 BNSS (person arrested to be informed of grounds of arrest) corresponds to Section 50 CrPC. Section 58 BNSS (production before the Magistrate within 24 hours) corresponds to Section 57 CrPC. Section 35 BNSS (arrest by police without warrant) corresponds to Section 41 CrPC, and Section 187 BNSS (remand) corresponds to Section 167 CrPC. The inherent powers of the High Court under Section 528 BNSS correspond to Section 482 CrPC.
