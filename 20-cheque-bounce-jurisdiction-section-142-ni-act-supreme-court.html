Jai Balaji Industries Ltd. v. M/s HEG Ltd.: Supreme Court Settles Where to File a Cheque Bounce Complaint

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Jai Balaji Industries Ltd. and Ors. v. M/s HEG Ltd.
BenchJustices J.B. Pardiwala & R. Mahadevan
Date28 November 2025
Citation2025 INSC 1362 : 2026(1) RCR (Criminal) 66 : 2025 SCC OnLine SC 2581
Key SectionsSections 138, 142(2) & 142A, Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

What the case was about

Cheque bounce litigation under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 is among the highest-volume categories of criminal work in Indian district courts — and for years, one question kept tripping up complainants and accused alike: which court actually has jurisdiction to try the complaint? This case gave the Supreme Court an opportunity to finally settle that question in a manner meant to bind every Magistrate in the country.

The facts were straightforward. A cheque for Rs. 19,94,996 drawn by Jai Balaji Industries Ltd. on its bank in Kolkata was deposited by the complainant, M/s HEG Limited, in its account with the State Bank of India, Bhopal branch. The cheque was dishonoured for insufficiency of funds, and after the accused disputed liability, HEG Limited filed its complaint before the Metropolitan Magistrate, Kolkata — a court where, as it turned out, evidence had already begun to be recorded. Midway through, the Negotiable Instruments (Amendment) Act, 2015 came into force, introducing Section 142(2) to clarify jurisdiction. Acting on this change, the Kolkata court returned the complaint for want of jurisdiction, and the complainant re-filed before the Judicial Magistrate First Class, Bhopal — the place where its bank account was actually maintained. The accused then challenged the Bhopal court's jurisdiction too, and asked that the case be sent back to Kolkata since evidence recording had already started there before the return. The resulting transfer petitions landed before the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The judgment, authored by Justice J.B. Pardiwala, methodically works through nearly two decades of conflicting Supreme Court precedent — K. Bhaskaran's expansive "any of five acts" test, Harman Electronics' narrowing of that test to exclude the place where notice was merely sent, and Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod's 2014 ruling that jurisdiction lay solely at the drawee bank where the cheque was dishonoured. It was precisely to overturn the practical difficulties created by Dashrath Rupsingh that Parliament inserted Section 142(2) through the 2015 Amendment Act.

The Court held that under Section 142(2)(a), a cheque bounce complaint concerning an account payee cheque must be filed in the court within whose local jurisdiction the payee's home branch is situated — that is, the specific branch where the payee actually maintains the account, not merely any bank of the same institution. Crucially, the Explanation to Section 142(2)(a) creates a legal fiction: even if the payee deposits the cheque for collection at a different, more convenient branch of the same bank, the law deems it to have been delivered at the home branch for jurisdictional purposes. The Court expressly disagreed with an earlier two-judge ruling in Yogesh Upadhyay v. Atlanta Ltd. (2023) 19 SCC 404, which had given primacy to wherever the cheque was actually deposited, and declared that ruling per incuriam for having overlooked the deeming fiction in the Explanation. On facts, this meant the complainant's home branch in Bhopal was where the case belonged — not Kolkata, where the cheque happened to have been deposited, and not the drawee bank's location either.

But the Court did not stop there. Recognising that the Kolkata court had already reached the stage of recording evidence under Section 145(2) before wrongly returning the complaint, the Bench invoked the principle from Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod that cases already at an advanced evidentiary stage should not be uprooted merely because a later clarification of the law shows the original court lacked jurisdiction. To avoid procedural injustice to the parties, the Supreme Court directed that the case be sent back to Kolkata and resume from before the point of return, rather than restart entirely afresh in Bhopal.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Cheque bounce complaints are a daily staple of practice before every Judicial Magistrate in Haryana, and jurisdictional objections are a favourite delaying tactic for defence counsel. This judgment gives practitioners a clean, three-step test: identify whether the cheque is an account payee cheque or a bearer cheque; if account payee, file only where the payee's home branch is situated, regardless of where the cheque was physically deposited for collection; if bearer, file where the drawee bank branch maintaining the drawer's account is situated. Complainants' counsel should resist the temptation to deposit cheques at a convenient branch merely to manufacture local jurisdiction — the deeming fiction in the Explanation will simply relocate the case back to the home branch regardless.

For accused persons and their counsel, this ruling closes off the once-fashionable argument that jurisdiction follows wherever the complainant chose to present the cheque. At the same time, defence counsel handling matters already pending at an advanced stage of evidence should be alert to the possibility that even a wrongly-seized court may be permitted to retain the case in the interests of avoiding procedural injustice, rather than have the matter transferred and restarted.

Statutory cross-references

The Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, including Sections 138, 142 and 145, remains a standalone special statute unaffected by the 2023 overhaul of the general criminal law, and Section 142(2) continues to override the ordinary place-of-trial provisions that would otherwise apply under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS), just as it previously overrode the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 — a special law occupies the field over the general procedural code. The transfer petition itself was filed under Section 446 of the BNSS, which corresponds to the erstwhile Section 406 of the CrPC, empowering the Supreme Court to transfer criminal cases and appeals from one High Court's jurisdiction to another where the interests of justice so require.
