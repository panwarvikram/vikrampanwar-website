Satnam Singh v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Says a Vanished Judicial File and a Resiled Witness Cannot Add Up to a Theft Conviction

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court
CaseSatnam Singh (since deceased) through his LR Rajwinder Kaur v. State of Punjab
BenchJustice Mandeep Pannu
DateDecided 2026 (reported 1 September 2026)
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 304
Key SectionsSs. 380 & 201, IPC, 1860 (corresponding to Ss. 305 & 238, BNS, 2023)

What the case was about

In June 2000, a judicial file went missing from the dais of the Court of the Chief Judicial Magistrate, Gurdaspur, shortly before it was listed for consideration on charge. The file was itself a criminal record — State v. Gurmit Singh, registered under Sections 420, 467, 468 and 471 IPC for cheating and forgery — and its disappearance meant the prosecution against Gurmit Singh and his associates effectively lost the paper trail it needed to proceed.

A separate FIR for theft was registered, and investigation led police to four men: Pradeep Kumar, Darshan Singh, Balwinder Singh, and Satnam Singh, a court driver. The prosecution's theory was that these four had orchestrated the disappearance to protect Gurmit Singh, himself declared a proclaimed offender who never came to trial on the theft charge. During interrogation, Darshan Singh allegedly gave a disclosure statement admitting he had burnt the file.

What followed was a case that outlived most of its own accused. By the time the matter reached the High Court in revision, three of the four original accused, including Satnam Singh and Darshan Singh, had died. Satnam Singh's revision petition was carried forward by his legal representative, his widow Rajwinder Kaur; the proceedings against Darshan Singh abated on his death. A prosecution built in the year 2000 was still being argued over in 2026, a quarter-century after the file itself vanished.

What the trial and appellate courts had held

The trial court acquitted all four of the more serious charge under Section 201 IPC (causing disappearance of evidence to screen an offender), because the one witness who was supposed to prove the file had been burnt, PW-1 Hardial Singh, did not support the prosecution's case at trial. Yet it convicted them under Section 380 IPC for theft, relying chiefly on Satnam Singh's presence in the court premises around the time the file disappeared. On appeal, the Additional Sessions Judge went further still — upholding the theft conviction, enhancing the sentence, and additionally convicting under Section 201 IPC too, reasoning the file had been stolen in concert to protect the original accused, in effect reinstating a charge the trial court had itself found unproven, without any fresh evidence in between.

What the High Court held

Justice Mandeep Pannu, hearing the revision petitions, returned to first principles on what circumstantial evidence can and cannot do. On the presence of Satnam Singh in the courtroom around the relevant time, the Court noted plainly: “The mere presence of Satnam Singh in the Court at the relevant time, followed by the disappearance of the file, could certainly give rise to suspicion, but suspicion, however strong, cannot take the place of proof.” Two witnesses, the court reader (PW-5) and the then Chief Judicial Magistrate (PW-6), had indeed established that Satnam Singh entered and left the courtroom close to when the file went missing. But entering and leaving a courtroom is not the same as removing a file from it, and the Court held that this evidence proved presence, nothing more.

The prosecution's other evidentiary thread, testimony from PW-4 about an alleged handover of the file among the accused, fared no better. That evidence rested on an extra-judicial confession said to have been made in front of a particular person, but the prosecution never examined that person as a witness — a lapse the Court held left “a significant gap in the prosecution case regarding the alleged conspiracy.” A confession nobody who actually heard it will come and vouch for is, functionally, hearsay dressed up as proof. As for Balwinder Singh, no overt act of any kind was attributed to him individually, and the judgment reaffirms that “mere presence of an accused along with the other persons, without any further reliable evidence demonstrating his participation… cannot by itself be made the basis of conviction.”

On the Section 201 IPC conviction the Appellate Court had added, the High Court was pointedly critical. The trial court's own unchallenged finding was that PW-1 Hardial Singh, the sole witness to the alleged burning, had resiled and denied even knowing Darshan Singh. That finding was never overturned by any fresh appraisal of evidence; the Appellate Court simply converted the same suspicious circumstances into a finding of guilt, which the High Court held was not permissible. Both revision petitions were allowed and the convictions set aside.

Why this matters for criminal practitioners in Haryana

This judgment is a clean, teachable illustration of a principle trial and revision practice in Haryana's district courts runs into constantly: circumstantial evidence has to close every gap in the chain, and presence at or near a scene is only ever the opening link, never the whole chain. Advocates defending cases built on "last seen," "was present," or "was seen entering/leaving" evidence will find this a useful citation precisely because the Court applies the rule to a mundane theft case rather than a high-profile murder trial, showing the principle is not reserved for capital offences.

It is also a caution against relying on extra-judicial confessions or disclosure statements without producing the person before whom the statement was allegedly made; a disclosure nobody independently corroborates is exceptionally fragile once challenged. And there is a structural lesson for advocates handling appellate and revisional matters out of Haryana's Sessions Courts: first appellate courts can reverse an acquittal-in-part where the evidence genuinely supports it, but that power is not a licence to reappraise settled findings of fact into a harsher outcome without identifying what, specifically, the trial court got wrong. Here, the Additional Sessions Judge added a Section 201 IPC conviction without disturbing the trial court's own finding that the key witness had not supported the prosecution, and the High Court treated that as impermissible bootstrapping of suspicion into guilt.

Finally, the twenty-six-year timeline, with three of four accused dying before their revision was even decided, is itself a quiet indictment of how long revisional remedies can take to work through India's appellate hierarchy — a reminder that even a straightforward theft case, absent a swift and well-evidenced trial, can consume the better part of a generation before final vindication arrives, if it arrives at all.

Statutory cross-references

The offences at issue, Section 380 IPC (theft in a building) and Section 201 IPC (causing disappearance of evidence of an offence, or giving false information to screen an offender), were tried and appealed under the old Indian Penal Code and Code of Criminal Procedure, since the offence predates the 2023 recodification. Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, the corresponding provisions are Section 305 BNS (theft in a dwelling house, building used as a place of worship, or for custody of property) and Section 238 BNS (causing disappearance of evidence of an offence, or giving false information to screen an offender). Practitioners handling fresh matters after 1 July 2024 should frame charges under these renumbered BNS provisions, while matters registered before that date, as in this case, continue to be governed by the IPC under which they were originally lodged.
