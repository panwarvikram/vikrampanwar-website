Daughter's Right in Ancestral Property: How Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act Works After Vineeta Sharma

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma & Ors.
BenchJustice Arun Mishra, Justice S. Abdul Nazeer and Justice M.R. Shah (three judges)
Date11 August 2020
Citation(2020) 9 SCC 1
Key SectionsSection 6, Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (as substituted by the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005); Section 8; Section 4(2) (omitted in 2005)

What the topic is about

“Does my sister have a share in our father's land?” and “My father died before 2005. Can I still claim my share as a daughter?” are among the most common questions heard at district courts in Haryana. Most families still think of jaddi (ancestral) land as passing from father to son. The law has not worked that way since 9 September 2005.

That was the day the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 came into force. It replaced Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act and made a daughter a coparcener by birth in a Mitakshara joint Hindu family, “in her own right in the same manner as the son”. For fifteen years afterwards, courts disagreed on whom the amendment covered. In Prakash v. Phulavati (2016) a two-judge bench held that both the father and the daughter had to be alive on 9 September 2005. In Danamma v. Amar (2018) another two-judge bench gave a share even though the father had died in 2001. A three-judge bench resolved the conflict in Vineeta Sharma.

The legal position

The practical takeaways from Vineeta Sharma and the text of Section 6 are these:

  • The right comes from birth, not from the father's survival. A daughter is a coparcener whether she was born before or after 2005. It does not matter whether her father was alive on 9 September 2005. Phulavati was overruled, and Danamma was overruled to the extent it conflicted.
  • The right can be claimed from 9 September 2005. The Court described the provision as retroactive: it operates from 2005, but on the basis of an earlier event, namely the daughter's birth.
  • Old settled transactions are protected. Under the proviso to Section 6(1), a sale, gift, other disposition, testamentary disposition or partition made before 20 December 2004 (the date the Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha) is not reopened.
  • Only real partitions count. The Explanation to Section 6(5) recognises only a partition made by a registered deed or by a court decree. The Court was wary of oral partitions and family arrangements put forward after the event to defeat daughters. It said an oral partition may be accepted only in exceptional cases, where it is backed by public documents and proved as convincingly as a court decree.
  • A notional partition is not an actual partition. Under the old proviso to Section 6, a notional partition was assumed when a coparcener died leaving female heirs. That was only a way to work out the deceased's share. It did not break up the coparcenary, so shares are finally determined when an actual partition takes place.
  • Pending cases follow the new rule. Suits and appeals pending on the point were to be decided according to Vineeta Sharma, and the Court asked that they be disposed of, as far as possible, within six months.

Being a coparcener also carries obligations. Section 6(1)(c) makes the daughter subject to the same liabilities as a son. She can seek partition, and under Section 6(2) she can dispose of her share by will. Marriage has no effect on any of this: a married daughter is as much a coparcener as an unmarried one.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Agricultural land is covered. Before 2005, Section 4(2) excluded tenancy rights in agricultural holdings from the Act, leaving them to State tenure laws. The 2005 amendment omitted Section 4(2). In a State where most family wealth is farmland, this is the point that matters most.

First check whether the property is really coparcenary. This is where many partition claims fail. Land a father inherited from his father after 1956 under Section 8 of the Act is, as a rule, his separate property, not coparcenary property in the hands of his children. The Supreme Court has said so in Yudhishter v. Ashok Kumar (1987) and Uttam v. Saubhag Singh (2016). Calling land “jaddi” in a village does not make it coparcenary in law. Where the property is not coparcenary, the daughter usually still gets a share, but as a Class I heir under Section 8 when her father dies without a will. The difference matters, because a father can dispose of his separate property by will, whereas a daughter's coparcenary interest is hers from birth.

Look at the documents behind any claimed partition. When brothers rely on an old family partition to defeat a sister's claim, ask whether there is a registered deed or a decree dated before 20 December 2004. If the partition was oral, or was recorded only in a khangi taksim or a later mutation, it is open to challenge under Vineeta Sharma. A mutation entry is only a fiscal record and does not by itself prove title or partition.

Relinquishment must be proper. Families often get daughters to sign a “haq tyag” or release in favour of brothers. Giving up an interest in immovable property generally needs a registered instrument. An unregistered affidavit or a statement made before the revenue staff is weak evidence of relinquishment.

Choosing the forum. A daughter who wants her share of agricultural land that is already recorded in her name as a co-sharer can often seek partition before the revenue officer under the partition chapter of the Punjab Land Revenue Act, 1887, as it applies in Haryana. Where title itself is disputed, for example where the brothers deny she has any share, set up a will, or rely on an old partition, the right course is a civil suit for declaration and partition. On limitation, Article 110 of the Limitation Act gives a person excluded from joint family property twelve years from the date the exclusion becomes known to them.

Statutory cross-references

  • Section 6, Hindu Succession Act, 1956 — coparcenary rights of daughters, as substituted with effect from 9 September 2005. Section 6(3) replaces survivorship with testamentary or intestate succession for deaths after 2005. Section 6(4) abolishes the “pious obligation” to pay a father's or grandfather's debts for debts contracted after 2005.
  • Section 8 and the Schedule — intestate succession to a male Hindu's separate property; the daughter is a Class I heir.
  • Section 4(2) — omitted by the 2005 Amendment, which brings agricultural holdings within the Act.
  • Section 17, Registration Act, 1908 — compulsory registration of partition deeds and releases of interest in immovable property.
  • Order XX Rule 18, CPC — decree in a suit for partition (preliminary decree, then final decree or execution through the Collector for revenue-paying land).
  • Article 110, Limitation Act, 1963 — suit by a person excluded from joint family property.
  • Criminal-law codes (IPC/BNS, CrPC/BNSS) are not directly engaged. Disputes over forged partition deeds or wills, however, may lead to complaints under the forgery provisions now found in Sections 336–340 BNS (formerly Sections 463–471 IPC).
