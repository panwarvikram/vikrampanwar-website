Jaspreet Singh v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Quashes an Attempt-to-Suicide FIR, Says Section 115 of the Mental Healthcare Act Bars Prosecution and Arms Act Charges Need Specifics

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — Jaspreet Singh v. State of Punjab & Anr., CRM-M-5128-2025
BenchJustice Neerja K. Kalson (single judge)
Date22 September 2026 (date of decision; reported 6–7 October 2026)
CitationCRM-M-5128-2025; Supreme (Online) (P&H) 2026 140367
Key SectionsSection 115 and 120, Mental Healthcare Act, 2017; Sections 309, 109, 107 IPC; Sections 27, 30 Arms Act; Section 528 BNSS

What the case was about

FIR No. 24 was registered on 9 March 2024 at Police Station Talwandi Sabo, District Bathinda, under Sections 309 and 109 IPC and Sections 27 and 30 of the Arms Act. The allegation was that the petitioner, Jaspreet Singh, shot himself in an attempt to end his life, using a licensed revolver that belonged to a co-accused. His father had told the police that the act arose from stress over household and family circumstances. A final report (challan) was filed on 17 September 2024.

The petitioner approached the High Court under Section 528 BNSS to quash the FIR, the challan and all consequential proceedings. He argued that the FIR itself showed severe stress, that nothing rebutted the statutory presumption in Section 115(1) of the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, and that the Arms Act allegations disclosed no offence. The State responded that a challan had been filed on sufficient material and that Section 115 could not be mechanically extended to the separate Arms Act offences.

What the Court held

Justice Kalson allowed the petition. On Section 309 IPC, the Court held that Section 115(1) of the 2017 Act presumes, unless proved otherwise, that a person who attempts suicide was under severe stress, and that such a person is not to be tried and punished under the IPC. Section 120 gives the Act overriding effect over inconsistent laws. The Court read this as a deliberate shift from penalisation to a therapeutic and rehabilitative approach, and distinguished Gian Kaur, which upheld Section 309 before the 2017 Act existed. Relying also on Common Cause v. Union of India and Ravinder Kumar Dhariwal v. Union of India, it found the presumption unrebutted on this record.

The Court then examined the remaining charges separately, noting that the Section 115 protection “does not conclude the matter.” The Section 109 IPC charge failed because abetment requires instigation, conspiracy or intentional aid under Section 107, and nobody was alleged to have abetted any offence; the revolver belonging to a co-accused did not change that.

On the Arms Act, the Court followed Surinder Singh v. State (UT of Chandigarh): after the amendment, Section 27 penalises use of a firearm only where it contravenes Section 5 or Section 7, and using a licensed weapon does not by itself attract it. No prohibited weapon was alleged and no Section 5 breach identified. Following Tarlochan Singh @ Rana v. State of Punjab, Section 30 needs a specific breach of a licence condition or of the Act or Rules, and none was pleaded. That the licence stood in another person’s name was not enough. Applying the Bhajan Lal category, the Court held that the allegations, taken at face value, did not make out the offences, so no appreciation of evidence was needed, and that filing a challan does not cure the absence of essential ingredients.

Why this matters for criminal practitioners in Haryana

Attempt-to-suicide FIRs under Section 309 IPC are still registered routinely, often with add-on charges, and they reach our trial courts as challans. This judgment gives defence counsel a clear, layered argument: first invoke the Section 115 presumption and the Section 120 override, then attack each add-on charge on its own statutory ingredients. The second step is the more transferable lesson. Arms Act charges are frequently tacked on to any incident involving a licensed weapon, and the Court insisted that the prosecution must identify which provision or licence condition was actually breached.

Procedurally, the case is also a useful reminder that a filed challan is not a shield against quashing. Where the defect is legal, meaning the allegations do not disclose the offence even if accepted in full, the High Court can act under Section 528 BNSS without weighing disputed facts. At the trial stage, the same points can be taken at the framing-of-charge or discharge stage if quashing is not pursued.

One caution: the presumption in Section 115(1) is rebuttable, and the order turned on the prosecution’s own case showing stress and nothing contrary on record. It is also a single-judge ruling on its facts, and the report available to me did not discuss how the BNS treats such cases.

Statutory cross-references

Section 309 IPC (attempt to commit suicide) has no direct counterpart in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023; Section 226 BNS is a different, narrower offence (attempt to commit suicide to compel or restrain the exercise of lawful power by a public servant). Section 109 IPC (abetment, where the act abetted is committed) corresponds to Section 49 BNS, and Section 107 IPC (abetment of a thing) to Section 45 BNS. Section 482 CrPC, the old inherent-powers provision, is now Section 528 BNSS. Sections 5, 7, 27 and 30 of the Arms Act, 1959 and Sections 115 and 120 of the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 are unaffected by the new codes.
