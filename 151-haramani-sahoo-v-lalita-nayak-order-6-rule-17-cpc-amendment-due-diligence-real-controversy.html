Haramani Sahoo v. Lalita Nayak: Supreme Court Allows a Plaint Amendment Made 14 Years Into a Title Suit, Holding That New Facts Tied to the Same Dispute Do Not Change the Suit’s Character

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Haramani Sahoo & Ors. v. Lalita Nayak & Ors.
BenchJustice B.V. Nagarathna and Justice R. Mahadevan (judgment by Justice Mahadevan)
Date12 October 2026
CitationCivil Appeal No. 13681 of 2026 (arising out of SLP (C) No. 25635 of 2023); neutral citation not available in the reports consulted
Key SectionsOrder VI Rule 17 and Section 151 CPC; Order XXIII Rule 3A CPC

What the case was about

In 2009 the original plaintiff filed a title suit in Cuttack seeking a declaration of title, recovery of possession, and rectification of registered sale deeds of 1971 and 1978 over a small parcel of about 0.12 acre. The suit crawled on and the pleadings were amended more than once. Years in, he learnt through inquiries at the Tahsildar’s office that a compromise decree had been passed in an earlier title suit of 1998, to which he was not a party, allotting part of the disputed land to one of the defendants.

He applied under Order VI Rule 17 read with Section 151 CPC to plead that decree and to seek a declaration that it was void. He died while the application was pending and his legal heirs carried it on. The trial court refused the amendment in September 2022 and the Orissa High Court declined to interfere in April 2023. The defendants had argued that the application came nearly fourteen years after the suit, changed its nature, was time-barred, and that Order XXIII Rule 3A barred any attack on a compromise decree by a separate challenge.

What the court held

The Supreme Court set aside both orders and permitted the amendment, subject to costs of ₹25,000 to the contesting respondents within two weeks, consequential amendments within four weeks, and liberty to the defendants to file additional written statements.

The test. The question is not whether the amendment enlarges the factual or legal questions in the suit. It is whether it brings in a distinct controversy unconnected with the original cause of action, or only adds facts and consequential reliefs that are closely tied to the dispute already before the court. Here the Court found the amendment substantially overlapped with the existing title dispute. Changing or adding to the relief, by itself, does not alter the nature of the suit.

Due diligence. After the 2002 amendment, the proviso to Order VI Rule 17 bars amendments after trial has begun unless the court is satisfied that the party could not have raised the matter earlier despite due diligence. The Court accepted that the plaintiff, a stranger to the earlier compromise proceedings, learnt of it late, that the vendors had not disclosed their source of title, and that certified copies were obtained only in June 2020. That met the proviso.

Limitation and Order XXIII Rule 3A left open. Because the date of knowledge was disputed, the Court refused to decide limitation at the threshold. It stressed that allowing the amendment is not an acceptance of its averments and gives no substantive right in respect of the decree. Limitation, maintainability, the effect of Rule 3A, the validity of the compromise decree, title and possession were all left to the trial court.

Avoiding fragmentation. Refusal would push the plaintiff into separate proceedings over the same land, inviting inconsistent findings. Any prejudice to the defendants, the Court said, could be met by an additional written statement and costs. The Court distinguished Basavaraj v. Indira, which the respondents had relied on, as turning on its own facts.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Amendment applications are among the commonest interlocutory fights in civil suits for declaration, possession and partition in our courts, and they are routinely opposed on the twin grounds of delay and change of character. This judgment gives the applicant a clear framing: show a genuine and documented reason for the late discovery, such as the date certified copies were obtained, and show that the new facts are connected to the controversy already pleaded. The opposing party should, in turn, concentrate on the proviso, the absence of particulars about when and how the new fact came to light, and on genuine prejudice, rather than on limitation alone, which courts will usually keep for trial.

Equally important is the practical outcome: costs and an opportunity for additional written statements are the usual price of a late amendment, and the court is unlikely to reject it merely because the suit is old. The ruling should not be read as a licence for casual amendments. The Court expressly kept the defendants’ limitation and Rule 3A objections alive, so careful drafting of the amended pleading, especially on the date of knowledge, will matter at the next stage.

Statutory cross-references

Order VI Rule 17 CPC (as amended in 2002, with its proviso on amendments after commencement of trial), Section 151 CPC (inherent powers) and Order XXIII Rule 3A CPC (bar on a suit to set aside a compromise decree on the ground that the compromise was unlawful) remain governed by the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, which the 2023 criminal-law reforms did not replace. Limitation questions on suits to set aside decrees or instruments fall under the Limitation Act, 1963, for the trial court to decide.
