When Can Maintenance Legally Be Denied to a Wife?

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing ProvisionsSection 125(4), CrPC, 1973 / Section 144(4)–(5), BNSS, 2023; Sections 24 & 25, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955
Leading CaseRohtash Singh v. Ramendri (Smt.) & Ors., Supreme Court of India
Citation(2000) 3 SCC 180
Recent ApplicationPunjab & Haryana High Court, order dated 12 May 2026 (Justice Neerja K. Kalson) — mere allegations of adultery held insufficient to deny interim maintenance

Maintenance is a right, not automatic — but it is also not absolute

Courts routinely describe maintenance under Section 125 CrPC (now Section 144 BNSS) as a measure of social justice meant to prevent destitution, not charity extended at the husband's discretion. But the same provision that creates this right also carves out specific, narrowly-construed exceptions where a wife's claim can be refused. Separately, under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, interim maintenance during matrimonial proceedings turns on a different test — whether the claimant genuinely lacks sufficient independent income, not on marital misconduct. Understanding both frameworks — and how narrowly courts read the exceptions — is essential before advising a client that maintenance can be resisted.

Ground one: living in adultery — not a single lapse, but a quasi-permanent union

Section 125(4) CrPC / Section 144(4) BNSS bars maintenance where the wife "is living in adultery." Courts have consistently held this phrase demands far more than an isolated incident or unproven suspicion — it requires an outright adulterous relationship in the nature of a quasi-permanent union with another man, continuing at or near the time the maintenance claim is made. A single meeting, a photograph at a social event, or an uncorroborated allegation does not meet this threshold.

This was reaffirmed by the Punjab & Haryana High Court on 12 May 2026, in a revision petition where a husband sought to resist interim maintenance by producing photographs of his wife with another man. Justice Neerja K. Kalson held that matrimonial litigation cannot become a forum for unsubstantiated character attacks, and that allegations of adultery — being disputed questions of fact — require cogent, admissible evidence and adjudication at final trial, not disposal at the interim stage on the strength of photographs alone that did not conclusively establish any illicit relationship.

Ground two: refusal to live with the husband, without sufficient reason

The second statutory exception applies where the wife, without sufficient reason, refuses to live with her husband. The key word is refusal, not mere failure or absence — and Indian courts have been careful to distinguish the two. Where a wife has left the matrimonial home because of cruelty, dowry harassment, or a husband's own misconduct, this is not "refusal without sufficient reason" at all; the statute itself recognises, both under the old Code and under BNSS Section 144, that if a husband has contracted a second marriage or keeps a mistress, that is itself just cause for the wife's refusal to live with him — flipping the exception back in the wife's favour.

In Rohtash Singh v. Ramendri, the Supreme Court held that even a wife who left the matrimonial home and refused to return remained entitled to maintenance after divorce, since a divorced wife falls squarely within the definition of "wife" under Section 125's Explanation, and is disqualified only if she has remarried or otherwise falls within the specific statutory exceptions — desertion by itself, without more, does not automatically forfeit the claim once the marriage has ended.

Ground three: living separately by mutual consent

Where spouses are living apart under an actual mutual agreement — for instance, recorded in a settlement, a memorandum of understanding, or clearly established conduct — maintenance can be denied on this ground alone. The burden lies on the husband to establish that the separation was genuinely consensual, not merely convenient to characterise it that way after the fact.

A different test under the Hindu Marriage Act: sufficient independent income

Sections 24 and 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 operate on an entirely separate footing from Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS. Interim maintenance under Section 24 is meant only to ensure that a financially weaker spouse is not handicapped in litigating the matrimonial case — it is not meant to equalise the parties' incomes or preserve an equivalent lifestyle. Courts have denied interim maintenance where both spouses are similarly qualified and earning comparably, holding that the mere fact a wife is educated or capable of earning is not, by itself, disqualifying — what matters is whether she actually possesses sufficient independent income for her support and litigation expenses at the time the application is decided.

Why this matters for Matrimonial practitioners in Haryana

For husbands' counsel, the message from the May 2026 Punjab & Haryana ruling is blunt: photographs, social media posts, or bare allegations of adultery will not survive scrutiny at the interim stage unless backed by material establishing a genuinely continuing illicit relationship — pleading adultery is easy, proving it to the standard courts demand is not. For wives' counsel, the reminder from Rohtash Singh is equally important: desertion, by itself, is rarely fatal to a maintenance claim, and a husband's own misconduct — bigamy, keeping a mistress, cruelty — can convert what looks like the wife's "refusal" into a fully justified departure that keeps the claim alive. And in HMA proceedings specifically, practitioners should lead clear evidence of actual income and expenses rather than relying on qualifications or employability alone, since that is the test family courts across Haryana are now applying at the interim stage.

Statutory cross-references

Section 125 of the CrPC, 1973 — including its Section 125(4) exceptions — now corresponds to Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023, with the exceptions and the husband's-misconduct proviso retained in substance under Section 144(4)–(5). Sections 24 and 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 are unaffected by the BNSS renumbering, since they arise under a separate personal-law statute rather than the criminal procedure code.
