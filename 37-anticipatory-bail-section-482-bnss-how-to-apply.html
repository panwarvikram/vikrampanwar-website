How to Get Anticipatory Bail in India: Section 482 BNSS Explained

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseGurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab; Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi)
BenchConstitution Bench (Sibbia); five-judge Bench (Sushila Aggarwal)
Date1980 and 2020 (governing precedents); provision in force since 1 July 2024
CitationAIR 1980 SC 1632 · (2020) 5 SCC 1
Key SectionsS. 482, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (replacing S. 438, CrPC)

What anticipatory bail is, and when people search for it

Anticipatory bail is a court's direction, obtained before an arrest actually happens, that if the applicant is arrested for a non-bailable offence, they will be released on bail. It exists precisely for the situation that sends most people searching for this topic at odd hours: a rumour, a legal notice, a summons, or knowledge that a rival party has lodged or is about to lodge a criminal complaint, and the fear of being picked up and locked in before there is any chance to respond. Since 1 July 2024, this remedy is governed by Section 482 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS), which has replaced the old Section 438 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. The substance of the law has not changed materially; the renumbering and some drafting tidy-ups are the main differences a practitioner needs to track.

The remedy is available only against apprehended arrest for a non-bailable offence. It is not available once a person has already been arrested (that calls for regular bail under Sections 480–483, BNSS, depending on the stage), and it is not a licence to pre-empt investigation altogether — it only protects against custodial arrest, not against being investigated, questioned, or chargesheeted.

Who can apply, and to which court

Any person who has a reasonable apprehension of arrest in connection with a non-bailable offence can apply. The apprehension must be concrete and specific — a vague, generalised fear of possible future prosecution is not sufficient; courts expect the applicant to point to something tangible, such as a named FIR, a police complaint already lodged against them, a legal notice threatening criminal action, or credible information that a complaint is imminent. An applicant does not need to wait for an FIR to actually be registered before approaching the court, but the closer the application is to a real, identifiable threat, the stronger it is.

Applications under Section 482 BNSS can be filed before the Court of Session or the High Court. In practice, and by long-standing judicial convention reaffirmed in Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia, the Sessions Court is treated as the ordinary first forum, with the High Court reserved for cases that are either more serious, involve a Sessions Court refusal, or raise questions the Sessions Court is unlikely to resolve satisfactorily. For most applicants in Haryana, this means the application is filed before the Sessions Court having territorial jurisdiction over the police station where the offence is alleged to have occurred, or is expected to be registered.

What the application needs, and what the court examines

The application is drafted as a petition under Section 482, BNSS, verified by an affidavit setting out the facts: the applicant's identity, the nature of the apprehended offence, the basis for the apprehension of arrest, and why custodial interrogation is not necessary. Supporting documents — copies of any FIR, legal notice, complaint, or correspondence — are annexed. Notice is ordinarily issued to the State (through the Public Prosecutor) and, depending on the practice of the particular court, to the complainant, before the application is finally decided, though interim protection can sometimes be granted at the first hearing pending notice.

Courts do not grant anticipatory bail mechanically. The factors courts weigh, as laid down across Sibbia, Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre v. State of Maharashtra, and Sushila Aggarwal, include: the nature and gravity of the accusation; the applicant's antecedents, including any previous convictions or pending cases; the possibility of the applicant fleeing from justice; whether the accusation has been made with the object of injuring or humiliating the applicant by having them arrested; and whether custodial interrogation is genuinely necessary to the investigation, as opposed to being sought as a matter of routine. In Sushila Aggarwal, a five-judge Bench also settled a long-running controversy by holding that anticipatory bail need not be limited to a fixed period or automatically expire at the stage of filing the chargesheet — the protection can, depending on the facts, continue until the end of the trial, unless the court itself limits it or specific circumstances warrant a shorter tenure.

Conditions the court can impose

Section 482(2), BNSS mirrors the old Section 438(2), CrPC and requires the court, while granting the relief, to impose conditions that the applicant: (i) makes themselves available for police interrogation as and when required; (ii) does not, directly or indirectly, make any inducement, threat, or promise to any person acquainted with the facts, so as to dissuade them from disclosing such facts to the court or the police, or tamper with evidence; (iii) does not leave India without the court's prior permission; and (iv) any other condition the court considers necessary, including one similar to those that can be imposed on regular bail under Section 480(3), BNSS. Courts have repeatedly cautioned, in decisions through 2026 as before, that bail conditions must be reasonable and proportionate, and cannot be humiliating, degrading, or unconnected to the purpose of securing the applicant's presence and cooperation.

Why this matters for practitioners in Haryana

Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, like most district court towns in Haryana, see a steady stream of anticipatory bail applications arising from matrimonial complaints under Sections 85–86, BNS (formerly Section 498-A, IPC), property and boundary disputes escalating into criminal complaints, and cheque bounce or financial disputes accompanied by allegations of cheating. Getting the drafting right at the first hearing — a specific, documented apprehension rather than a vague one, and a clear-eyed acknowledgment of any adverse antecedents rather than concealment — materially improves the odds before the Sessions Court, and avoids the delay and expense of a subsequent High Court round.

Statutory cross-references

Section 482, BNSS, 2023 corresponds to Section 438, CrPC, 1973. Regular (post-arrest) bail, previously under Sections 437 and 439, CrPC, now falls under Sections 480, 482, and 483, BNSS. The "written grounds of arrest" safeguard discussed elsewhere on this blog operates alongside, not instead of, the anticipatory bail remedy — an applicant who is arrested despite a pending anticipatory bail plea, or without being furnished grounds of arrest, has independent recourse on that ground as well.
