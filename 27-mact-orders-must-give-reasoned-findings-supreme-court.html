Oriental Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Durg Roadways: Supreme Court Says MACT Orders Cannot Just Recite Pleadings Without Reasoning

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseOriental Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Durg Roadways Private Limited & Ors.
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & Nongmeikapam Kotiswar Singh, Supreme Court of India
Date20 July 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (SC) 696
Key SectionsSs. 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

The appeal before the Supreme Court arose from a bus accident that occurred in Nepal, and the narrow legal question was whether Oriental Insurance Co. Ltd. was liable to pay compensation under a motor insurance policy for an accident that took place outside India. On the facts, the insurer's appeal against the Tribunal's award was dismissed, and the insurer's liability to pay compensation was upheld.

But the more consequential part of the judgment had little to do with the Nepal accident itself. While disposing of the appeal, the Bench of Justice Sanjay Karol and Justice Nongmeikapam Kotiswar Singh used the occasion to record its unease with how Motor Accident Claims Tribunals across the country are writing their orders. The Court noted that in this case, and in "several" others that had come up before it, the Tribunal had extensively recorded the parties' submissions and the evidence led during trial, but had failed to connect that material to its final conclusion in any meaningful way — the order read more like a transcript of the hearing than a reasoned adjudication.

This is not a stray observation. Anyone who practises regularly before MACTs — including the Tribunals functioning in Haryana's district courts — will recognise the pattern the Supreme Court is describing: pages of recorded testimony and counsel's arguments, followed by a compensation figure that appears without the reader being able to trace how the Tribunal actually arrived at it.

What the court held

The Bench was direct about why this matters. As the judgment, authored by Justice Karol, put it, Tribunals are approached by people who have suffered accidents or lost family members — both are situations nobody wants to be in — and it is "only to be expected" that whatever conclusion a Tribunal reaches, that conclusion must be supported by "adequate and clear reasoning." The Court said the "tone, tenor and extent" of some Tribunal orders coming up before it had caused it "considerable disturbance," and it reminded Tribunals of their judicial responsibility to arrive at conclusions through proper legal reasoning — not merely record the raw material and stop there.

The Court also flagged the practical downstream cost of poorly reasoned orders. When a Tribunal's award doesn't explain its own logic, an aggrieved party finds it far harder to decide whether to accept the award or to appeal it, and settlement becomes correspondingly harder to negotiate. The Bench observed that the clearer the reasoning, and the higher a Tribunal's fidelity to binding Supreme Court precedent — since Supreme Court law applies uniformly across the country — the more the "large gap" between the date a claim petition is filed and the date it is finally disposed of will shrink, along with the number of appeals clogging the higher courts. In other words, reasoned orders are not just fairer to the claimant standing in front of the Tribunal; they reduce appellate litigation system-wide.

On the substantive question actually before it — cross-border liability for an accident in Nepal under a policy issued in India — the Court declined to disturb the Tribunal's finding of the insurer's liability, dismissing Oriental Insurance's appeal.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

For advocates appearing before the Motor Accident Claims Tribunals at Charkhi Dadri, Bhiwani, and elsewhere in Haryana, this judgment is a useful tool at two different stages of a case.

First, at the drafting and argument stage, it gives counsel for claimants a concrete basis to press Tribunals for orders that actually engage with the evidence — income proof, medical records, the notional-income and multiplier calculations, and the specific heads of compensation — rather than orders that summarise the record and jump to a lump-sum figure. Where a Tribunal's draft reasoning during dictation or in a reserved order appears to be heading toward a bare recitation, this ruling is squarely on point to remind the court, respectfully, of what the Supreme Court now expects.

Second, at the appellate stage, this judgment strengthens the ground of "non-application of mind" or "absence of reasons" as an independent basis to challenge a MACT award before the High Court under Section 173 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, separate from challenging the quantum or the finding of liability itself. An award that recites the evidence at length but never explains how that evidence produced the final compensation figure is now more vulnerable to being set aside or remanded purely on reasoning grounds — a point worth pleading explicitly rather than folding into a general quantum challenge.

Insurers' counsel should also take note: the Court's emphasis on reasoned findings cuts both ways. A Tribunal order that mechanically accepts an insurer's defence without analysing the evidence is just as vulnerable as one that mechanically accepts a claimant's case.

Statutory cross-references

The core provisions in play remain unchanged by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, since motor accident compensation claims are governed by the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 rather than the criminal codes. Section 166 (application for compensation) and Section 168 (award of the Claims Tribunal) of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 remain the operative provisions, and an appeal against a Tribunal's award still lies under Section 173 of the same Act. Practitioners should note that the reasoning requirement the Supreme Court has now underlined applies equally to orders passed by Lok Adalats and Tribunals exercising summary procedure under the Act, and is likely to be cited going forward alongside the Court's earlier rulings insisting on structured income assessment (as in the Rashmirekha Tripathy line of cases decided earlier this month) and on not deducting collateral benefits such as Mediclaim reimbursement from the compensation award.
