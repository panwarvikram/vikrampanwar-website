Bona Fide Requirement in Eviction: P&H High Court Says a Tenant Cannot Dictate Terms to the Landlord

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — Civil Revision arising from a Ludhiana shop eviction petition
BenchJustice Sudeepti Sharma
DateReported 4 June 2026 (the Rent Appellate Authority's order under challenge was dated 19 May 2025)
CitationNeutral citation/CR number not traceable in open sources at time of writing — see disclaimer
Key SectionsS.13, East Punjab Urban Rent Restriction Act, 1949 · analogous S.13, Haryana Urban (Control of Rent & Eviction) Act, 1973

What the case was about

A landlord in Ludhiana sought eviction of a tenant from a shop on Brown Road so that his married son could start an independent spare-parts business. The landlord's case was that the son neither owned nor occupied any other commercial premises, and that the family genuinely needed the shop back. The Rent Controller and, on appeal, the Appellate Authority both found in the landlord's favour and ordered eviction.

The tenant carried the matter to the High Court in revision, raising two defences that are extremely common in rent litigation across Punjab and Haryana. First, he argued that the landlord had failed to prove his status as "owner-landlord" of the property, since the shop had at one point belonged to a family trust rather than to the landlord personally. Second, he suggested that even if the son needed a shop, the landlord could accommodate that need in other ways without recovering possession from the sitting tenant.

What the court held

Justice Sudeepti Sharma rejected both arguments and upheld the eviction. On ownership, the Court examined the documents on record and the landlord's cross-examination and found that the property, though once held by a family trust, was by the relevant date owned exclusively by the landlord — sufficient to establish him as landlord entitled to rent and to eviction.

More significantly, the Court went further and held that even if the ownership question were set aside entirely, the tenant's challenge would still fail. Section 13 of the East Punjab Urban Rent Restriction Act, 1949 only requires that the person seeking eviction be a "landlord" — and the Act's definition of landlord expressly includes a trustee. A trustee who receives rent on behalf of a trust is therefore fully competent to maintain an eviction petition, regardless of whether beneficial title has separately vested in an individual.

On the second defence — the tenant's suggestion that the landlord could meet his son's needs some other way — the Court was equally direct. Relying on Supreme Court authority, it held that a Rent Controller must proceed on the presumption that a landlord's stated requirement is bona fide, and that it is not open to a tenant to dictate how else the landlord should adjust himself instead of recovering the tenanted premises. The Court also reiterated that "for his own use" under rent legislation is not limited to the landlord's personal use — it extends to use by dependent family members, or by a person on whom the landlord is himself dependent. Finding no illegality in the Appellate Authority's order, the High Court dismissed the tenant's revision petition and disposed of all pending applications.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Eviction petitions on the ground of bona fide personal necessity are among the most frequently litigated civil matters in district courts across Haryana, and this ruling is useful on two fronts that come up constantly in practice. The first is the trustee-as-landlord point. Family-owned commercial property in small-town Haryana is very often held through informal or formal trusts, HUFs, or family arrangements, and tenants routinely attack the landlord's locus standi by pointing to gaps in the paper chain of title. This judgment confirms that strict proof of exclusive personal ownership is not always necessary — a person receiving rent in a trustee or landlord capacity, as statutorily defined, can maintain the petition.

The second and arguably more important point is the "alternative arrangement" defence. Tenants facing a genuine bona fide requirement claim frequently try to defeat it by proposing that the landlord's family member could work from home or share space with a relative instead of recovering the tenanted premises. This judgment gives practitioners representing landlords a clean, citable line of reasoning to shut that argument down at the threshold — the presumption runs in the landlord's favour once a genuine need is shown, and the tenant does not get to redesign the landlord's business plans.

For tenants' counsel, the takeaway is the mirror image: an "alternative accommodation" defence rarely succeeds. Better results come from genuinely disproving the bona fide character of the requirement — for instance, showing the landlord already owns comparable premises, or that the stated need is a pretext for re-letting at a higher rent.

Statutory cross-references

The East Punjab Urban Rent Restriction Act, 1949 continues to govern rented premises in Punjab, while Haryana enacted its own parallel legislation, the Haryana Urban (Control of Rent and Eviction) Act, 1973, after the reorganisation of the state — Section 13 of the Haryana Act mirrors the 1949 Punjab Act's scheme on grounds of eviction, including bona fide personal necessity, and the definition of "landlord" under Section 2(c) of the Haryana Act likewise extends beyond the sole registered owner. Rent legislation is a civil enactment and is entirely unaffected by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, both of which recodify only the criminal law (replacing the IPC and CrPC respectively). Procedurally, revisions and appeals under both Rent Acts continue to draw on the general principles of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 — particularly the appellate framework under Order XLI CPC and the limited-interference standard associated with revisional jurisdiction under Section 115 CPC — even where the Rent Act itself provides a self-contained appeal and revision mechanism.
