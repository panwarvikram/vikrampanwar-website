State of Andhra Pradesh v. Suda Suresh Veera Venkata Naga Raju: Supreme Court Says Police Custody Under Section 187(2) BNSS Can Be Sought in Parts, Not Just in the First 15 Days

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — State of Andhra Pradesh v. Suda Suresh Veera Venkata Naga Raju
BenchJustices Vikram Nath & Sandeep Mehta
Date27 July 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (SC) 722
Key SectionsS. 187(2) & (3), BNSS, 2023 (cf. S. 167, CrPC, 1973) · S. 38, BNSS, 2023 · S. 23, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (cf. S. 27, Evidence Act, 1872)

What the case was about

The case arose out of an alleged custodial death. One Gade Sai Krishna was apprehended by police personnel on 6 May 2026 and allegedly brought to Krishna Lanka Police Station, Vijayawada. According to the prosecution, he was never produced before a Magistrate and subsequently went missing; his body remains untraced to date. Following a complaint lodged by the victim's mother, a Special Investigation Team (SIT) was constituted to probe the matter, and the police officer implicated in the case was arrested on 23 June 2026 and remanded to judicial custody. The investigating agency then sought twelve days of police custody to facilitate further investigation into the officer's alleged role.

The Magistrate granted police custody but attached fifteen stringent conditions to it — among them, that interrogation be confined to the Central Prison, that it be covered by continuous videography, that the accused's advocate be present throughout the interrogation, and that there be a non-extendable outer limit on how long police custody could run. On revision, the Andhra Pradesh High Court retained most of these safeguards with only minor modifications, and cut down the sanctioned police custody to just eight days. The State of Andhra Pradesh then approached the Supreme Court, arguing that these conditions, taken together, would seriously hamper a legitimate custodial investigation into a serving police officer's alleged involvement in a death that occurred nearly 160 kilometres from where he was being held.

What the court held

A bench of Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta, in a judgment authored by Justice Mehta, sided substantially with the State. The Court's central holding was on the structure of police custody under the new criminal law: "Section 187(2) and (3) BNSS, unlike the erstwhile Section 167 under the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 enlarges the window during which police custody, in the aggregate not exceeding fifteen days, may be sought by the investigating agency, such custody being available in parts, during the first forty or sixty days of the total permissible period of detention, rather than being confined to the first fifteen days of remand alone." In other words, the fifteen-day cap on police custody survives under the BNSS, but it is no longer a use-it-in-the-first-fortnight-or-lose-it entitlement — it can be drawn down in instalments across the whole 40- or 60-day detention window, depending on the offence.

The Court reasoned that this change was designed for situations exactly like this one, where fresh facts, discoveries, or disclosure statements leading to recoveries (governed by Section 23 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, the successor to Section 27 of the Evidence Act) might emerge only as the investigation progresses. An "unduly rigid or premature foreclosure" of that statutory window, whether by a Magistrate or a supervisory court, would run counter to the provision's object. The Court accordingly set aside the condition placing an absolute, non-extendable outer limit on custody.

The Court also struck down the condition requiring the accused's advocate to be present throughout interrogation, holding this went beyond what Section 38 BNSS — governing an arrested person's right to consult a legal practitioner — actually guarantees. The Central-Prison-only condition was modified as unworkable, with the SIT given liberty to interrogate the accused at any designated facility of its choosing. Videography was retained, though clarified as not needing to be uninterrupted or cover transit. The SIT was directed to ensure the accused's safety and well-being throughout custody, a pointed reminder given that the underlying case itself concerns an alleged custodial death.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

This is among the first Supreme Court rulings to work through how the "in parts" police custody mechanism under Section 187(2) and (3) BNSS operates, and it will be cited often in remand and bail litigation now that the BNSS governs all offences after 1 July 2024. For prosecutors and investigating agencies in Haryana's district courts, it confirms that a magistrate's grant of an initial tranche of police custody does not exhaust the State's entitlement to seek further custody later in the 40- or 60-day window, provided the aggregate stays within fifteen days and fresh investigative need is shown. For defence counsel, it is double-edged: it forecloses the argument that custody must be confined to the first fifteen days of remand, but confirms that the fifteen-day aggregate itself remains a hard ceiling regardless of how it is split.

The Court's reading of Section 38 BNSS — protecting an arrested person's right to consult a lawyer, not a right to have that lawyer present throughout interrogation — is directly relevant to advocates in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani appearing at remand hearings, since it narrows one line of argument often raised for clients facing custodial interrogation. The judgment's insistence on videography and on the agency's duty to safeguard the accused's well-being still gives defence counsel a basis to press for those specific safeguards.

Statutory cross-references

Section 187(2) and (3) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023 replace Section 167 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, with the key structural change being that the fifteen-day aggregate cap on police custody may now be availed in parts across the first 40 or 60 days of detention, rather than only within the first fifteen days of remand. Section 38 of the BNSS, dealing with an arrested person's right to consult a legal practitioner, corresponds to Section 41-D of the CrPC, 1973 (as inserted in 2010). Section 23 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, governing the admissibility of facts discovered in consequence of information received from an accused in custody, corresponds to Section 27 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872.
