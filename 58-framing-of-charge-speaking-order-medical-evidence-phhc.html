"Inscrutable Face of a Sphinx": P&H High Court Quashes a Murder Charge Framed Without Reasons in a Tarn Taran Land-Dispute Case

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / CasePunjab & Haryana High Court · Petition against charge-framing order of the Additional Sessions Judge, Tarn Taran
BenchJustice N.S. Shekhawat (Single Bench)
DateDecided 1 July 2026 · impugned trial court order dated 10 January 2023
CitationReported by Lawyer eNews, 4 August 2026 · neutral citation not yet available at the time of writing
Key SectionsSs. 227 & 228, CrPC (Ss. 250 & 251, BNSS, 2023) · Ss. 302 & 304, IPC (Ss. 103 & 105, BNS, 2023)

What the case was about

The petitioners had been facing trial before the Additional Sessions Judge, Tarn Taran, on an order dated 10 January 2023 that framed charges against them under Section 302 IPC (murder), and in the alternative under Section 304 IPC (culpable homicide not amounting to murder). The prosecution's case was that the petitioners had strangled the deceased following a dispute over irrigation of adjoining agricultural land. The petitioners' defence was that this was a fabricated case, engineered against the backdrop of a long-running land dispute between the families, and that a civil suit for permanent injunction over the very same land was already pending between them at the time.

What made the trial court's order vulnerable was the medical record. The post-mortem report, followed by an opinion from a Board of Doctors at Government Medical College, Amritsar, recorded "NAD" — no abnormality detected — on the neck of the deceased, and attributed the cause of death to failure of the heart and lungs, pointing towards a natural death rather than strangulation as alleged. A status report filed by the SSP, Tarn Taran also stated there was no incriminating evidence against the petitioners. Despite this, the trial court framed a murder charge relying, in substance, on the oral version of witnesses the petitioners described as already inimical to them because of the pending civil dispute.

What the court held

Justice N.S. Shekhawat held that the trial court had acted mechanically, framing the charge on suspicion and the oral word of interested witnesses without engaging at all with the medical evidence that contradicted the prosecution's version. The Court was emphatic that the ingredients of the specific offence sought to be foisted on an accused — here, murder or culpable homicide — must be shown to be prima facie made out on the material actually on record before cognizance is taken and a charge is framed; a charge cannot rest on suspicion alone.

The heart of the ruling, though, is about the quality of the order itself, not just its conclusion. The Bench held that an order framing charge is a judicial or quasi-judicial act, and like any such act, it must be a "speaking order" that discloses the application of mind. Citing the Supreme Court's observations in State of Punjab v. Bhag Singh on reasons being the "live links" between a decision-maker's mind and the case before it, the Court said that an order without reasons becomes what it memorably called the "inscrutable face of a sphinx" — impossible for a higher court to test on appeal or revision, because there is nothing on the face of the order showing why the material was found sufficient. "Failure to give reasons amounts to denial of justice," the Court observed, adding that reasons are what substitute subjective satisfaction with objective, reviewable reasoning.

Applying this, the Court found that the trial court had never engaged with the contradiction between the strangulation theory and the post-mortem findings, nor with the pending civil litigation that gave the defence a plausible motive-to-falsely-implicate argument, nor with the police's own status report. The charge-framing order and the consequential proceedings dated 10 January 2023 were set aside, and the matter was remanded to the trial court with a direction to pass a fresh, speaking order after actually weighing the witness statements, the medical record, and the other material on file. The High Court was careful to clarify that it had not gone into the merits of the underlying allegations — the trial court remains free to frame the same charge again, provided it does so with reasons this time.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Charge-framing hearings in busy district courts are frequently treated as a formality — a quick reading of the challan followed by a one-line order listing the sections invoked. This judgment is a direct, citable tool against that practice, and it is particularly relevant in rural Haryana practice, where land and irrigation disputes routinely spill over into criminal complaints against the opposing side in a pending civil matter. Defence counsel should treat the charge stage as a genuine opportunity: where the medical or forensic record cuts against the prosecution's narrative, or a status report found nothing incriminating, that material belongs in a discharge application or in submissions opposing charge, and a trial court's failure to address it in a reasoned order is now a clean ground for challenge before the High Court.

For prosecutors, the flip side is equally useful: an order framing charge that is well-reasoned and addresses contrary evidence is far less vulnerable at the revision or quashing stage later, saving years otherwise lost to remands like this one.

Statutory cross-references

The framework for discharge and framing of charge in a sessions case, found in Sections 227 and 228 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, now corresponds to Sections 250 and 251 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, and the entire body of case law on reasoned orders at the charge stage — including the "speaking order" requirement applied here — continues to guide courts under the renumbered provisions. The substantive offences at issue, Section 302 IPC (murder) and Section 304 IPC (culpable homicide not amounting to murder), now correspond to Sections 103 and 105 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 respectively. As the underlying incident and trial in this case predate 1 July 2024, the matter itself continues to be governed by the IPC and CrPC as they stood at the relevant time.
