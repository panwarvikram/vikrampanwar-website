John Mathew v. Santha Paul: Supreme Court Says a Preliminary Issue Cannot Be Carved Out Once Composite Trial Has Begun

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — John Mathew v. Santha Paul & Ors.
BenchJustices K.V. Viswanathan & Arun Palli
Date21 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1020 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 964
Key SectionsOrder XIV Rule 2, CPC, 1908 · Article 59 & Article 65, Limitation Act, 1963

What the case was about

The dispute traced back to a General Power of Attorney the plaintiff had executed in 2005 in favour of one of the defendants, authorising him to manage a piece of immovable property. The plaintiff's case was that he revoked that GPA in 2009, but the attorney-holder went ahead anyway and sold the property to a third defendant without authority, without the plaintiff's consent, and without any real consideration changing hands. In 2013 the plaintiff filed a civil suit seeking a declaration that the sale deed was void and, in the alternative, recovery of possession.

The defendants resisted on more than one front, but the plea that would eventually decide the case at three levels of courts was limitation. They argued that since the suit really sought cancellation of a registered instrument, the three-year period under Article 59 of the Limitation Act applied and had long since expired, rather than the longer period available for a possession suit. All issues in the case, including this limitation issue, were framed together by the trial court back in 2015. Trial then proceeded in the ordinary way: the plaintiff led and closed his evidence, and the defendants began leading theirs. It was only in 2018, with the defendants' evidence underway, that an application was moved asking the court to hive off the limitation issue and decide it first, as a preliminary issue, under Order XIV Rule 2(2) of the Code of Civil Procedure.

The trial court allowed that application, tried the limitation issue in isolation, held the suit time-barred, and dismissed it without ever going into the merits of the GPA revocation or the validity of the sale. The first appellate court affirmed that outcome. It was only when the matter reached the High Court in second appeal that the tables turned: the High Court held the suit was within limitation on the facts pleaded and remitted it for trial on merits. The defendants carried that reversal to the Supreme Court, which is how the timing question — not just the limitation question itself — came to be examined at the highest level.

What the court held

The Supreme Court's judgment is less about whether the suit was, in fact, barred by limitation and much more about when a court is even permitted to ask that question in isolation. Order XIV Rule 2(2) CPC allows a court, notwithstanding sub-rule (1)'s general preference for trying all issues together, to try issues of jurisdiction or a bar to the suit created by any law as preliminary issues, postponing the framing of other issues until after that preliminary issue has been determined. The Court held that this discretion is real, but it is not open-ended, and it does not survive indefinitely once a suit has been set down for a composite trial.

The bench identified, in effect, a working sequence for when Rule 2(2) can be invoked: where only the qualifying issue (jurisdiction or a statutory bar such as limitation) is framed to begin with; where all issues are framed together but the court, at that very stage, directs that the qualifying issue be tried first; or where, after framing, the matter is listed for evidence but evidence has not yet actually begun. What it cannot do, the Court held, is reopen that question once the parties have already been led into a common trial of all the issues generally. In its own words, extracted across the reporting on the judgment, the power to direct that an issue be "tried first" is a power of prospective ordering of the trial, not a licence for retrospective severance of an issue already absorbed into a composite trial that has commenced.

Applied to the facts, that timing rule was fatal to the defendants' application. The issues, including limitation, had been framed together in 2015, and by the time the 2018 application for preliminary trial was filed, the plaintiff had completed his evidence and the defendants had themselves begun leading theirs. On the Court's own sequence, that placed the case in the impermissible category: trial on all issues had already commenced, leaving the trial court no statutory occasion to sever the limitation issue and decide it on a truncated record. Whether the limitation finding was itself correct became secondary to this structural defect in how it was reached, and the Supreme Court left the merits of limitation, and everything else in the suit, to be decided at trial, directing the case to be disposed of within roughly three months of remand.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Applications under Order XIV Rule 2(2) are a routine feature of civil litigation before the Civil Judge and Additional District Judge courts in Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri, and limitation is by far the most common ground on which they are moved. This judgment gives both sides of the bar a clear, citable timing test rather than the loose practice of moving such an application whenever it seems tactically convenient. A defendant who wants a limitation or jurisdiction plea tried first has to move at the framing-of-issues stage, or at the latest before evidence on the general issues has actually begun. Waiting until the plaintiff's evidence is complete, or worse, until one's own evidence is underway, and only then asking the court to carve out a preliminary issue, is now against the grain of this ruling.

For plaintiffs' counsel, the judgment is a shield against exactly the kind of mid-trial ambush that occurred here: a suit contested on merits for years, only to be short-circuited by a limitation plea introduced after the evidentiary record has already substantially formed. It also matters for how issues are argued at the framing stage itself — a party who genuinely believes limitation or jurisdiction will dispose of the suit should press for prior trial of that issue then and there, rather than treating the point as something revivable later. Courts, too, will need to be more disciplined about recording, at the framing stage, whether a preliminary trial is being directed, since silence at the right moment may foreclose the option once evidence gets underway.

Statutory cross-references

This is a civil procedure ruling and does not engage the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita or Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita framework, which govern substantive and procedural criminal law respectively. The governing provisions remain Order XIV Rule 2 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, which continues in its 1976-amended form and has not been altered by any of the recent criminal law reforms, read together with Article 59 (cancellation of instruments) and Article 65 (suits for possession based on title) of the Limitation Act, 1963, both of which remain unchanged.
