Daljitinder Kaur v. Amarinder Singh Brar: P&H High Court Says a Missing Medical Certificate Can't Sink a Plea to Record Evidence by Video Conferencing

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseDaljitinder Kaur @ Daljit Inder Arora v. Amarinder Singh Brar & Ors.
BenchJustice Deepinder Singh Nalwa, Punjab & Haryana High Court
DateOrder dated 1 September 2026
CitationCR-6441-2026 (O&M) · Reported by LiveLaw, 1 September 2026
Key SectionsArts. 225 & 227, Constitution of India · P&H High Court Video Conferencing Rules

What the case was about

A 72-year-old woman, the plaintiff in a civil suit pending before a trial court in Punjab, found herself unable to travel to depose in person. She suffers from arthritis and had been advised a knee replacement. Rather than let her case stall, she moved an application asking the trial court to let her record her evidence through video conferencing instead of appearing physically.

The trial court turned her down. Its reasoning was narrow but, on the face of it, plausible: she had not filed a medical certificate along with her application to substantiate the claim of physical infirmity. No certificate, no proof of infirmity, no case for video conferencing — that was the logic. She carried the order in revision to the Punjab and Haryana High Court under Section 115 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.

On paper, this looks like a small, almost administrative dispute over a scheduling accommodation. In practice, it sits at the intersection of two things that matter a great deal to ordinary litigants: how rigidly a procedural rule can be read to shut a person out of the courtroom, and how far courts in Haryana and Punjab have actually operationalised the video conferencing infrastructure they have had on the books for years.

What the court held

Justice Deepinder Singh Nalwa set aside the trial court's order and restored the petitioner's application for fresh consideration. The core of the ruling is that a medical certificate is evidence a court may ask for and weigh — it is not a jurisdictional precondition without which an application for video conferencing cannot even be entertained.

The High Court anchored its reasoning in the Video Conferencing Rules framed by the Punjab and Haryana High Court itself under Articles 225 and 227 of the Constitution, which govern the High Court's power to make rules regulating practice and procedure in subordinate courts. Those Rules expressly contemplate recording the evidence of a person who, "on account of sickness or physical infirmity," is unable to reach either the Court Point or the Remote Point physically. The Court held: "The mere absence of a medical certificate at the stage of consideration of the application could not be made the basis for rejecting the prayer, particularly when the Rules themselves provide for recording of evidence through video conferencing in appropriate cases of physical infirmity."

Two things stand out in how the Court framed this. First, it treated the Video Conferencing Rules as a genuine procedural safeguard for litigants with real mobility constraints, not a discretionary convenience a trial court could shrink at will. Second, it took care to note that the Rules themselves build in enough protection against misuse — the Court observed that they "contemplate adequate safeguards to ensure the sanctity and authenticity of the proceedings, including verification of identity, administration of oath, recording of objections, preservation of the audio-visual recording and incorporation of the signed transcript on the judicial record." In other words, video conferencing under these Rules is not an unsupervised shortcut; it comes with its own chain of custody for the testimony, which is precisely why a missing certificate at the threshold shouldn't have ended the enquiry.

The Court weighed the petitioner's age and her documented arthritis and knee condition, and directed that she may file a fresh application, along with supporting material, for the trial court to decide afresh "in accordance with law and in the light of the Video Conferencing Rules framed by this Court, particularly the provisions relating to examination of persons through video conferencing, as well as the medical condition and age of the petitioner-plaintiff."

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Every advocate practising in the district courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani has clients who are elderly, unwell, settled abroad, or otherwise genuinely unable to appear physically for evidence — an ageing landowner in a partition suit, an NRI plaintiff in a property dispute, a widow pursuing a succession matter who can no longer travel. Trial courts, understandably cautious about the authenticity of remote testimony, sometimes default to demanding documentary proof before they will even consider a video conferencing request. This judgment tells them that is the wrong sequencing.

The practical takeaway is that an application for video conferencing under the High Court's Rules should be considered on its substance — the nature of the infirmity, the litigant's age, the practicality of travel — rather than dismissed at the door for a missing enclosure. That does not mean medical evidence becomes irrelevant; the Court's own directions make clear the trial court will still weigh "the medical condition and age of the petitioner-plaintiff" when the application is renewed. What changes is that the absence of a certificate at the first instance is a curable gap, not a fatal one.

For drafting practice, the safer course is still to attach whatever medical documentation is available — even an OPD slip, a prescription, or a specialist's advice note — at the time of filing, since that avoids the entire detour through revision. But where a client's application has already been rejected purely for want of a formal certificate, this ruling gives a clean, citable basis to challenge that order rather than start the litigation over from scratch or force an infirm litigant to travel regardless.

It is also a useful reminder that the Video Conferencing Rules exist and have teeth. Many practitioners in the mofussil courts rarely invoke them, defaulting instead to adjournment requests or, worse, letting a case proceed ex parte because an elderly or outstation client simply cannot appear. This judgment is a nudge to treat video conferencing as a genuine, rule-backed alternative rather than a last resort.

Statutory cross-references

This is a Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 matter — the CPC has not been renumbered or replaced by the 2023 criminal law reforms, so Section 115 CPC (revision) and Order XVIII (examination of witnesses) continue to apply unchanged. The underlying evidentiary question of how audio-video electronic records are proved, however, now sits under Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which corresponds to the erstwhile Section 65-B of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, and governs the admissibility of such recordings as secondary evidence.
