Ramkrushna Prahllad Dongardive v. State of Maharashtra: Supreme Court Says No Entrustment, No Criminal Breach of Trust, and Restores a Cashier’s Acquittal After 33 Years

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Ramkrushna Prahllad Dongardive v. State of Maharashtra
BenchJustice Ujjal Bhuyan and Justice Atul S. Chandurkar (judgment authored by Justice Chandurkar)
Date8 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1099 · Criminal Appeal No. 1432 of 2019
Key SectionsS. 405, 409 IPC · S. 100(4), 100(7) CrPC · S. 114(g) Evidence Act

What the case was about

The appellant, Ramkrushna Prahllad Dongardive, was a cashier-cum-counter clerk in the Telephone Department at Aurangabad. Between July and October 1993 he collected telephone bills from subscribers. The department alleged that of roughly ₹8.74 lakh collected, about ₹3.88 lakh reached the Government account late and about ₹4.76 lakh never reached it at all. He was tried for criminal breach of trust by a public servant under Section 409 of the Indian Penal Code.

The Chief Judicial Magistrate acquitted him in 2002, finding the evidence vague, the registers missing and the seizure of papers from his house unproved. The Bombay High Court reversed that acquittal in October 2017, convicted him and gave one year’s rigorous imprisonment with a ₹5,000 fine. The matter reached the Supreme Court more than three decades after the alleged shortfall.

What the Supreme Court held

Allowing the appeal on 8 October 2026, the Court restored the acquittal. The first step in its reasoning was the standard for interfering with an acquittal: an appellate court may re-appreciate evidence, but an acquittal strengthens the presumption of innocence, and the trial court’s view must be given due weight. The High Court, in the Bench’s view, had not done so.

On the offence itself, the Court went back to basics. Criminal breach of trust needs two things proved by the prosecution: that property was entrusted to the accused (or that he had dominion over it), and that he dishonestly misappropriated or used it. Temporary misappropriation can qualify, but only when dishonesty is shown. Here the Bench found that entrustment was never properly established because the original cashbook, daily register and audit records were not produced. The complainant department itself held those records. The trial court was therefore justified in drawing an adverse inference against the prosecution, and the High Court had wrongly expected the accused to produce them.

The seizure evidence fared no better. Both panch witnesses turned hostile, the accused had not signed the seizure memo, and he was not given a list of the articles taken, contrary to Section 100(4) and 100(7) of the CrPC. The Court repeated the settled position that such non-compliance does not by itself make a seizure void, but said it must be weighed with the rest of the record, and in an appeal against acquittal it supported the trial court’s doubt. A document relied on for the shortfall carried no signature or writing of the appellant and nothing tied him to its figures.

The Court also flagged a procedural lapse: the High Court convicted him for the first time in appeal and sentenced him without hearing him on sentence. A court that records a first-time conviction must give the accused that hearing. Since the charge failed, this did not change the result, but the point is worth remembering. Bail bonds were cancelled.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Departmental complaints against cashiers, clerks, patwaris and bank employees are routine in our trial courts, and they often rest on a shortage computed from registers that the department keeps. This ruling gives the defence a clear line: if the prosecution cannot produce the primary records, entrustment is not proved, and the burden does not shift to the accused to explain a shortfall. Cross-examination of the department’s witness should therefore establish, early and on record, which registers existed, who held them and why they were not produced.

For the Section 100 point, defence counsel should check whether a list of seized articles was prepared, signed by witnesses and handed over. Defects alone will not win a case, but combined with hostile panch witnesses they feed directly into reasonable doubt. For appeals against acquittal in the High Court, the judgment is a useful reminder that a second view being possible is not enough to reverse. And where a High Court or Sessions Court convicts in appeal for the first time, counsel should insist on being heard on sentence.

Statutory cross-references

Section 405 IPC (criminal breach of trust) now corresponds to Section 316(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, and Section 409 IPC (by a public servant, banker, merchant or agent) to Section 316(5) BNS. Section 100 CrPC (search of a place) corresponds to Section 103 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. The hearing on sentence after conviction is now under Section 258(2) BNSS (earlier Section 235(2) CrPC), and the adverse-inference presumption for withheld evidence appears in Section 119(1)(g) of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (earlier Section 114(g) of the Evidence Act). The offence here pre-dates the new codes, so IPC and CrPC governed the case itself.
