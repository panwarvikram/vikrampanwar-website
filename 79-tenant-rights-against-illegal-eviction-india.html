Tenant Rights Against Illegal Eviction in India: What a Landlord Can and Cannot Do

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing LawTransfer of Property Act, 1882 · Haryana Urban (Control of Rent & Eviction) Act, 1973 · State Rent Acts
ForumRent Controller / Civil Court of competent jurisdiction
Core PrincipleNo eviction without notice, valid ground, and a court/Rent Controller's order
Self-Help by LandlordIllegal — can attract civil suit for restoration and BNS offences
Key SectionsS. 106, Transfer of Property Act, 1882 · Ss. 329(3), 331(2) & 333, BNS, 2023 · Ss. 13 & 15, Haryana Urban (Control of Rent & Eviction) Act, 1973

What the topic is about

A version of the same complaint turns up regularly at the district courts in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani: a landlord, frustrated by a slow eviction process or simply unwilling to go to court at all, changes the locks while the tenant is out, disconnects the electricity or water connection, or sends men to physically remove the tenant's belongings from a rented shop or house. Tenants often assume that because the landlord owns the property, there is little they can do about it. That assumption is wrong, and correcting it early — ideally before the lockout happens, but certainly right after — is often the most valuable thing an advocate does in these matters.

Indian tenancy law rests on one governing idea: ownership of a property does not carry with it the right to use force, threats, or trickery to recover possession from someone lawfully occupying it. Once a landlord lets a tenant into possession, that possession is protected in law until it is lawfully terminated through notice and, where the tenant does not vacate voluntarily, through an order of eviction from a Rent Controller or civil court. Self-help — padlocks, power cuts, thrown-out furniture — is not a shortcut to that process; it is a separate wrong the tenant can sue and, in appropriate cases, complain criminally about, regardless of how strong the landlord's underlying case for eviction might otherwise be.

What the legal position is

Two layers of law operate on every tenancy in Haryana. The first is the general law of leases under the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, which applies where a tenancy is not otherwise regulated by a special rent statute (typically higher-value or newer commercial lettings, and tenancies outside notified municipal limits). Section 106 fixes the default notice period for terminating a tenancy — fifteen days for a lease of immovable property for agricultural or manufacturing purposes, and the same fifteen days for other leases (residential and commercial) unless the lease deed itself specifies a different, reasonable period — and requires that notice to be in writing, signed, and either served personally, sent by post to the party's address, or affixed to a conspicuous part of the property if direct service is not practicable. A landlord who terminates without a valid notice, or who acts before the notice period has run, has not lawfully ended the tenancy at all; the occupation continues to be that of a tenant, not a trespasser, however the landlord chooses to characterise it.

The second layer, and the one that governs most residential and small commercial tenancies actually litigated in Haryana's district courts, is the Haryana Urban (Control of Rent & Eviction) Act, 1973. It applies to buildings within the notified urban areas of the State and restricts eviction to the specific grounds set out in Section 13 — principally non-payment of rent, subletting without consent, the tenant's own bona fide requirement of an alternative premises, material impairment of the property's value or utility, use for a purpose other than that for which it was let, and the landlord's own bona fide personal necessity (residential or for carrying on business). Crucially, Section 13 requires the landlord to make an application to the Rent Controller and obtain an eviction order; nothing in the Act allows a landlord to short-circuit that process because he believes his ground is obviously made out. Section 15 empowers the Rent Controller to execute an eviction order once passed, again through the machinery of the court rather than the landlord's own hands. Where a tenancy falls outside the Rent Act's coverage — because the building is not within notified limits, or is otherwise excluded — the landlord must instead file a civil suit for possession, on notice, before the ordinary civil court.

What happens when a landlord bypasses all of this? The law treats self-help eviction as an independent wrong on two fronts. Civilly, a tenant forcibly dispossessed without due process can sue for restoration of possession and, depending on the facts, damages for the loss and inconvenience caused — courts have consistently protected even a bare licensee's settled possession against dispossession otherwise than by due process of law, a principle that applies with even greater force to a tenant holding a subsisting tenancy. Criminally, forcibly entering a tenant's premises to dispossess them, or removing their belongings, can attract prosecution for house-trespass and mischief under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (Sections 329 and 331, corresponding to the erstwhile Sections 441/448 and 452 IPC), and criminal intimidation under Section 351 BNS where threats accompany the act, alongside possible liability under Section 33 of the Police Act or local municipal by-laws for illegal disconnection of essential services.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

The practical reality in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani is that a tenant who has just been locked out is panicked, often without immediate access to important documents left inside the premises, and inclined to accept whatever the landlord offers to avoid confrontation. The first and fastest legal step in most such cases is an application for a temporary injunction under Order XXXIX Rules 1 and 2 CPC, restraining the landlord from further interference and, where possession has already been lost, a suit for mandatory injunction or restoration of possession — filed on an urgent basis, since delay is often read (fairly or not) as acquiescence. Advocates should also advise clients to preserve evidence of the tenancy itself (rent receipts, the rent agreement, bank transfer records, utility bills in the tenant's name) and of the manner of dispossession (photographs, an FIR or police complaint filed the same day, and, where possible, independent witnesses), since these disputes are frequently fought on who actually held possession and when it changed hands.

On the landlord side, the counsel's role is often to redirect a frustrated client away from self-help and toward the Rent Controller or civil court, precisely because a botched forcible eviction can convert a strong eviction case into a losing one — a landlord facing a restoration suit or a criminal complaint has effectively handed the tenant a second, independent cause of action, wholly apart from the merits of the original tenancy dispute, and courts do take a dim view of landlords who had a lawful remedy available and chose not to use it.

Statutory cross-references

House-trespass and criminal trespass, previously Sections 441, 448 and 452 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860, now correspond to Sections 329 and 331 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023; mischief causing damage, formerly Section 425/427 IPC, corresponds to Section 324 BNS; and criminal intimidation, formerly Section 503/506 IPC, now corresponds to Sections 351 and 352 BNS. The procedural law governing any criminal complaint arising from a forcible eviction is now the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023, in place of the CrPC, 1973. The Transfer of Property Act, 1882 and the Haryana Urban (Control of Rent & Eviction) Act, 1973 remain unamended by the 2023 criminal law reforms and continue to govern the civil aspects of tenancy termination and eviction.
