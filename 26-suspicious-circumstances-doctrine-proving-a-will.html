Jagwinder Singh v. Ramandeep Khimareet Kaur: P&H High Court Rejects a Will Executed in Favour of a Four-Year-Old, Reaffirms the Suspicious Circumstances Doctrine

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseJagwinder Singh @ Joginder Singh v. Ramandeep Khimareet Kaur & Others · Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Vikram Aggarwal
DateDecided 12 May 2026 (reported 1 July 2026)
CitationRSA No. 2630 of 2025 (O&M)
Key SectionsS. 63, Indian Succession Act, 1925; S. 68, Indian Evidence Act, 1872; Art. 65, Limitation Act, 1963

What the case was about

The dispute concerned 64 Kanal 12 Marlas of agricultural land in Village Dialpura Bhaika, Bathinda, belonging to one Gursewak Singh, who died on 1 June 1992. His daughter, Ramandeep Khimareet Kaur, sued for a declaration that Mutation No. 2833 dated 4 April 1994 — which had transferred the land to her cousin, Jagwinder Singh, on the strength of a Will — was illegal and did not bind her share as a legal heir.

The Will in question was dated 23 February 1984 but was registered only on 11 March 1993, nearly ten months after Gursewak Singh's death. The plaintiff pointed to a string of irregularities: her father was not even in India when the Will was supposedly executed; it surfaced and was registered only after he had died; and, most strikingly, the beneficiary it named, Jagwinder Singh, was only about four years old at the time the document was allegedly signed.

The trial court and the first appellate court both found the Will unproved and decreed the suit in the daughter's favour. Jagwinder Singh carried the matter to the High Court by way of a regular second appeal, also arguing that the 2014 suit — filed twenty years after the 1994 mutation — was time-barred.

What the court held

Justice Vikram Aggarwal reaffirmed the settled rule that the burden of proving a Will always rests on the person who relies on it, and that where a document is surrounded by suspicious circumstances, that burden becomes correspondingly heavier — the propounder must clear every one of those suspicions to the court's satisfaction, not merely produce an attesting witness and call it a day.

The Court then catalogued the suspicious circumstances that Jagwinder Singh had failed to dispel. The attesting witness examined in support of the Will admitted in cross-examination that he was not even on visiting terms with the deceased, undermining his claimed familiarity with the execution. The original Will was never produced; although the trial court had permitted secondary evidence to be led, the conditions attached to that permission were not fulfilled, and no explanation was offered for the original document's disappearance. The registration itself, occurring nearly ten months after the testator's death, was found impossible to reconcile with a defence witness's claim that the deceased had personally appeared before the registering authority. And the stated justification for the bequest — that the beneficiary had "taken care of" the testator — collapsed once the evidence showed the beneficiary was a toddler at the relevant time, incapable of rendering any such service. The Will's silence on the deceased's daughters, while purporting to exclude natural heirs in favour of a four-year-old, compounded the suspicion.

On limitation, the Court rejected the argument that the suit was barred by the two-decade gap between the 1994 mutation and the 2014 suit. It held that the cause of action arises when the plaintiff acquires knowledge of the disputed document and mutation, not from the date of the mutation itself, and that a suit of this nature — effectively for possession and declaration flowing from the invalid Will — is governed by the twelve-year limitation period, making the 2014 suit maintainable. Finding no misreading of evidence or error of law by the courts below, the High Court dismissed the second appeal and upheld the decree invalidating the Will.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Testamentary disputes over agricultural land are a staple of civil litigation in Haryana's district courts, and this judgment is a compact, practical restatement of the suspicious circumstances doctrine first laid down by the Supreme Court in H. Venkatachala Iyengar v. B.N. Thimmajamma and applied in countless cases since. For counsel drafting a plaint or written statement in a Will dispute, the judgment offers a checklist of the kind of facts that a court will treat as red flags: a beneficiary incapable, by age or circumstance, of having earned the bequest as claimed; delayed registration, particularly registration occurring after the testator's death; the propounder's inability to produce the original document or satisfy the conditions for secondary evidence; and an attesting witness whose testimony does not withstand cross-examination on basic facts of acquaintance and presence.

The limitation holding is equally significant for practice. Landowners and their heirs in Haryana frequently discover a disputed mutation or Will years after it was recorded, and this judgment confirms that the limitation clock for a suit challenging such a document runs from knowledge, not from the date of the instrument, and that the twelve-year period for possession-linked suits under the Limitation Act applies rather than a shorter period. This gives practitioners a clear basis to resist a limitation objection in Order VII Rule 11 CPC applications where a client's suit follows years after the impugned mutation, provided the plaint properly pleads the date of knowledge.

Statutory cross-references

The substantive law on execution of Wills continues to sit in the Indian Succession Act, 1925, which has not been renumbered or replaced by the 2023 criminal-law recodification. Section 63 of that Act, requiring attestation by two or more witnesses, remains the governing provision. The evidentiary requirement to examine at least one attesting witness, previously Section 68 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, now corresponds to Section 66 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which came into force on 1 July 2024 and applies to evidence led in trials conducted thereafter. The twelve-year limitation period for suits relating to possession of immovable property continues under Article 65 of the Limitation Act, 1963, which is unaffected by the new criminal codes.
