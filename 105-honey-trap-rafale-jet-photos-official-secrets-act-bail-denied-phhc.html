Being "Honey Trapped" Is No Excuse: P&H High Court Denies Bail Over Rafale Jet Photos Sent to a Pakistan-Linked Contact

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — bail petition arising from FIR under the Official Secrets Act & S. 152, BNS (identity of petitioner withheld in public reporting given the nature of the case)
BenchJustice Vikram Aggarwal
DateOrder dated on or about 10 September 2026; reported 11 September 2026
Citation2026 LL (PH) 326
Key SectionsSs. 3, 4, 5 & 13, Official Secrets Act, 1923 · S. 152, BNS, 2023

What the case was about

The petitioner, employed in a supervisory capacity with access to the Air Force Station at Ambala Cantt, was arrested on 2 January 2026 after secret information led investigators to him. On examining his phone, agencies found that he had been in contact with a woman going by the name Avni, through three mobile numbers traced back to Pakistan, and that over the course of this contact he had shared photographs of the Air Force Station, the surrounding Army area, and Rafale fighter jets stationed there. Forensic examination of the device at the DITAC Lab also showed that a number of these photographs had since been deleted. An FIR followed under Section 152 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, which criminalises acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India, along with Sections 3, 4 and 5 of the Official Secrets Act, 1923. Investigation concluded and a final report was filed, at which point the petitioner moved the High Court for regular bail.

His counsel's case rested on three limbs. First, that the arrest proceeded purely on "secret information" without any complaint from the Defence Authorities, and that the mandatory sanction for prosecution under Section 13 of the Official Secrets Act had not even been obtained at the time of arrest. Second, that the Rafale photographs were nothing exotic — the aircraft's images were freely available on Google, so nothing genuinely "secret" had changed hands. Third, and most strikingly, that the petitioner himself was the victim here: a man lured into an online relationship by an operative using a fabricated persona, with no independent motive of his own to compromise national security. With investigation complete, the chargesheet filed, and thirteen prosecution witnesses yet to be examined, his counsel argued that continued incarceration served no further purpose, and relied on a trio of coordinate Bench rulings granting bail in comparable circumstances.

What the court held

Justice Vikram Aggarwal was unpersuaded, and the language of the order is unusually direct for a bail matter. The Court accepted, for the sake of argument, that the petitioner may genuinely have been drawn into a honey trap — but treated that as beside the point. Being deceived into a relationship, the Court held, does not licence what a person chooses to do once inside it. The petitioner had access to a Rafale-equipped Air Force Station in his official capacity, and having chosen to photograph and transmit images of that station and its aircraft to an unknown contact operating from across the border, "the investigating agencies were justified in treating it as a case of spying," whatever the eventual trial might reveal about his personal culpability. The order pointedly separates two questions that the defence had tried to fuse into one: whether the petitioner was a victim of romantic manipulation, and whether what he then did with sensitive material was itself lawful. The Court answered only the second question, and answered it against him.

On the "freely available on Google" argument, the Court did not accept that publicly circulating images of a fighter aircraft neutralise the seriousness of photographing and transmitting images of the specific station, its layout, and its surrounding army area to a foreign-linked contact — the offence lies in the act of covert transmission to a hostile-linked recipient from a position of authorised access, not merely in the informational novelty of the image itself. The Court also treated the deletion of photographs revealed by forensic examination as a factor cutting against the petitioner, consistent with consciousness of guilt rather than innocent sharing.

On the procedural objections, the Court noted that the status report on record showed the requisite sanction under the Official Secrets Act had since been obtained, and that this development, far from helping the petitioner, made the matter "even more serious" since prosecution could now proceed on solid procedural footing. On the argument that nine months in custody and a completed investigation should by themselves justify bail, the Court held that mere completion of investigation and this length of custody did not automatically entitle an accused to bail, distinguishing the coordinate Bench precedents relied upon as arising on entirely different facts, and reiterating the well-settled position that bail matters admit of no straitjacket formula. The petition was dismissed as devoid of merit, with the Court clarifying that its observations were not to be read as a finding on the ultimate merits of the case, which remain for trial.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Ambala Cantt sits well within this High Court's ordinary bail jurisdiction, and Haryana's belt of cantonment towns and border-adjacent districts means Official Secrets Act matters, though rare compared to the everyday NDPS or cheque-bounce docket, are not hypothetical for practitioners here. The order is a useful marker for how this Bench is likely to approach any future bail application where an accused's defence concedes the underlying act (sharing sensitive material) but seeks to explain away intent through personal victimhood, whether that is a honey trap, coercion, or claimed ignorance of a document's sensitivity. The lesson for defence counsel is that a "victim of deception" narrative, however sympathetic, needs to be paired with a genuine dispute over the act itself or the absence of any transmission of sensitive material — conceding the transmission while blaming the manipulation tends to read, on this Bench's reasoning, as an admission that leaves only intent contested, and intent is squarely a matter for trial, not bail.

The order is also a reminder to counsel handling Official Secrets Act matters generally that the sanction requirement under Section 13 is a live issue worth checking at the very first hearing — here, its absence at the time of arrest was raised but ultimately overtaken by the prosecution curing the defect during the pendency of the petition, which the Court treated as strengthening rather than weakening the case against bail. Where sanction genuinely remains missing through to the bail hearing, that argument retains real force and should be pressed independently of any merits-based defence, since a prosecution lacking mandatory sanction may be vulnerable at a later stage regardless of the bail outcome.

Statutory cross-references

Section 152 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 is the successor provision addressing acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India, occupying territory previously covered in substantial part by Section 124-A IPC (sedition) before its removal, and by residual provisions on acts prejudicial to national security; the Official Secrets Act, 1923 itself remains unamended and continues to operate in parallel with the BNS as a standalone special statute, with Sections 3 to 5 covering espionage and wrongful communication of sensitive information, and Section 13 governing the sanction required before a competent authority can launch prosecution under the Act.
