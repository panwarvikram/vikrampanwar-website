Alladin Charities v. Mohammed Saleem (2026 INSC 1122): Supreme Court Says an Executing Court Must Hold a Full Inquiry Into a Third Party’s Title Under Order XXI Rule 97 CPC

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Alladin Charities and Zakat Wakf v. Mohammed Saleem @ Mohd. Wajid Ali & Anr.
BenchJustice S.V.N. Bhatti and Justice N.V. Anjaria
Date14 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1122; 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 1056
Key SectionsOrder XXI Rules 35, 58, 97, 99 and 101 CPC

What the case was about

A buyer obtained an ex parte decree in 2015 for specific performance of an agreement to sell about 373 square yards of shop property in Hyderabad, together with possession. When the decree-holder went to execute it, a Waqf trust stepped in and resisted delivery of possession. It said the shops belonged to the Waqf, tracing its title to a Nizam-era Farman, a Muntakhab, a 1976 trust deed and proceedings under the Waqf Act, 1995.

The Executing Court rejected the trust’s objection. Its first reasoning was procedural: the objector had invoked Order XXI Rule 58 CPC, which deals with claims against attachment, whereas the decree sought delivery of possession. Its second was factual: the trust had not proved that the property was notified Waqf property. The Telangana High Court accepted that the application should really have been under Order XXI Rule 97, but still dismissed the appeal because it found too little material to link the disputed municipal numbers to the Waqf’s land. The trust came to the Supreme Court.

What the Court held

The Bench of Justice S.V.N. Bhatti and Justice N.V. Anjaria allowed the appeal on 14 October 2026, set aside both orders and sent the matter back to the VII Senior Civil Judge, City Civil Court, Hyderabad. It did not decide who owns the shops. It held that an objection of this kind has to be tried properly, with a full inquiry, and not disposed of on a thin reading of the record.

The judgment leans on Periyammal v. V. Rajamani (2025) 9 SCC 568 and restates the working scheme of Order XXI in four points. First, Rule 35 lets the Executing Court remove a person who is bound by the decree and refuses to vacate. Second, Rules 97 to 101 cover obstruction by “any person”, which includes a stranger asserting an independent right, title or interest. Third, Rule 97 operates before possession is delivered, while Rule 99 is for someone who has already been dispossessed and wants restoration. Fourth, where a purported stranger resists, the decree-holder’s remedy is Rule 97. The decree-holder cannot sidestep the objection by asking again and again for a fresh possession warrant under Rule 35.

Once a Rule 97 application is before the court, Rule 101 requires it to decide all questions of right, title or interest between the parties, not merely whether the application was neatly labelled. The Court also noted that some findings below did not conform to settled principles, and gave both sides liberty to file fresh pleadings and lead evidence. No costs were awarded.

The wrong-section point

The appellant argued that quoting the wrong provision is no ground to reject a third party’s objection. The Court’s order does not turn into a long discussion of this, but the direction to hold a fresh merits inquiry means the dismissal did not survive. For practitioners the safe reading is that the label on the application should not decide the fate of a genuine claim, though it remains good practice to cite Rule 97 correctly.

Why this matters for civil practitioners in Haryana

Execution is where decrees in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani land-possession suits often stall, and obstruction by a third party is the commonest reason. This ruling cuts both ways. For a judgment-debtor or an outsider, it is a reminder that a genuine claim of independent title must be heard in a full inquiry and that the burden is not to be casually put on the objector. For a decree-holder, it closes the shortcut: if someone claims to be a stranger, file under Rule 97, expect a trial-like inquiry with evidence, and do not keep seeking warrants under Rule 35.

Practically, objectors should file their application under Rule 97 with their title documents and revenue or municipal records identifying the exact property, since identity of the property was central here. Decree-holders should lead evidence on identity and on whether the objector is truly a stranger. Executing courts should frame issues and record evidence rather than decide on affidavits alone.

Statutory cross-references

The provisions involved are in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: Order XXI Rules 35, 58, 97, 99 and 101. The CPC has not been replaced by the BNSS or BNS, so there is no new-code equivalent to map. The Waqf Act, 1995 was referred to only as the basis of the trust’s claim and was not construed.
