Conspiracy to Murder Inside Court Premises Is Terror, Not Just Crime: P&H High Court Denies UAPA Bail

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — regular bail plea by a man accused of harbouring and financially aiding members of designated terrorist Goldy Brar's network
BenchJustice Deepak Sibal & Justice Lapita Banerji
DateReported 17 July 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 237
Key SectionsUnlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 — bail provisions applicable to terrorist-act and conspiracy offences

What the case was about

The Punjab & Haryana High Court has refused regular bail to a man accused of sheltering and funding members of the network run by Goldy Brar — a gangster formally designated a terrorist under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 (UAPA), and named by Indian and international agencies in connection with organised crime and cross-border extortion rackets. The specific allegation against this accused was that he provided harbour and financial assistance to associates of the network who were, in turn, part of a conspiracy to murder a rival gangster inside court premises — that is, within the physical building where judicial proceedings are conducted.

The location of the intended killing is what gives this case its edge. Court premises are meant to be among the most secure public spaces in the country, protected precisely because they host the daily business of justice: undertrials being produced, witnesses testifying, lawyers and litigants moving freely between courtrooms. A plot to execute a rival inside that space is not simply an isolated act of gang violence; it is an assault on the functioning of the justice system itself, and on the basic assurance that people can attend court without fear for their lives.

The accused sought regular bail, arguing the case against him rested on secondary allegations — harbouring and financing others rather than being a direct conspirator or shooter. The High Court had to decide whether that distance from the core conspiracy was enough to entitle him to bail under the stringent standards UAPA prescribes for terrorism-linked offences.

What the court held

The bench of Justice Deepak Sibal and Justice Lapita Banerji rejected the bail plea, holding that a conspiracy to kill a person inside the "Temple of Justice" itself reflects an intent to strike terror into the public at large. The Court's underlying reasoning, as reported, was direct: if a person is not secure inside court premises, then citizens generally would naturally be terrorised. That reasoning treats the choice of location as doing real legal work — it is not incidental to the offence but central to why the conduct meets the threshold of a "terrorist act" rather than an ordinary murder conspiracy.

This matters because UAPA bail operates under a materially higher bar than ordinary criminal bail. Under the scheme of the Act, courts assessing bail must consider whether, on the material available, there are reasonable grounds for believing the accusation against the person is "prima facie true" — and if the court cannot record that the accusation is not prima facie true, bail must be refused. That reverses the ordinary presumption of liberty that governs most bail applications, and it explains why courts examine the nature of the underlying conduct so closely: whether it is calculated to spread terror, intimidate the public, or threaten the security of the State, as opposed to being an act of private vengeance dressed up in serious language.

By finding that a plot to kill inside a courthouse independently supports an inference of intent to terrorise the public, the High Court effectively held that the venue of an intended killing can itself elevate a conspiracy into an act that meets the UAPA threshold — separate from and in addition to any organisational links to a designated terrorist network. That is a significant doctrinal point for future UAPA bail litigation arising out of targeted-killing conspiracies linked to organised crime syndicates operating out of India and abroad.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Punjab and Haryana have both seen a steady stream of UAPA prosecutions tied to transnational gangster networks, gang rivalries that spill into targeted killings, and extortion rackets run out of foreign jurisdictions. Defence counsel handling bail applications in such matters need to understand that courts are increasingly willing to treat the symbolic and practical significance of a crime scene — a courthouse, a public event, a religious site — as independent evidence bearing on intent to terrorise, not merely as an aggravating sentencing factor after conviction.

For counsel representing accused persons whose role is peripheral — harbouring, financing, or logistical support rather than direct participation in violence — this ruling is a reminder that UAPA's reach extends well beyond the principal conspirators. Sections of the Act dealing with raising funds for terrorist acts and harbouring persons who have committed such acts, or are likely to, can pull in accused persons who never handled a weapon. Any bail strategy built on "I was only on the periphery" needs to grapple squarely with how courts are reading these provisions, and with the reversed burden UAPA bail carries.

Prosecutors and investigating agencies, for their part, will likely cite this order when the location of an intended or actual act of violence has any public or institutional significance — courts, police stations, government offices, places of worship — to argue that the choice of venue alone supports a terrorism charge rather than an ordinary offence against the person.

Statutory cross-references

The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 remains a standalone special statute and has not been merged into the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023; it continues to operate independently alongside the new criminal codes. Where an accused also faces conspiracy or murder charges under the general criminal law arising from the same facts, the relevant provisions now fall under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 — conspiracy to commit an offence under Section 61 BNS (corresponding to the erstwhile Section 120B IPC), and murder under Section 103 BNS (corresponding to the erstwhile Section 302 IPC). Bail applications in cases involving both UAPA and BNS charges typically require the accused to separately satisfy the bail thresholds under each statute, with the UAPA bar generally being the more difficult one to cross.
