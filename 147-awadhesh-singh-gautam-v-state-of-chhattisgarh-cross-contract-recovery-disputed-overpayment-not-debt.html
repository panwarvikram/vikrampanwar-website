M/s Awadhesh Singh Gautam v. State of Chhattisgarh (2026 INSC 1072): Supreme Court Says a Disputed Overpayment Claim Is Not a Debt and Cannot Be Recovered From a Contractor's Other Running Bills

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — M/s Awadhesh Singh Gautam v. State of Chhattisgarh & Ors.
BenchJustice P.S. Narasimha and Justice Alok Aradhe
Date30 September 2026 (as per the weekly judgment listing)
Citation2026 INSC 1072
Key SectionsArticle 226 and Article 14 of the Constitution; Clause 53.1(ii) of the General Conditions of Contract; Section 73, Indian Contract Act, 1872 (damages); principles of natural justice

What the case was about

A partnership firm had executed road works for the Chhattisgarh Rural Road Development Agency under the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana. Complaints about its earlier works led the Collector of South Bastar (Dantewada) to appoint a five-member committee. The committee compared the money paid with the work actually measured and reported an excess payment of ₹2,01,06,117 (payments of about ₹3.56 crore against work worth about ₹1.55 crore). It recommended recovery, blacklisting and action against the agency's own officers.

The first attempt to recover the money, a revenue-recovery demand notice under Section 146 of the Chhattisgarh Land Revenue Code, was quashed by the High Court. An FIR for cheating, forgery and breach of trust was registered against the firm's partner and agency officers. The department then tried a different route: the Executive Engineer passed an order blocking ₹84,17,003 from sums currently payable to the firm under three separate, ongoing "subsequent works", to be adjusted against the earlier excess payment. The firm challenged this; the High Court dismissed its writ petitions, reasoning that the matter turned on disputed facts and the firm could pursue alternative remedies.

What the Court held

The Supreme Court allowed the appeals and quashed the recovery order. The ruling rests on two connected points.

First, the contract did not authorise it. The State relied on the cross-contract recovery clause, Clause 53.1(ii) of the General Conditions of Contract. The Court read that clause as operating only where a contract has been terminated for the contractor's fundamental breach of its defects-liability and maintenance obligations, and even then only to recover the excess cost of rectification over that same contract's security deposit and performance security. Nothing on record showed the earlier or the subsequent works had been terminated. The allegation here was manipulated measurement during execution, a different thing from post-completion maintenance default. The Integrity Pact clause was also not invoked in the order and no breach had been found.

Second, an unproved overpayment is not a debt. Until the overpayment is established in accordance with law, the Court said, the claim is a disputed and unadjudicated claim for damages, not a debt presently due. A party cannot treat such a claim as an ascertained sum and appropriate money payable under a wholly separate contract. The order was also passed without notice, consent or sanction, which offended natural justice.

The Court also corrected the High Court's approach. The real question was not a disputed question of fact; it was whether the State could, in law and under the contract, appropriate disputed sums from amounts admittedly payable under unrelated contracts. That is a legal question a writ court can and should answer. The State was directed to release the ₹84,17,003 with interest at 6% per annum from 27 September 2025 until payment.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Government contractors in Haryana, from PWD and Panchayati Raj road works to HSVP and irrigation contracts, often find that a department withholds money from one bill to square an alleged excess on another contract. Awadhesh Singh Gautam gives contractors a precise checklist for challenging this: identify the exact clause relied on, show that its pre-conditions (termination, breach of a particular obligation) are not met, and argue that the claim is merely an allegation of damages until adjudicated.

It also helps in recovery suits and execution matters. A claim that has not been crystallised through arbitration, a civil suit or a statutory determination cannot be set off unilaterally against money admittedly due. Departments will need to follow their contract's dispute mechanism, issue notice and hear the contractor before holding back sums. For private parties too, the principle that an allegation of overpayment or damages is not an ascertained debt is useful when resisting unilateral deductions.

A word of caution: the ruling is confined to the clauses and facts before the Court. A contract with a clear, wide set-off or lien clause, properly invoked after notice, may stand on different footing. Practitioners should read the contract's recovery and dispute-resolution clauses closely before advising either side, and should not assume that every writ will succeed where facts are truly contested; the point of distinction here was that the legal question could be decided on the face of the contract and the order.

Statutory cross-references

The writ jurisdiction invoked is Article 226 of the Constitution, and the arbitrary-State-action argument sits under Article 14. Damages for breach are governed by Section 73 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, which remains unchanged. The parallel FIR in the background was registered under IPC ss. 420, 467, 468, 471, 409 and 120-B; their BNS 2023 counterparts are ss. 318(4), 338, 336(3), 340(2), 316(5) and 61(2) respectively. The revenue-recovery route referred to is s. 146 of the Chhattisgarh Land Revenue Code, 1959; readers in Haryana should check which local recovery statute, if any, a department invokes.
