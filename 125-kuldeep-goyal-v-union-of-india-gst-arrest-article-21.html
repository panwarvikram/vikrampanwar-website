Kuldeep Goyal v. Union of India: P&H High Court Calls a GST Arrest Timed Around a Court Hearing a "Brazen Act of Highhandedness"

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — Kuldeep Goyal v. Union of India & Ors.
BenchChief Justice Ashwani Kumar Mishra & Justice Rohit Kapoor
Date25 September 2026
CitationCWP-31964-2026 (O&M) · 2026 TAXSCAN (HC) 1598
Key SectionsArticle 21, Constitution of India · Ss. 69 & 70, CGST Act, 2017

What the case was about

Kuldeep Goyal runs Ansh Steel Alloys out of Ludhiana. The CGST department accuses the firm of claiming roughly ₹35 crore in fraudulent Input Tax Credit through fake invoices — a serious allegation, and one Goyal disputes. Back in May 2026 he moved the Punjab & Haryana High Court in a separate writ petition, CWP-18184-2026, challenging the department's decision to attach his firm's bank account without first issuing a show-cause notice. That petition did not ask the Court to shield him from the investigation; it simply asked the department to follow its own procedure before freezing his working capital.

The case proceeded on what looked like an entirely cooperative track. On 22 September 2026, the Court recorded an undertaking from Goyal's counsel that he would personally appear before the Adjudicating Authority the next morning, 23 September, at 11 a.m. The Bench, satisfied with that assurance, posted the writ petition for a status report on 29 September — giving the department a week to report back on how the appearance had gone. Goyal kept his word. He appeared as promised, at the time promised.

What the court held

What happened next is the reason this order is being read well beyond Ludhiana. Instead of treating the 11 a.m. appearance as compliance, the department issued a fresh summons under Section 70 of the CGST Act fixing a fresh appearance for 12:05 p.m. the same day — after Goyal had already appeared. The Digital Identification Number generated for that very summons was time-stamped 12:07 p.m., two minutes after the appearance time the summons itself recited. A summons cannot be numbered after the appointment it is fixing has already notionally passed; the Bench termed this "prima-facie manipulation" of departmental records. Within hours, an arrest was authorised, and the record shows Goyal taken into custody at 4:45 a.m. on 24 September 2026 — five days before the status report the department itself had agreed to file.

Hearing the fresh writ petition Goyal filed from custody (CWP-31964-2026), Chief Justice Ashwani Kumar Mishra and Justice Rohit Kapoor did not treat the timing as coincidence. An arrest engineered around a pending status report, built on a summons whose own paperwork could not have existed at the time it claims to, was, in the Bench's words, a "brazen act of highhandedness" and prima facie a violation of Article 21 of the Constitution. The Court directed the Chief Judicial Magistrate, Ludhiana, to release Goyal forthwith, subject to him surrendering his passport, not travelling abroad without the Court's leave, and keeping the local police informed of his whereabouts. It did not stop at personal liberty. The Principal Commissioner of the CGST Commissionerate, Ludhiana, and the Senior Intelligence Officer who signed off on the summons were impleaded as respondents in their personal capacity, and put on notice to explain the manipulation before the Court records any disciplinary finding against them. Both writ petitions now stand listed together for 29 September 2026 — the very date the department had originally promised to report back.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

GST enforcement runs on its own arrest and summons machinery, separate from the ordinary criminal process, but this order sits squarely inside the arrest-safeguards line this blog has tracked through the Sandeep Kumar v. Pankaj Nain matters on Section 41-A notices. The DIN-timestamp mismatch here is, in substance, the tax-enforcement equivalent of an antedated FIR — a record that claims to have been created before the event it documents. For counsel resisting coercive tax action, three practice points follow. First, get every undertaking about a client's cooperation recorded in the court's own order rather than left to correspondence; it was that recorded undertaking, and the specific 29 September deadline built around it, that let the Bench see the arrest for what it was. Second, when contesting an arrest under Sections 69 or 70 of the CGST Act, always pull the DIN generation log alongside the summons date and time — it is now judicially recognised as verifiable, tamper-evident metadata that can expose a fabricated sequence exactly as it did here. Third, personal-capacity impleadment of the officer who signed the summons or authorised the arrest is an available and increasingly used remedy where liberty has been curtailed through engineered process, not simply an ultra vires exercise of statutory power.

For district court practice in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, where GST search-and-seizure actions against local traders and transporters are not uncommon, the broader lesson is that a pending writ petition and a recorded undertaking do not, by themselves, put a client beyond departmental reach. What protects a client is a High Court actively watching the file — and a record clean enough that any deviation from what was promised shows up immediately, exactly as it did in Goyal's DIN log.

Statutory cross-references

The CGST Act, 2017 runs a self-contained arrest and summons code rather than borrowing wholesale from the general criminal law — Sections 69 and 70 are the relevant provisions here, not any BNS or BNSS offence-creating section. But Section 69(3) of the CGST Act still anchors bail and remand to "the provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 relating to bail," and after the 2023 recodification that cross-reference is read, through the repeal-and-savings clause in Section 531(1) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS), as pointing to the BNSS's corresponding provisions — broadly Sections 478 to 496 BNSS on bail, and Sections 35 to 62 BNSS on arrest procedure, including the Section 35 notice-before-arrest safeguard this blog has discussed at length in the Sandeep Kumar series. Article 21's due-process content — written and honest reasons, contemporaneous documentation, no arrest engineered around pending court process — applies with equal force whether the arrest arises under the CGST Act's own scheme or under an ordinary BNS/BNSS prosecution, which is the constitutional thread connecting a tax arrest like this one to any other arrest this blog covers.
