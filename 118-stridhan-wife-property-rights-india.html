Stridhan: What Counts as a Wife's Absolute Property, and How to Recover It

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Leading CasesPratibha Rani v. Suraj Kumar & Anr.; Rashmi Kumar v. Mahesh Kumar Bhada; Krishna Bhattacharjee v. Sarathi Choudhury & Anr.
CourtSupreme Court of India
Date1985 – 2015 (doctrine remains current law)
CitationAIR 1985 SC 628 · (1997) 2 SCC 397 · (2016) 2 SCC 705
Key SectionsS. 316, BNS, 2023 (corresponding to Ss. 405 & 406, IPC) · Ss. 12, 18 & 19, Domestic Violence Act, 2005 · S. 27, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955

What "stridhan" actually covers

Stridhan, literally a woman's wealth, is a distinct legal category under Hindu law, and confusing it with joint family property or ordinary "dowry" is where most disputes go wrong from the outset. It covers everything gifted to a woman before, at, or after her marriage — jewellery, cash, clothes, household articles and immovable property — given by her parents, relatives, friends, or her husband and his family, as well as anything she earns or acquires through her own skill or effort during the marriage. What it does not cover is property that genuinely belongs to the joint Hindu family or to the husband alone, and the distinction matters enormously in practice: gifts made specifically to the couple jointly, or property purchased in the husband's name with his own funds, do not automatically become stridhan merely because the wife had access to or use of them. The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 criminalises the demand and giving of dowry as a condition of marriage, but genuine, voluntary gifts made out of love and affection at the time of marriage are not dowry in that prohibited sense — they are stridhan, and the two statutes serve entirely different purposes. Establishing what was actually given, and by whom, is almost always the first and hardest evidentiary battle in a stridhan claim, which is why a careful list of articles, ideally corroborated by photographs, purchase bills, or witnesses present at the time of the gift, matters far more than most clients initially realise.

What the law holds: ownership, and how to recover it

The Supreme Court settled the ownership question decisively in Pratibha Rani v. Suraj Kumar & Anr., holding that stridhan remains a woman's absolute property throughout her marriage; she can deal with it exactly as she pleases, and neither her husband nor his relatives acquire any independent title over it merely because it was handed to them for safekeeping at the time of the wedding. The husband, at most, holds it as a trustee or custodian, not an owner. A decade later, in Rashmi Kumar v. Mahesh Kumar Bhada, a three-judge bench reaffirmed this position and went further: a husband's dishonest refusal to return his wife's stridhan on demand amounts to criminal breach of trust, triable under what was then Section 406 of the Indian Penal Code, and a wife is free to pursue a criminal complaint for that offence quite independently of any civil suit for recovery. On the question of when a stridhan claim becomes time-barred, Krishna Bhattacharjee v. Sarathi Choudhury & Anr. held that wrongful retention of stridhan is a continuing wrong under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 — the deprivation renews itself for as long as the property is withheld, so a claim under the Act cannot be thrown out merely because years have passed since the couple separated. In practical terms, a wife today has three routes open to her, often pursued together: a criminal complaint for breach of trust; an application under Sections 12, 18 and 19 of the DV Act before a Magistrate, which can direct return of stridhan as an interim or final relief without the wife having to file a fresh civil suit; and, where a matrimonial petition is already pending, a claim for return of stridhan articles as part of that proceeding under Section 27 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, which allows the court to make orders regarding property presented at or about the time of marriage that belongs jointly to both spouses or, on the facts, is shown to belong to the wife alone.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Stridhan disputes are a near-constant feature of matrimonial litigation in Haryana's district courts, almost always arriving alongside a Section 498A complaint, a maintenance petition, or a DV Act application once a marriage has broken down, and the outcome usually turns on documentation prepared long before the dispute ever reaches a courtroom. Counsel drafting the initial complaint or DV Act application should press clients hard for specifics — an itemised list with approximate weight and value for jewellery, the source of each gift, and any surviving proof such as bills, bank withdrawal records for cash gifts, or wedding photographs showing the articles — because vague, omnibus claims of "gold ornaments and cash" invite exactly the scrutiny that sinks them at trial. On the defence side, the burden is squarely on the husband and his family to show either that the disputed items were never entrusted to them at all, or that they were returned, since Pratibha Rani and Rashmi Kumar place the practical onus of explaining non-return on whoever held the property last. Given Krishna Bhattacharjee, limitation is rarely a winning defence in a DV Act stridhan claim, so defending counsel should focus instead on disputing entrustment and valuation rather than delay.

Statutory cross-references

Criminal breach of trust over stridhan, tried under Sections 405 and 406 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 in the cases discussed above, now corresponds to Section 316 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 for conduct occurring on or after 1 July 2024, carrying the same core elements of entrustment and dishonest misappropriation. The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961, the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and Section 27 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 are unaffected by the 2023 criminal law recodification and continue to apply exactly as before.
