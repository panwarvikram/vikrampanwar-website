Mihir Prakash Bajoria v. Vidushi Jain Bajoria: Supreme Court Says an Anti-Suit Injunction Can't Rest on a Guess That a UK Divorce Decree Will Be Unenforceable

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseMihir Prakash Bajoria v. Vidushi Jain Bajoria
BenchJustices S.V.N. Bhatti & N.V. Anjaria
Date22 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1034 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 979
Key SectionsS. 13, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 · Doctrine of anti-suit injunction & forum non conveniens · S. 13, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955

What the case was about

The parties married in Kolkata on 15 December 2018 under Hindu rites and moved almost immediately to London, where they lived together at a flat on Academy Gardens between 2018 and 2020. In March 2022 the husband, who had grown up in the UK from the age of fourteen or fifteen, arranged a spousal visa for the wife. The couple returned to India in October 2023, and the marriage broke down within weeks, with the parties separating in November 2023.

What followed was a race between two jurisdictions. On 4 September 2024 the husband filed a divorce petition before the Alipore Family Court in West Bengal, pleading irretrievable breakdown of marriage — a ground the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 does not itself recognise, though courts sometimes invoke it under their inherent powers. Five weeks later, on 10 October 2024, the wife filed her own divorce petition in a UK Family Court, along with a separate claim for ancillary financial relief. Each spouse was now pursuing a parallel case in a different country, and the husband moved to stop the second one.

The Alipore trial court granted him an ex-parte anti-suit injunction on 1 November 2025, restraining the wife from continuing her UK proceedings. Its reasoning was that irretrievable breakdown is not a recognised ground for divorce in India, so any decree the UK court eventually passed on that basis would run into the bar on foreign judgments under Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, and would simply be unenforceable here. On appeal, the Calcutta High Court took the opposite view. On 15 December 2025 it set aside the injunction, holding that the couple's established matrimonial life in London made the UK the more appropriate forum, and that restraining a foreign suit at such an early, undecided stage offended the comity that Indian courts owe to other sovereign courts. The husband carried the fight to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The Supreme Court dismissed the husband's appeal and upheld the Calcutta High Court's order. Writing for the bench, the Court treated the case as an occasion to restate how narrowly the power to injunct a foreign proceeding must be used. An anti-suit injunction, it held, is not a routine case-management tool; it is an assertion of authority over a case pending before a foreign sovereign court, and for that reason it demands, in the Court's words, "extremely careful" consideration and must be granted only sparingly.

On the facts, the balance of connecting factors plainly favoured London as the natural forum: both spouses had built a substantial part of their matrimonial life there, the husband had lived in the UK since his teenage years, the wife by then qualified as a habitual resident, and the couple's matrimonial home and financial interests were rooted in England. Against that backdrop, the Court found the trial court's approach faulty at its foundation. Enjoining a foreign suit while it is still at an "inchoate" stage — before the foreign court has even ruled on jurisdiction or decided the case on merits — was, in the Court's assessment, a patent error. The husband's argument that any eventual UK decree would fail the Section 13 CPC test was rejected as premature: a court cannot injunct a foreign proceeding today on the speculation that the judgment it produces tomorrow might not be enforceable. If and when a foreign decree is actually passed, the party resisting it retains every right to challenge its recognition in India at that later stage, on whichever of the Section 13 grounds genuinely apply. What a court cannot do is short-circuit that inquiry in advance by stopping the foreign case from being heard at all.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, like much of rural and semi-urban Haryana, send a steady stream of residents abroad — the UK, Canada, and the Gulf chief among the destinations — and a meaningful share of matrimonial disputes in this district now involve at least one spouse who has lived overseas. Bajoria gives practitioners here a clear, current statement of how the Supreme Court expects such disputes to be handled when proceedings are launched on two continents at once.

The practical lesson for a lawyer advising an NRI client is this: filing for an anti-suit injunction the moment a spouse initiates foreign proceedings is not a low-risk manoeuvre. Courts will scrutinise where the couple actually built their matrimonial life, not merely where the marriage was solemnised or where one party happens to hold Indian citizenship. A client who spent years settled abroad, with property, income, and family ties rooted there, should expect that forum to be treated as the natural one, whatever ground the Indian petition invokes. Equally, counsel defending against an ex-parte injunction obtained in India now has strong appellate authority for the proposition that such relief cannot survive merely on the theory that a foreign decree will later prove unenforceable; that question has to wait until there is an actual decree to test against Section 13. For clients contemplating dual filings as a tactical move, this judgment is a caution that the Supreme Court will not readily bless attempts to use domestic injunctions to freeze a spouse out of a foreign court that has closer, genuine ties to the marriage.

Statutory cross-references

This judgment turns on civil and matrimonial statutes that fall outside the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 renumbering exercise, since neither the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 nor the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 was touched by the 2023 criminal-law reforms. Section 13 of the CPC, which governs when a foreign judgment operates as res judicata in India and lists the grounds on which it can be refused recognition, remains in its original form and is the provision practitioners must return to once a foreign decree is actually in hand. Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act, which lists the recognised grounds for divorce, also stands unamended; irretrievable breakdown continues to sit outside that statutory list, available to litigants only through the Supreme Court's discretionary Article 142 powers in exceptional cases, not as a ground a family court can grant on its own.
