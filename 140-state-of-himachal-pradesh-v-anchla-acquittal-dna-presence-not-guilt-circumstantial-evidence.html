State of Himachal Pradesh v. Anchla @ Chanchla (2026 INSC 1074): Investigating Officer Must Collect Evidence, Not Create It — A DNA Match Shows Presence, Not Guilt

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — State of Himachal Pradesh v. Anchla @ Chanchla, Criminal Appeal No. 166 of 2019
BenchJustice Manoj Misra (author) and Justice Vijay Bishnoi
Date1 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1074
Key SectionsSection 302 IPC (murder); circumstantial evidence; appeal against acquittal; DNA and call-detail evidence

What the case was about

In September 2011, a woman and her young stepson were found strangled inside their house at Diur in Chamba district, Himachal Pradesh. The prosecution said the accused, the boy’s half-sister from his father’s first marriage, had killed them to get property that was allegedly bequeathed to the child. There was no eyewitness, so the State relied on circumstances: a taxi driver who said he drove her to Diur and back and noticed a bleeding thumb, a hotel worker who said he saw her in Chamba afterwards, the recovery of the dead woman’s phone and keys from the accused’s house, a last-seen account from the dead woman’s father, and blood inside the house whose DNA matched the accused.

The Sessions Court convicted her under Section 302 IPC in 2012. The Himachal Pradesh High Court acquitted her on 28 July 2014, and the State came to the Supreme Court.

What the Supreme Court held

The appeal was dismissed and the acquittal stood. The Bench began with the settled limits of its own jurisdiction: an acquittal is interfered with only where the High Court’s view is contrary to the evidence, palpably erroneous or one that no reasonable court could take. On the merits, the Court held that the circumstances were not proved beyond reasonable doubt and did not form a complete chain excluding every hypothesis consistent with innocence.

On the two key witnesses, the Court was blunt. The investigating officer could not explain why or when he recorded the statements of the taxi driver and the hotel worker. Nothing in the accused’s disclosure statement or the father’s evidence pointed to either of them, the police reached the taxi driver only after the arrest, by which time they already knew about the thumb injury, no blood was found in the taxi, and no guest register or other hotel record was produced. The Court found both witnesses untrustworthy and said they appeared to have been “set up” to complete the chain. It put the principle in one line: the role of the investigating officer is to collect evidence and not to create it. The father’s last-seen account had also improved on his earlier statement.

The DNA evidence did not rescue the case. The blood match showed only that the accused had been in that room at some point, not that she was there on the date of the killings, and the Court noted she visited the house regularly. Her blood was not found on the victims’ clothes or on the cloth used to strangle them. The Court also called the failure to collect mobile tower locations for both phones a serious investigational lapse, and observed that the call records of the deceased’s phone were never exhibited, which suggested that vital information was being held back. Finally, there was no evidence of any bequest to the boy, and the accused would not have been the sole beneficiary in any case.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Murder trials in the Sessions Courts at Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri frequently rest on circumstances: last seen together, recovery of articles, and now increasingly forensic and digital material. This judgment gives defence counsel clear lines of cross-examination. Ask the investigating officer why and when each witness was examined, what led him to that witness, and whether the witness was found before or after the police knew the fact he speaks about. A witness who appears only after the police already hold the key detail is exactly what the Court distrusted here.

On forensic evidence, the decision is a reminder that a DNA match is evidence of presence, not of guilt. Where the accused had lawful or habitual access to the place, a match has little value unless the prosecution fixes the time. For the prosecution, the lesson is equally practical: collect the tower location and call detail records of both the accused’s and the victim’s phones, exhibit them, and prove them through a proper certificate. Failing to do so invites an adverse inference.

For State counsel considering appeals against acquittal, the case is a cautionary example. Where the High Court has taken a plausible view of unreliable witnesses, the Supreme Court will not substitute its own.

Statutory cross-references

Section 302 IPC corresponds to Section 103 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. The accused’s disclosure statement, earlier governed by Section 27 of the Evidence Act, now falls under Section 23 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, and electronic records such as call detail records are dealt with in Section 63 BSA (earlier Section 65B of the Evidence Act). The accused’s examination, earlier Section 313 CrPC, is now Section 351 BNSS, and appeals against acquittal, earlier Section 378 CrPC, are now Section 419 BNSS. The 2011 offence itself was tried under the IPC and CrPC as they then stood.
