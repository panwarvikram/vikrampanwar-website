New India Assurance v. Dolly Satish Gandhi: Supreme Court Says Mediclaim Reimbursement Cannot Be Deducted From Motor Accident Compensation

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseNew India Assurance Company Limited v. Dolly Satish Gandhi & Anr.
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & Vipul M. Pancholi, Supreme Court of India
DateReported 16 May 2026
Citation2026 INSC 498
Key SectionsS. 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

Insurance companies contesting MACT claims have long tried a particular argument wherever an injured claimant happened to also hold a Mediclaim or health insurance policy: if the claimant has already been reimbursed for hospital and treatment costs by their own insurer, awarding the same medical expenses again under the Motor Vehicles Act amounts to a "double benefit" — an unjust windfall that the Tribunal should deduct before finalising the award.

This appeal arose out of exactly that dispute. A Bombay High Court judgment had rejected the insurer's deduction argument and held that Mediclaim amounts received by a claimant are not liable to be set off against MACT compensation. New India Assurance Company Limited carried the question to the Supreme Court, relying on the "double benefit" principle and on earlier decisions such as Reliance General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Shashi Sharma (2016) that had gone the other way.

The problem the Supreme Court actually had to resolve was broader than one appeal. The judgment itself records that High Courts across the country — Bombay, Delhi, Kerala, Punjab & Haryana, Madras and Madhya Pradesh among them — had taken divergent views on whether Mediclaim reimbursement should be deducted from motor accident compensation. Practitioners appearing before MACTs in Haryana would have encountered this exact uncertainty: whether to concede a deduction, or to resist it, often depended on which line of High Court authority the Tribunal happened to prefer.

What the court held

The Bench of Justice Sanjay Karol and Justice Vipul M. Pancholi rejected the insurer's argument and held, in clear terms, that Mediclaim or medical insurance reimbursement cannot be deducted from compensation awarded under the Motor Vehicles Act — even where that compensation includes an award under the head of medical expenses.

The Court's reasoning turned on the distinct legal character of the two remedies. Compensation under the MV Act is a statutory entitlement that arises the moment there is a motor accident causing injury or death — it flows from legislative mandate and exists independent of anything the claimant may have privately arranged. A Mediclaim policy, by contrast, is a contractual benefit: the insured pays premiums over years specifically to protect against medical uncertainty, and the payout when a claim arises is simply the fruit of that private bargain. As the Bench put it, "a statutory benefit flows from the authority of law, while a contractual benefit flows from the will and agreement inter se parties."

Relying on the Supreme Court's earlier rulings in Helen C. Rebello v. Maharashtra SRTC (1999) and Patricia Jean Mahajan v. National Insurance Co. Ltd. (2002), the Bench reiterated that the "principle of loss and gain has to be on the same plane within the same sphere." Because a Mediclaim payout and MV Act compensation do not arise from the same sphere — one is insurance the claimant paid for, the other is tortious liability of the wrongdoer's insurer — treating them as interchangeable and netting one against the other would let the offending vehicle's insurer benefit from the claimant's own foresight and years of premium payments. The Court was direct about this: "only because they appear the same or similar, they cannot be termed as 'double benefit'."

The Bench also went out of its way to address the underlying problem of conflicting precedent that had produced this appeal in the first place, describing it as a shared institutional responsibility of the Bar and the Bench to maintain consistency, and noting that counsel are duty-bound to place before the Court judgments that cut against their own case, not merely those that support it. Having settled the legal position, the Supreme Court dismissed the insurer's appeal and remanded the matter for determination consistent with the principles it laid down.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

This ruling closes off a deduction argument that insurers have repeatedly raised before Tribunals in Haryana, and it does so with binding, all-India authority — which matters because the judgment expressly notes that Punjab & Haryana High Court decisions were among those reflecting the earlier divergence. Claimant-side counsel filing under Section 166 no longer need to fear that disclosing a Mediclaim policy, or the fact of reimbursement already received, will be used to whittle down the medical-expenses head of the award. That fact can now be placed on record without strategic hesitation.

For insurer-side counsel, the "double benefit" line of argument on Mediclaim specifically is no longer available, though the judgment leaves open — and does not disturb — other established deduction principles, such as deductions for amounts genuinely received from the same tortfeasor's insurer or under statutory heads that do overlap. The distinction the Court draws is narrow and precise: it turns on whether the second benefit arises from the same sphere as the MACT award, or from an entirely independent contractual source.

Practically, this also affects how claim petitions and written statements should be drafted at Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri MACTs going forward — claimants can plead medical expenses in full regardless of insurance reimbursement, and Tribunals now have a single, settled Supreme Court authority to rely on instead of choosing between conflicting High Court lines.

Statutory cross-references

The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 is a standalone special statute and is unaffected by the transition from the IPC/CrPC to the BNS/BNSS, 2023 — Sections 166 and 168 governing MACT claims and awards remain unchanged. Where a motor accident also gives rise to criminal liability for rash or negligent driving, the erstwhile Section 304A IPC (causing death by negligence) now corresponds to Section 106, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, and Section 279 IPC (rash driving) corresponds to Section 281, BNS — relevant where a parallel criminal case runs alongside the MACT claim, though the two proceedings remain legally independent of each other.
