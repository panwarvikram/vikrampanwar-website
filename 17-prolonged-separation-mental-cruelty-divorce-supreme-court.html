ST v. VS: Supreme Court Holds Prolonged Separation Can Be Treated as Mental Cruelty Under the Hindu Marriage Act

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseST v. VS
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & Augustine George Masih, Supreme Court of India
DateReported 4 June 2026
Citation2026 INSC 620
Key SectionsS. 13(1)(ia), Hindu Marriage Act, 1955; Art. 142, Constitution of India

What the case was about

Two doctors married in December 2007 in Gujarat, but the wife continued to serve as a Gynaecologist in a government hospital there while the husband was in State service in Rajasthan. According to the husband, the wife resided with him at the matrimonial home for only about two to three months across the entire marriage, sociocultural differences quickly surfaced, and even during that brief cohabitation she slept separately and locked her room from inside. No child was born of the marriage.

The husband filed for divorce before the Family Court, Bharatpur, under Section 13(1)(ia) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, on the ground of cruelty. The Family Court dismissed the petition, holding that cruelty had not been established. On appeal, the Rajasthan High Court reversed that finding and granted a divorce decree, holding that the wife had denied conjugal relations, insulted the husband publicly, and that the couple had by then remained separated for over fifteen years. The wife appealed to the Supreme Court, arguing that she had never abandoned the marriage and remained willing to reconcile, and that the High Court had effectively granted divorce on desertion and irretrievable breakdown — grounds that had not even been pleaded in the original petition.

This is a fact pattern that shows up constantly in Family Court matters in Haryana too: a marriage that has been dead in substance for years, one spouse still resisting divorce on principle, and a Family Court unsure whether prolonged separation alone — without a clean, single, provable act of cruelty — is enough to grant relief.

What the court held

The Supreme Court dismissed the wife's appeal and upheld the divorce, but its reasoning is what makes the judgment significant. The Bench of Justice Sanjay Karol and Justice Augustine George Masih held that an Appellate Court may legitimately treat a prolonged period of separation, coupled with complete cessation of cohabitation and emotional alienation, as an indicator of mental cruelty under Section 13(1)(ia) of the Hindu Marriage Act — provided it is careful to ensure that the party seeking divorce does not thereby profit from their own wrong or unilateral desertion.

On the specific facts, the Court found that even during the short period the parties lived together, the wife's persistent refusal of conjugal relations amounted to mental cruelty, relying on the well-established line from Samar Ghosh v. Jaya Ghosh (2007) that denial of conjugal rights without reasonable cause is cruelty in itself. The Bench observed that courts have "repeatedly established that withholding sexual intimacy inflicts severe emotional distress and undermines the bedrock of marriage."

More importantly for practitioners, the Court addressed the wife's technical objection — that desertion had not been specifically pleaded, so the High Court could not rely on separation and estrangement to grant divorce. The Bench rejected a narrow, label-driven approach to matrimonial pleadings, holding that "matrimonial disputes could not be confined to isolated legal labels" and that courts must examine the overall conduct of the parties. It also held that an Appellate Court, since an appeal is a continuation of the suit, is entitled to take into account developments and conduct occurring during the pendency of litigation — including a further decade and more of separation that accumulated while the case wound through the courts.

Drawing on Nayan Bhowmick v. Aparna Chakraborty (2025), the Court held that a prolonged mutual failure to accommodate each other can itself amount to cruelty to both sides, since compelling estranged spouses to remain bound to a marriage that exists only on paper serves neither party. Finally, invoking its extraordinary power under Article 142 of the Constitution and the line of authority in Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan (2023) and Vikas Kanaujia v. Sarita (2025), the Court held the case was independently fit for dissolution on the ground of irretrievable breakdown of marriage — even though that ground formally exists outside the text of Section 13.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Family Courts at Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri regularly see long-pending matrimonial matters where the parties have been separated for years, sometimes decades, with no single dramatic act of cruelty to point to — just accumulated estrangement. This judgment gives practitioners representing a spouse seeking divorce a clearer basis to argue that prolonged separation and cessation of cohabitation, taken together with conduct during the marriage, independently supports a cruelty finding under Section 13(1)(ia), without needing to separately plead and prove desertion or irretrievable breakdown as distinct causes of action.

For the opposing side, the ruling is a reminder that resisting divorce purely on the technical ground that the "correct" statutory label was not pleaded is unlikely to succeed if the overall record shows genuine, long-term estrangement — though the safeguard the Court builds in is important to remember: an appellate or trial court must still ensure the petitioning spouse is not profiting from their own wrongful conduct, such as a unilateral, unjustified walkout. That inquiry into who caused the separation, and whether it was reasonable, remains very much alive and should be a central part of the defence in any matter where separation is being used as evidence of cruelty.

The judgment also reaffirms the Supreme Court's continuing willingness to use Article 142 to end marriages that are irretrievably broken even where fault is difficult to apportion cleanly — a useful line of authority to cite in matters stuck for years at the Family Court or District Court stage with no realistic prospect of reconciliation.

Statutory cross-references

The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 is a personal-law statute and is unaffected by the transition from the IPC/CrPC to the BNS/BNSS, 2023 — Section 13(1)(ia) on cruelty remains unchanged. Where allegations of matrimonial cruelty also give rise to a criminal complaint, the erstwhile Section 498A IPC (cruelty by husband or relatives) now corresponds to Sections 85 and 86, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, and applications for maintenance that were formerly filed under Section 125 CrPC now proceed under Section 144, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.
