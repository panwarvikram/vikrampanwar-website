Buy Land While a Suit Is Pending, and You Inherit the Suit's Fate: P&H High Court Applies Lis Pendens Under Section 52 TPA

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / CasePunjab & Haryana High Court · Regular Second Appeal arising from a suit for declaration of ownership over agricultural land
BenchJustice Harsh Bunger (Single Bench)
DateDecided 17 July 2026
CitationReported by Lawyer eNews, 3 August 2026 · neutral citation not yet available at the time of writing
Key SectionsS. 52, Transfer of Property Act, 1882 · Second Appeal, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908

What the case was about

The dispute traces back to 1983, when Gurbachan Singh, the recorded owner of a parcel of agricultural land, filed a civil suit against one Amarjit Singh and another defendant challenging a power of attorney and a sale that Amarjit Singh claimed authorised him to deal with the land. The civil court hearing that 1983 suit passed an injunction restraining any alienation of the land pending its decision. Despite that injunction being on record, Amarjit Singh went ahead and executed registered sale deeds in favour of a fresh set of buyers between late 1984 and early 1985.

The 1983 suit did not go to a contested verdict. It ended in a compromise decree declaring Gurbachan Singh the absolute owner of the land, leaving Amarjit Singh with no residual right to have sold anything to anyone. Years later, the 1984-85 buyers filed their own suit for declaration of ownership and possession, relying purely on their registered sale deeds and treating the 1985 compromise decree as a collusive arrangement that did not bind them. The First Appellate Court rejected that suit, holding the buyers could not get a better title than their vendor actually had. That dismissal was carried to the High Court by way of a regular second appeal.

What the court held

Justice Harsh Bunger framed the case around two questions that come up constantly in property litigation: can a purchaser who bought land while a suit over that very land was pending claim a title independent of that suit's outcome, and can a party simply brand an inconvenient prior decree as "void" in a fresh, unrelated suit without ever asking a court to set it aside?

On the first question, the Court applied the rule of lis pendens under Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 in its plainest form. Section 52 does not stop anyone from selling property that is the subject of pending litigation — what it does is subordinate whatever the buyer gets to the result of that litigation. The Court put it directly: any party purchasing a property that is the subject matter of litigation, during the pendency of that litigation, is bound by the judgment ultimately rendered between the original parties, and that binds subsequent purchasers even though they were never made parties to the suit. Since the 1984-85 sale deeds were executed while the 1983 suit was actively pending and in the teeth of an existing injunction against alienation, the buyers stepped into Amarjit Singh's shoes with all the same vulnerabilities he carried — nothing more.

On the second question, the Court rejected the buyers' attempt to treat the 1985 compromise decree as collusive and non-binding. Relying on the Supreme Court's rulings in Meghmala v. G. Narasimha Reddy and M. Meenakshi v. Metadin Agarwal, the Bench held that a decree passed on compromise carries exactly the same binding force as one passed after a contested trial, and that a litigant who genuinely believes a prior decree is void has to say so in the appropriate proceeding and get it formally set aside — not simply plead around it in a later, unconnected suit. The Court also invoked the Supreme Court's decision in Union of India v. Major S.P. Sharma on the finality of judicial orders, observing that allowing decrees to be collaterally attacked at will would breed exactly the kind of confusion the doctrine of finality exists to prevent. Since the buyers never formally sought to set aside the 1985 decree despite the decree being squarely on record, their suit for declaration could not survive. The second appeal was dismissed.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Land transactions in the Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani belt frequently sit downstream of unresolved family partition disputes and injunction suits that a buyer may never hear about unless someone specifically checks. This ruling is a useful reminder to build that check into every conveyancing file: a search of the civil court's pending-case register against the specific khasra numbers is not a formality, it is what stands between a client and a sale deed that turns out to be worth nothing once an older suit concludes. A registered sale deed, by itself, proves nothing about the vendor's title if he was under a live injunction or was himself a defendant in a suit over the same land.

The second half of the ruling is equally practical for litigation files. Clients who want to resist an old decree often instinctively argue, in whatever forum they are currently in, that the earlier order was "fraudulent" or "collusive" and need not be honoured. This judgment is a clean authority against that shortcut — a decree stays binding until a competent court sets it aside in proceedings brought specifically for that purpose. Advising a client to file that challenge promptly, rather than raising it defensively years later, can be the difference between a live claim and a suit dismissed on this ground alone, as happened here.

Statutory cross-references

Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 and the general law of civil procedure under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 are unaffected by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, both of which reformed only the criminal law and criminal procedure. Civil suits, second appeals, and the doctrine of lis pendens continue to be governed exactly as before under the TPA and the CPC, and the Supreme Court precedents on collateral challenges to decrees and on the binding force of compromise decrees remain fully applicable law.
