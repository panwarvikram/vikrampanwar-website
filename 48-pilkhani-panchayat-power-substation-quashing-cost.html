Pilkhani Gram Panchayat Case: P&H High Court Quashes 8-Year Criminal Case Over Land Given for a Power Sub-Station, Calls It "Total Misuse" of Criminal Law

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — Gurdev Singh v. State of Haryana & Ors. (quashing of criminal complaint concerning Pilkhani Gram Panchayat, Ambala)
BenchJustice Sanjay Vashisth
DateOrder dated 24 July 2026 (reported 28 July 2026)
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 242
Key SectionsCheating, forgery & criminal conspiracy provisions, IPC (as alleged in the private complaint) · Quashing jurisdiction under S. 482, CrPC — now S. 528, BNSS, 2023

What the case was about

For years, the village of Pilkhani in Ambala district went dark after sunset for want of a nearby power sub-station. To fix that, the Gram Panchayat resolved to give up 32 kanals and 11 marlas of its own common village land so that Haryana Vidyut Parsaran Nigam Limited (HVPNL) could build a 66 KV sub-station there. The tehsildar fixed a market valuation, the transaction went through a registered sale deed, and a sum of over ₹1.01 crore was credited directly into the Gram Panchayat's own account — not to any individual. The sub-station was built, and power finally reached the village.

What the sarpanch and eight panches who signed off on that decision got in return was not gratitude but an eight-year criminal ordeal. A villager, Gurdev Singh, who claimed a share in the land as one of its mushtarka malkan (joint co-sharers of village common land), filed a private criminal complaint in 2018 alleging that the panchayat resolution had been manipulated and the sale deed fabricated — invoking allegations of cheating, forgery, and criminal conspiracy against all nine panchayat functionaries. A magistrate's court in Ambala summoned all of them to face trial in March 2019, and the matter dragged on for years before reaching the High Court on a petition to quash the complaint and the summoning order.

What the court held

Justice Sanjay Vashisth allowed the quashing petition in full, and did so in unusually strong terms. Several facts weighed with the Court. First, the complainant had approached the police before filing his private complaint, and the police had found no substance in his allegations — a sequence the Court treated as bearing on the bona fides of the private complaint that followed. Second, nothing in the complaint suggested that any of the nine accused had personally pocketed even a single rupee of the sale consideration; the money was shown to be sitting untouched in a fixed deposit in the Gram Panchayat's own account. Third, the sale deed itself had never been independently challenged or set aside in any civil proceeding. Fourth, every step of the transaction — the tehsildar's valuation, the Block Development and Panchayat Officer's clearance, and HVPNL's own approvals — had gone through institutional oversight, not a unilateral decision by the sarpanch.

The judge went on to describe the panchayat's initiative as a "progressive and developmental project" of a kind "rarely undertaken" by village panchayats in this part of the country, and held that it deserved to be encouraged, not punished through a criminal trial. He characterised the prosecution as a "total misuse of the criminal law" and quashed both the complaint and the summoning order. As a mark of disapproval, the Court imposed a cost of ₹1 lakh on the complainant, payable to the Gram Panchayat, with a further ₹1,000 for every day of delay if the amount is not paid by 17 October 2026. Notably, the Court also directed that a copy of the order be circulated to panchayats across Punjab and Haryana, holding it up as a model to be followed.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Sarpanches and panches across rural Haryana routinely face private criminal complaints over ordinary panchayat land decisions — particularly transfers or leases of shamlat (common village) land for roads, schools, water works, or, as here, power infrastructure. Such complaints, often filed by a disgruntled co-sharer or a rival who missed out on compensation, can hang over a functionary's head for years even where the transaction was entirely above board. This ruling gives practitioners a usable template for a quashing petition: show that the transaction went through statutory checkpoints (tehsildar valuation, BDPO sanction, departmental approval), that the sale consideration remains with the panchayat rather than any individual, that the sale deed was never separately set aside in a civil suit, and that the complainant tried the police route first and was turned away before filing a private complaint.

For advocates in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, where shamlat land disputes recur in district court practice, the decision is a useful counter to the tactic of using a criminal complaint as leverage in what is really a civil dispute over land shares. The escalating daily cost for delay is itself a signal that High Courts will make frivolous private complaints against public functionaries genuinely expensive, not merely embarrassing.

Statutory cross-references

The quashing jurisdiction exercised here rested on Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, which now corresponds to Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023. The original private complaint (filed in 2018) invoked the general IPC offences of cheating, forgery, and criminal conspiracy; for conduct occurring after 1 July 2024, these broadly correspond to the relevant cheating, forgery, and criminal-conspiracy provisions of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, though the underlying facts and complaint in this case were governed entirely by the IPC/CrPC regime given their 2013–2019 timeline. Panchayat land alienation itself continues to be governed by the Punjab Village Common Lands (Regulation) Act, 1961 (as applicable to Haryana) and the Haryana Panchayati Raj Act, 1994, together with departmental instructions requiring tehsildar valuation and Block Development and Panchayat Officer sanction before common land can be transferred.
