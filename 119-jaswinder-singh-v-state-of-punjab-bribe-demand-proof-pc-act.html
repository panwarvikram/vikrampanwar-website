Jaswinder Singh v. State of Punjab (2026 INSC 1031): Supreme Court Clears a Patwari Because No One Saw Him Demand or Take the Bribe

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Jaswinder Singh (Dead Through LRs) v. State of Punjab, Criminal Appeal No. 2137 of 2014
BenchJustice Ujjal Bhuyan (author) and Justice Arun Palli
Date22 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1031
Key SectionsSections 7 and 13(1)(d) read with 13(2), Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (pre-2018 text)

What the case was about

In 2002, a man in Hoshiarpur district wanted the land of his deceased brother mutated in favour of the brother's legal heirs. The widow had applied for the mutation, and the file landed with the local Patwari, Jaswinder Singh. According to the complainant, the Patwari asked for ₹10,000 to do the job and eventually settled for ₹9,500.

The complainant went to the Punjab Vigilance Bureau. A classic trap followed: nineteen ₹500 notes were dusted with phenolphthalein powder, a friend of the complainant was appointed as the shadow witness, and two government officials were joined to the raiding party. On 19 April 2002 the raid took place, the notes were said to have been recovered from the Patwari, and his fingers turned the sodium carbonate solution pink.

The Special Judge, Hoshiarpur convicted him in February 2004 under Section 7 read with Section 13(2) of the Prevention of Corruption Act and gave him two years' rigorous imprisonment with a ₹10,000 fine. The Punjab & Haryana High Court dismissed his appeal in November 2013. He reached the Supreme Court in 2014, was granted bail after about seven months in jail, and died while the appeal was pending. His legal representatives were substituted in 2018 and pursued the appeal to clear his name. The judgment came twelve years after the appeal was filed, and more than two decades after the trap.

What the court held

The Supreme Court allowed the appeal, set aside both the trial court and High Court judgments, and declared that the charges against the Patwari had not been proved.

The reasoning turned entirely on the evidence of the two independent witnesses, not on any new point of law. The bench restated the settled position from three decisions:

  • P. Satyanarayana Murthy v. State of A.P. (2015) 10 SCC 152 — proof of demand is the very core of an offence under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d)(i)–(ii); recovery of money without proof of demand does not make out the offence.
  • Neeraj Dutta v. State (GNCT of Delhi) (2023) 4 SCC 731 (Constitution Bench) — demand and acceptance must both be proved as facts in issue. They can be proved by direct evidence or, where the complainant's evidence is unavailable, by circumstantial evidence. But there still has to be some reliable evidence of them.
  • Aman Bhatia v. State, 2025 SCC OnLine SC 1013 — mere possession and recovery of tainted notes, without proof of demand, is not enough.

Applied to the record, the prosecution collapsed on its own witnesses. The shadow witness, whose only job was to watch the transaction and give the signal, testified that he had stepped out of the office because the Patwari seemed busy. He came back in only after the complainant signalled that the money had been paid. He said plainly that no payment took place in front of him, that he stood behind the raiding party during the search, and that the recovery was not made in his presence. The official witness from the raiding party likewise admitted he did not see the money being handed over.

There was also a conflict about where the notes were found. The complainant said trouser pocket. The official witness said the front pocket of the shirt. The shadow witness's police statement recorded that he had been told it was the shirt pocket. The Court described these as discrepancies, but its conclusion rested on the more basic point: apart from the complainant, nobody saw a demand, nobody saw a payment, and nobody saw an acceptance. On that record neither demand nor acceptance was proved, and the conviction could not stand.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Trap cases against Patwaris, Kanungos, Naib Tehsildars and clerks in mutation and registry work are a staple of the Special Courts in Haryana, and they are almost always built the same way as this one. The ruling is a useful checklist for both sides.

For the defence, the first question in cross-examination is not “were the notes recovered?” but “who actually heard the demand and saw the handover?” A shadow witness who was outside the room, or who arrived only on a signal, cannot supply what the complainant alone asserts. The phenolphthalein test proves that the accused touched the notes; it says nothing about why. Inconsistencies about the place of recovery also matter, because they go to whether the recovery was actually witnessed at all.

For the prosecution and investigating agencies, the case is a warning about how trap proceedings are staged. If the shadow witness is not placed where he can hear and see the transaction, the case rests on the complainant, who is by definition an interested witness. Neeraj Dutta allows demand to be inferred from circumstances, but only from circumstances that are themselves proved. Recovery by itself is not one of them.

A note on the presumption. Prosecutors often rely on the presumption under Section 20 of the PC Act. That presumption only comes into play once acceptance of the gratification is proved. Where the handover itself is unwitnessed, as here, there is nothing for the presumption to attach to.

Finally, the case shows that an appeal against conviction does not end when the accused dies. Legal representatives can carry it on and win an acquittal that clears the family name and can matter for service and pension benefits.

Statutory cross-references

  • Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988: The case was decided on the pre-2018 text of Sections 7 and 13(1)(d). The Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018 recast Section 7 and removed Section 13(1)(d) altogether, so criminal misconduct is now limited to the new Section 13(1)(a) (fraudulent misappropriation) and 13(1)(b) (illicit enrichment). The demand-and-acceptance principle remains central to trap cases under the new Section 7. The PC Act is a special statute and was not absorbed into the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.
  • Section 20 PC Act — presumption where a public servant accepts gratification; it depends on acceptance first being proved.
  • Section 161 CrPC (police statements used to show contradictions) → Section 180 BNSS, 2023.
  • Section 313 CrPC (examination of the accused) → Section 351 BNSS, 2023.
  • Section 394 CrPC (abatement of appeals; a near relative may continue an appeal against a sentence of imprisonment) → Section 435 BNSS, 2023.
  • Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (burden of proof and appreciation of evidence) → Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023.
