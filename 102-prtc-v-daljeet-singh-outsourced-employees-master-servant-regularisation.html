PRTC v. Daljeet Singh & Ors.: P&H High Court Says Outsourced Bus Staff Can't Claim Regularisation Without a Documented Appointment

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePepsu Road Transport Corporation v. Daljeet Singh & Ors. (LPA No. 1549 of 2026 & three connected appeals), P&H High Court
BenchJustice Harsimran Singh Sethi & Justice Minderjeet Yadav
DateDecided & reported 10 September 2026
Citation2026 LL (PH) 325
Key SectionsPunjab Ad hoc, Contractual, Daily Wage, Temporary, Work Charged and Outsourced Employees' Welfare Act, 2016 · Regulation 8, PEPSU RTC Employees (Conditions of Appointment and Service) Regulations, 1981

What the case was about

A group of drivers, conductors and similar staff had spent years behind the wheel and at the ticket window for the Pepsu Road Transport Corporation (PRTC) — except that, on paper, none of them was ever PRTC's employee. Every one of them had been recruited and appointed by a private manpower agency, M/s S.S. Service Providers, under a supply contract between the agency and PRTC, and then simply deployed to work PRTC's routes and depots.

Relying on the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in Jaggo v. Union of India — which held that an employee doing the same work for ten continuous years is entitled to regularisation — the workers moved the High Court seeking to be declared PRTC employees and regularised, along with Old Pension Scheme benefits for those who had joined before 1 January 2004. They argued that PRTC exercised full supervisory and disciplinary control over their day-to-day work, that the outsourcing arrangement was a fig leaf, and that the Single Judge who first heard the matter was right to "lift the veil" and treat PRTC as their real employer "for all intents and purposes."

The Single Judge agreed and allowed their writ petitions. PRTC appealed to a Division Bench through four connected Letters Patent Appeals, arguing that it had never issued a single appointment order — not even a temporary or officiating one — to any of the respondents at any stage of their service, and that the point was already settled against outsourced workers by an earlier Division Bench ruling in Nishan Singh v. State of Punjab (2013).

What the court held

The Division Bench sided entirely with PRTC and set aside the Single Judge's order. Its starting point was a simple evidentiary rule: a master-servant relationship is a question of documents, not of impressions. Long years of deployment, close supervision, or even control over the manner in which the work is done cannot, by themselves, convert an outsourced worker into a direct employee of the entity that benefits from that work. What is required is a paper trail — an actual appointment order, or at minimum some formal act by which the principal employer took the worker onto its own rolls, even provisionally.

Applying that test, the Bench found the respondents' case had nothing to stand on. It was undisputed that PRTC had never issued any of them so much as a temporary or ad hoc appointment letter. Their salaries and provident fund had always been handled by the outsourcing agency, which was recorded as their employer for PF purposes — a detail the respondents never seriously contested and one the Court treated as fatal to their claim of a direct employment bond with PRTC.

The Court also drew a sharp factual line between this case and the Supreme Court's ruling in Jaggo, which the workers had leaned on heavily. Jaggo, the Bench explained, dealt with employees who had originally been recruited directly by the principal employer and were later shifted onto outsourced arrangements — a demotion of status that the Supreme Court refused to let stand. Here, by contrast, the respondents had been hired by the outsourcing agency from day one and had never, at any point, held any status with PRTC. Reading Jaggo's observations outside that specific factual context, the Bench held, would stretch the ruling well past what it actually decided.

On the Punjab Ad hoc, Contractual, Daily Wage, Temporary, Work Charged and Outsourced Employees' Welfare Act, 2016, the Court noted that Section 6 only obliges an establishment to bring outsourced workers onto contract if they have completed three years of continuous service before the Act came into force, and only where the establishment can bear that financial burden without passing it on to the State exchequer. There was no finding that PRTC had ever taken these workers onto contract, or that it was financially equipped to absorb them; the Single Judge, the Bench observed, had in fact gone further and extended benefits even to employees who joined as late as 2017 — well beyond anything the 2016 Act could support. A separate attempt to draw an equality argument from 110 similarly-placed employees who had earlier been regularised also failed, since those workers turned out to be direct contractual appointees of PRTC, never routed through a private agency, and so were not comparable at all.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Outsourcing is now the default mode of staffing across Haryana's transport corporations, municipal bodies, hospitals and universities, and disputes over regularisation are a recurring feature of service-law practice at the district court and High Court level. This ruling gives practitioners on both sides a clear, workable test: before advising a client to litigate for regularisation, look for paper. A driver or clerk who can point to even a single order, memo or communication in which the principal employer treated him as its own — however informally — has a real case. One who can only point to years of loyal, supervised service, with every wage slip and PF contribution routed through a contractor, is fighting an uphill battle after this judgment.

For counsel representing outsourced workers, the practical lesson is to build the documentary record early — RTI applications for attendance registers, duty rosters signed by the principal employer's officers, correspondence addressed directly to the worker by the employer rather than the contractor — rather than relying on the sheer length of service or the intensity of supervisory control, both of which this ruling treats as legally insufficient on their own. For counsel defending public sector undertakings and government departments, the judgment is a template defence: produce the outsourcing contract, show that wages and PF ran through the agency, and distinguish Jaggo on the specific ground that the workers were never directly recruited employees whose status was later downgraded.

The ruling also reinforces the limited reach of the 2016 Punjab Welfare Act for outsourced employees — it is a narrow, conditional obligation tied to a fixed cut-off and the employer's proven financial capacity, not a general regularisation gateway, and practitioners should not overstate its scope when advising clients either way.

Statutory cross-references

This case turns on service jurisprudence and State legislation rather than the criminal codes, so there is no BNS/BNSS/BSA migration to track. The governing framework remains the Punjab Ad hoc, Contractual, Daily Wage, Temporary, Work Charged and Outsourced Employees' Welfare Act, 2016 (particularly Section 6), Regulation 8 of the PEPSU Road Transport Corporation Employees (Conditions of Appointment and Service) Regulations, 1981, and the constitutional principle against back-door regularisation traced to Secretary, State of Karnataka v. Uma Devi, AIR 2006 SC 1806.
