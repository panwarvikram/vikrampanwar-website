Judicial Separation Under Section 10 of the Hindu Marriage Act: What It Does, How It Differs From Divorce, and When Courts Have Refused It

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseHirachand Srinivas Managaonkar v. Sunanda; P&H HC decision on estoppel
BenchSupreme Court: Justices D.P. Mohapatra & Doraiswamy Raju · P&H HC: Justices Sureshwar Thakur & Sudeepti Sharma
Date20 March 2001 (Supreme Court) · 18 July 2024 (P&H High Court)
Citation(2001) 4 SCC 125 · 2024 LiveLaw (PH) 162
Key SectionsSs. 10, 13(1), 13(1A)(i) & 23, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955

What the topic is about

Not every spouse who walks into a matrimonial lawyer's office wants a divorce. Many want a formal, court-recognised break from cohabitation without permanently dissolving the marriage — whether for religious reasons, social pressure, the hope of reconciliation, or simply because they are not yet ready to close the door entirely. Section 10 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 exists precisely for this situation. It allows either spouse to seek a decree of judicial separation on any of the grounds available for divorce, without asking the court to end the marriage itself.

Judicial separation is often misunderstood, including by litigants who file for it as a halfway measure without appreciating what it actually changes and what it leaves untouched. It is also, as one Punjab & Haryana High Court ruling discussed below shows, sometimes misused as a second attempt at relief already refused under a different provision. A district court practice built around matrimonial matters in Haryana sees judicial separation petitions with some regularity, whether as a standalone relief, a fallback plea, or a stepping stone that a party later relies on to seek divorce once a year has passed without reconciliation.

What the legal position is

Section 10(1) of the Hindu Marriage Act permits either party to a marriage to present a petition for judicial separation on any of the grounds specified in Section 13(1) — the same fault grounds available for divorce, such as adultery, cruelty, desertion for two years, conversion, unsoundness of mind, virulent and incurable leprosy, venereal disease in a communicable form, renunciation of the world, and presumption of death. A wife may additionally rely on the special grounds available to her alone under Section 13(2). Since the 1976 amendment brought the grounds for judicial separation and divorce into alignment, a petitioner effectively has to prove the same case whether they seek judicial separation or divorce; the difference lies entirely in what relief is asked for and what the decree does.

That difference is captured in Section 10(2). Once a decree of judicial separation is passed, it is no longer obligatory for the petitioner to cohabit with the respondent. The marital tie itself, however, survives intact. Neither spouse may remarry, and neither ceases to be the other's legal spouse for purposes such as inheritance, unless a subsequent decree of divorce is obtained. The provision also allows the court, on the application of either party, to rescind the decree if it considers it just and reasonable to do so — recognising judicial separation as a potentially reversible arrangement rather than a final severing of the relationship.

Judicial separation also has a strategic dimension within the Act's own scheme. Under Section 13(1A)(i), if there has been no resumption of cohabitation between the parties for a period of one year or more after a decree of judicial separation, either party may then present a petition for divorce on that ground alone, without having to re-establish the original fault ground. This is why judicial separation is sometimes used, deliberately, as a first step toward an eventual no-fault-style divorce: a spouse who cannot immediately make out a clean divorce case, or who wants a formal cooling-off period recorded by a court, may obtain judicial separation first and let the statutory one-year clock run.

That route is not, however, available to a party whose own conduct is responsible for the non-resumption of cohabitation. In Hirachand Srinivas Managaonkar v. Sunanda, (2001) 4 SCC 125, the Supreme Court considered a husband who sought divorce under Section 13(1A)(i) after a decree of judicial separation, but who had himself failed to comply with a maintenance order in favour of his wife and daughter despite having the means to pay. The Court held that Section 23(1)(a), which requires a petitioner to show they are not taking advantage of their own wrong, applies equally to a Section 13(1A)(i) petition. A spouse cannot engineer the year of non-cohabitation through their own default and then rely on that default to claim divorce as of right.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Judicial separation petitions before the district courts and Family Courts in Haryana most often arise in three situations: as a standalone relief sought by a spouse unwilling to pursue outright divorce; as an interim or alternative prayer alongside a divorce petition; and, less legitimately, as a second bite at relief after a divorce petition on similar facts has failed. The Punjab & Haryana High Court addressed the third situation directly. Hearing an appeal against a Family Court order that had rejected a judicial separation petition, the Division Bench of Justices Sureshwar Thakur and Sudeepti Sharma held that filing a Section 10 petition after a binding and unchallenged dismissal of an earlier Section 13 divorce petition on the same or similar grounds is "a gross abuse of the process of law" and "a cleverly deployed stratagem" to circumvent the earlier, conclusive verdict. The Court invoked the principle of constructive res judicata, holding that once the dismissal of the divorce petition had attained finality, the party was estopped from re-litigating essentially the same grievance under a different label. The appeal was dismissed as not maintainable.

For practitioners, the ruling cautions against treating Section 10 and Section 13 as interchangeable fallback options to be tried one after another on the same facts. A judicial separation petition filed after an unsuccessful, unappealed divorce petition on the same ground will not survive scrutiny; a client who genuinely wants both possibilities kept open should plead judicial separation as an alternative relief within the same proceeding, or appeal the original dismissal, rather than filing a fresh petition once the first has become final.

On the drafting side, counsel advising a client unsure whether to seek judicial separation or outright divorce should flag the practical consequences clearly: a decree ends the obligation to cohabit and typically settles interim maintenance and custody, but it does not open the door to remarriage and does not, by itself, affect succession rights between the spouses. Where the real objective is an eventual clean break, judicial separation followed by a Section 13(1A)(i) petition after a year of continued separation can work, but only if the client's own conduct during that year cannot later be characterised, as in Hirachand Srinivas Managaonkar, as taking advantage of their own wrong.

Statutory cross-references

Judicial separation is a creature of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 and has no direct BNS or BNSS analogue, since it is a civil matrimonial remedy rather than a criminal provision. Practitioners handling allied criminal proceedings arising from the same marital breakdown — such as complaints under Section 85, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (corresponding to Section 498-A, IPC, cruelty by husband or relatives) or maintenance proceedings under Section 144, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (corresponding to Section 125, CrPC) — should note that a judicial separation decree, by itself, does not affect the maintainability of these separate remedies, though the facts pleaded and proved in a judicial separation petition can be relevant evidence in parallel proceedings.
