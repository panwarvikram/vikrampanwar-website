Mutual Consent Divorce Under Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act: The Two Motions, the Cooling Period, and When It Can Be Waived

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing LawHindu Marriage Act, 1955
Leading CaseAmardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur
BenchJustices A.K. Goel & U.U. Lalit, Supreme Court of India
Date12 September 2017
Citation(2017) 8 SCC 746
Key SectionsS. 13B, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 · S. 28, Special Marriage Act, 1954 · S. 21, Family Courts Act, 1984

What the topic is about

Mutual consent divorce is the route most Haryana couples actually use to end a Hindu marriage without fighting a contested case on grounds like cruelty or desertion. Instead of one spouse having to prove a fault ground against the other under Section 13, both spouses agree that the marriage has broken down, that they have been living separately for at least a year, and that they cannot live together — and they ask the court to dissolve the marriage on that joint basis under Section 13B. It is faster, cheaper, and spares both sides the evidence, cross-examination and mudslinging a contested divorce petition otherwise demands. Its equivalent for inter-religious or civil marriages is Section 28 of the Special Marriage Act, which follows an almost identical two-motion structure.

The catch that trips up most first-time petitioners is that mutual consent divorce is not a single filing that ends in an immediate decree. Section 13B builds in a deliberate pause — commonly called the "cooling period" or "cooling-off period" — precisely because Parliament wanted couples to have one last, structured opportunity to reconsider before the marriage is formally dissolved. Understanding that structure, and when a Haryana family court can skip it, is the difference between a divorce that takes six weeks and one that drags on for the better part of a year.

What the legal position is

Section 13B(1) lays down three conditions before a joint petition can even be filed: both parties must present the petition together; they must have been living separately for one year or more immediately before filing; and they must jointly state that they have been unable to live together and have mutually agreed the marriage should be dissolved. "Living separately" does not require two different addresses — the Supreme Court and various High Courts have long recognised that spouses can be "living separately" for this purpose even under the same roof, so long as they have stopped discharging marital obligations towards each other. This is what is called the First Motion, and once it is filed and both parties are examined on oath by the court, the case is effectively admitted.

Section 13B(2) then requires the parties to move a Second Motion, and it is here that the cooling period sits: the second motion cannot be moved before six months have elapsed from the date of the first motion, and it must be moved within eighteen months of that date, failing which the petition is treated as having lapsed. In practice, family courts in Haryana list the case for the second motion roughly six to seven months after the first, and if either party fails to appear or withdraws consent at any point before the decree is actually passed, the joint petition falls through entirely — unlike a contested divorce, mutual consent gives either spouse an absolute right to unilaterally withdraw consent right up to the moment the decree is signed, and courts cannot force the divorce through against a withdrawn consent.

The most significant modern development is when courts can waive that six-month wait altogether. In Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, (2017) 8 SCC 746, the Supreme Court held that the cooling period under Section 13B(2) is directory, not mandatory, where the court is satisfied that a case is made out to waive it. Justice A.K. Goel, writing for the bench, laid down the guiding conditions: the statutory one-year separation period preceding the first motion must already be over; all efforts at mediation or reconciliation between the parties must have genuinely failed; the parties must have truly settled their differences, including on alimony, custody of children, and other pending matters; and the waiting period itself would only prolong their agony with no possibility of reconciliation. Where these conditions are met, the parties can move an application, and the concerned court can proceed to waive the waiting period and decide the second motion straightaway — sometimes on the very day the first motion is recorded, if a district's family court practice permits combining the two hearings.

This waiver has become especially relevant for NRI spouses, couples who have already lived apart far longer than a year, or those with pending or parallel litigation (such as a Section 498-A complaint or a maintenance case) they want resolved as part of a comprehensive settlement without further delay. Haryana's district and family courts have applied Amardeep Singh regularly in appropriate cases, though the discretion to waive remains exactly that — discretion, not an entitlement, and courts routinely decline to waive the period where a settlement appears rushed, one-sided, or entered into under pressure.

Why this matters for matrimonial law practitioners in Haryana

The procedural mechanics matter enormously in day-to-day practice. A joint petition under Section 13B must be filed before the district court or family court having jurisdiction — ordinarily where the marriage was solemnised, where the parties last resided together, or where the wife currently resides, per Section 19 of the Act as amended. The petition needs a joint memorandum of settlement covering permanent alimony (even if the amount is nil, that should be recorded), custody and visitation arrangements for any children, and the return of streedhan or jointly held assets, since an incomplete settlement is the single most common reason a second motion gets adjourned or, worse, a decree gets challenged later for having glossed over unresolved issues. Advocates drafting these petitions in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani should also flag any interconnected criminal or maintenance proceedings early, since a genuine, comprehensive settlement quashing those parallel proceedings is often exactly the kind of circumstance that supports an Amardeep Singh waiver application.

Equally important is managing client expectations about the two-motion structure itself. Clients frequently assume mutual consent divorce is instantaneous once both parties agree; explaining the mandatory (absent waiver) six-month gap, and the fact that either spouse can walk away from the process at the second-motion stage even after signing the first-motion papers, prevents avoidable conflict and repeat consultations. Where genuine urgency exists — a scheduled remarriage, an emigration timeline, or a settlement that both sides want locked in quickly — a well-drafted waiver application citing Amardeep Singh, supported by proof that mediation has failed and the settlement is complete, gives the court a proper basis to move the second motion forward without the default wait.

Statutory cross-references

Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 applies to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs married under the Act. Couples married under the Special Marriage Act, 1954 — including inter-religious and civil marriages — follow the parallel two-motion mutual consent procedure under Section 28 of that Act, which carries an identical one-year separation requirement and six-to-eighteen-month window, and to which the Amardeep Singh waiver principle has also been extended by subsequent High Court rulings. Family Courts constituted under the Family Courts Act, 1984 have exclusive jurisdiction over such petitions in districts where a Family Court has been established; in districts without one, the petition is filed before the ordinary District Court exercising matrimonial jurisdiction. None of these provisions have been renumbered or altered by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita or Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, since the 2023 codification touched only the criminal law statutes and left matrimonial legislation untouched.
