Delhi Race Club v. State of Uttar Pradesh: Cheating and Criminal Breach of Trust Under Section 406 and 420 IPC Cannot Co-Exist — 2024 INSC 626

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtSupreme Court of India
BenchJustices J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra
Date of Judgment23 August 2024
Citation2024 INSC 626 : (2024) 10 SCC 690
Key SectionsSections 405, 406, 415, 420 IPC; Sections 190, 200, 202, 204, 482 CrPC

What the case was about

An unpaid supplier of horse feed dragged the Delhi Race Club (1940) Ltd. and two of its office bearers into a criminal complaint before the Additional Chief Judicial Magistrate, Khurja, Bulandshahar. The complainant's firm had supplied grain and oats to the Club since 1990. From 1995 onward, at the Club's instruction, invoices were raised in the name of a related body, the Delhi Horse Trainers Association. Payments continued regularly until 2017, after which Rs. 9,11,434 fell due and was never paid.

Rather than filing a civil recovery suit, the complainant lodged a criminal complaint alleging cheating and criminal breach of trust under Sections 406, 420, and 120B IPC. The Magistrate, after a Section 202 CrPC inquiry, summoned the Club, the Association, and four individuals — but only for the offence under Section 406 IPC (criminal breach of trust), leaving out cheating entirely. The Allahabad High Court declined to quash this summoning order, holding that the Club's conduct in redirecting invoices to the Association showed "mala fide intention." The Club then appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The Supreme Court set aside both the High Court's order and the Magistrate's summoning order, and quashed the complaint in its entirety. The reasoning breaks down into several parts:

  • No entrustment, so no Section 406 IPC: This was a plain case of an unpaid seller. Once goods are sold and delivered, ownership passes to the buyer under Sections 20 and 24 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. Without entrustment of property, there is no criminal breach of trust — non-payment of a sale price is a civil wrong, not a crime.
  • No case for cheating either: Nothing in the complaint showed dishonest intention at the inception of the transaction, which is the essential mens rea for cheating under Section 415 IPC. Even if the Magistrate had summoned the accused under Section 420 IPC instead, that too would have failed for want of ingredients.
  • Cheating and criminal breach of trust are mutually exclusive: The Court was emphatic that the two offences cannot co-exist on the same set of facts. If the accused is said to have dishonestly misappropriated entrusted property (breach of trust), the same facts cannot simultaneously support an allegation of deception from the very outset (cheating). The two provisions, the Court said, "are not twins that cannot survive without each other."
  • No vicarious liability for office bearers: The IPC contains no provision fixing vicarious criminal liability on office bearers of a company merely because the company is accused. The Secretary and Honorary President could only be personally proceeded against if the complaint contained direct, specific allegations against them — which it did not.
  • Magistrates must apply independent judicial mind: Summoning an accused is a serious step. The order must reflect genuine application of mind to whether the essential ingredients of the specific offence alleged are actually made out — not mechanical acceptance of the complainant's characterisation of the dispute.

The Court closed with an unusually pointed observation: despite 162 years of the IPC's existence, courts and police continue to conflate cheating with criminal breach of trust. It directed that police officers across the country be given proper training to understand this distinction, and sent copies of the judgment to the Union Law Secretary and Union Home Secretary.

Why this matters for Criminal practitioners in Haryana

This judgment is directly useful at the district court level, where complaints under Sections 406 and 420 IPC (now Sections 316 and 318 BNS) are routinely filed together as a matter of drafting habit, particularly in commercial payment disputes. Defence counsel appearing before the District Courts in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani now have clear Supreme Court authority to argue that a single transaction cannot attract both offences, and that a Magistrate's summoning order lacking specific reasoning on ingredients is vulnerable to quashing under Section 482 CrPC (now Section 528 BNSS).

For practitioners advising businesses and traders — a significant part of the Haryana district court docket — this case is a ready precedent to resist the criminalisation of what are, in substance, recovery suits. Clients owed money by counterparties who redirect payment obligations to related entities should be advised to pursue civil recovery, since courts will scrutinise criminal complaints for genuine entrustment and inception-stage dishonest intent rather than accept characterisations of "cheating" at face value.

The ruling on vicarious liability is equally significant for anyone defending directors, secretaries, or office bearers of companies, societies, or associations impleaded merely by virtue of their position — a common pattern in complaints arising from disputes with clubs, cooperative societies, and small firms across Haryana.

Statutory cross-references

Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, criminal breach of trust under old Section 406 IPC now corresponds to Section 316 BNS, and cheating under old Section 420 IPC corresponds to Section 318 BNS. The scope of magisterial inquiry under Section 202 CrPC is now governed by Section 225 BNSS, while the power to quash proceedings under Section 482 CrPC now sits at Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023. The Court's reasoning on the fine distinction between the two offences applies with equal force under these renumbered provisions, since the substantive ingredients of both offences remain unchanged under the BNS.

This is a significant Section 406 IPC Supreme Court ruling for anyone drafting or defending complaints involving unpaid consideration, and a useful citation alongside S.W. Palanitkar v. State of Bihar and Hari Prasad Chamaria v. Bishun Kumar Surekha on the entrustment and inception-intent requirements.
