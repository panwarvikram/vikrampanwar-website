XXX v. State of Kerala: Supreme Court Says Complaints Against Public Servants Must Be Backed by an Affidavit Under Section 175(4) BNSS

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — XXX v. State of Kerala & Ors.
BenchJustices Dipankar Datta & Manmohan
Date27 January 2026
Citation2026 INSC 88 : 2026(1) RCR (Criminal) 683 : 2026 SCC OnLine SC 114
Key SectionsSections 175(3), 175(4), 218 & 333, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023

What the case was about

This appeal arose out of deeply serious allegations: the appellant alleged that she was sexually assaulted by three police officers on separate occasions in 2022, in the course of pursuing an unrelated property dispute. After a complaint she made following the second incident produced an internal police report calling her allegations untrue, and little happened for two years, she renewed her efforts following a change in the district's Superintendent of Police in September 2024. She wrote to the local police station, escalated to the District Police Chief, and then moved the Judicial Magistrate First Class, Ponnani, under Section 210 read with Section 173(4) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS), seeking a direction to register an FIR against the officers.

Because the accused were police officers and the alleged acts touched on their official interactions with a complainant, the Magistrate invoked Section 175(4) of the BNSS — a wholly new provision with no precursor in the old Code of Criminal Procedure — and called for a report from a superior police officer before deciding whether to order investigation. While that process was still underway, the appellant separately approached the Kerala High Court by writ petition, asking it to declare that the protection under Section 175(4) does not extend to acts, like rape, that fall outside a public servant's official duties, and to direct registration of an FIR in line with this Court's earlier ruling in Lalita Kumari v. Govt. of U.P. A Single Judge agreed and directed the Magistrate to register an FIR within ten days; a Division Bench then reversed that order on appeal by one of the accused officers, holding that the writ court should not have interfered while the Magistrate's own proceedings were still pending. The appellant carried the matter to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The judgment, authored by Justice Dipankar Datta, does two distinct things: it resolves, for the first time, a genuinely difficult question of statutory interpretation around the brand-new Section 175(4) BNSS, and it separately corrects what the Court saw as a jurisdictional overreach by the writ court.

On the interpretation question, the Court held that sub-section (4) of Section 175 is not a standalone provision independent of sub-section (3), nor is it a proviso to it in the technical sense — it is best understood as an extension of sub-section (3), operating harmoniously with it. Sub-section (3) is the general provision empowering a Magistrate to order investigation, and it expressly requires the underlying application to be supported by an affidavit, reflecting the safeguard against frivolous complaints that this Court had earlier recognised in Priyanka Srivastava v. State of U.P. Sub-section (4) layers an additional, special safeguard on top of that general power specifically where the complaint concerns a public servant's acts in the discharge of official duty: before ordering investigation, the Magistrate must call for a report from an officer superior to the accused, and must consider the accused public servant's own version of events. Crucially, the Court held that because sub-section (4) merely adds to sub-section (3) rather than displacing it, the affidavit requirement in sub-section (3) continues to apply even to complaints against public servants — a magistrate cannot be more exacting with an ordinary citizen's complaint than with one directed at a police officer or other public servant. The Bench drew a telling parallel to the judiciary's own internal practice, noting that the Chief Justice of India's 2014 directive already requires complaints against judicial officers to be supported by sworn affidavits, and found no rational basis to hold public servants generally to a lower standard.

The judgment then lays down a practical, three-step guide for judicial magistrates confronted with a complaint against a public servant: first, determine whether the acts complained of were, or prima facie appear to have been, committed in the discharge of official duty; second, if so (or if there is genuine doubt), call for a report from the accused's superior officer and consider the accused's own version under Section 175(4); and third, in every case, ensure the underlying complaint is supported by an affidavit in the manner prescribed by Section 333, BNSS, before ordering investigation.

On the second question, the Court firmly held that the Single Judge of the Kerala High Court had exceeded his jurisdiction. The writ petition had prayed only for registration of an FIR and a declaration about the scope of Section 175(4) — it did not challenge the Magistrate's own order calling for a report. Since that order was a judicial order passed in a pending criminal proceeding, it could not be interfered with through a writ petition under Article 226 at all; the correct remedy, if any, lay under Section 528 of the BNSS or Article 227. A writ court, the Bench emphasised, cannot grant relief that was never prayed for, nor can it convert itself into a magisterial fact-finding forum by declaring, in the abstract, whether specific alleged acts fell within or outside the discharge of official duty. The Supreme Court therefore upheld the Division Bench's reversal, while leaving the appellant free to press all her contentions, including that the officers' acts were not in the discharge of official duty, before the Magistrate himself.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Section 175 BNSS applications for FIR registration are now a routine substitute for the old Section 156(3) CrPC applications used daily in Haryana's district courts, and complaints naming police officers, revenue officials, or other public servants as accused are far from rare. This judgment gives practitioners clear, citable guidance: any such application, even one invoking the special Section 175(4) procedure, must be supported by an affidavit, and magistrates are expected to follow the three-step sequence the Court has now laid down. Counsel drafting such applications should build the affidavit and the factual case for why the alleged conduct falls, or does not fall, within official duty from the outset, since that characterisation drives which procedural track the Magistrate must follow. This ruling is also a caution against premature writ petitions filed while a Section 175 application is still pending before a Magistrate.

Statutory cross-references

Sections 175(3) and 175(4) of the BNSS correspond to and expand upon Section 156(3) of the erstwhile Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, which contained no equivalent special safeguard for public servants. Section 218 of the BNSS, discussed in the judgment regarding the requirement of prior sanction before cognizance, corresponds to Section 197 of the CrPC, and its third proviso — excluding the sanction requirement for public servants accused of specified sexual offences — refers to Sections 64 to 79, 143, 199 and 200 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, which together cover rape and related offences formerly found in Sections 375 to 377 and related provisions of the Indian Penal Code, 1860.
