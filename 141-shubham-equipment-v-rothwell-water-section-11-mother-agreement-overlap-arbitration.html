Shubham Equipment v. Rothwell Water Company: Supreme Court Says Overlap With a “Mother Agreement” Cannot Block a Section 11 Reference at the Threshold (2026 INSC 1098)

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Shubham Equipment Pvt. Ltd. & Anr. v. Rothwell Water Company Ltd. & Ors.
BenchChief Justice Surya Kant, Justice Joymalya Bagchi and Justice V. Mohana
Date8 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1098; 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 1036
Key SectionsSection 11, Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (appointment of arbitrator)

What the case was about

Two Indian and Korean businesses had layered three separate contracts on top of one another, each with its own arbitration clause, and then fell out. Shubham Equipment Pvt. Ltd. (SEPL), a Thane-based tank-cleaning company, and Rothwell Water Company Ltd. (RWCL) of Korea first signed a Licence Agreement on 30 January 2019, with arbitration to be held in Seoul under Korean law. On 1 October 2021 they signed a Joint Venture Agreement (JVA) to set up an Indian company, Rothwell Water (India) Pvt. Ltd., with a Mumbai arbitration clause. A Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) dated 1 February 2023 added a services arrangement, again with a sole arbitrator seated in Mumbai.

In October 2024 SEPL terminated the JVA, alleging that RWCL had incorporated another Indian entity, Rothwell Business International Pvt. Ltd. (RBIPL), in breach of the non-compete clause. SEPL claimed about Rs 20 crore for business loss and a further Rs 4.24 crore under the MoU. After arbitration notices in November 2024, it filed two petitions under Section 11 for appointment of an arbitrator. The Supreme Court first sent the parties to mediation, which failed.

RWCL resisted on three fronts. It said the Licence Agreement was the “mother agreement” whose Seoul clause should govern everything; that RBIPL, a non-signatory, could not be dragged in; and that it had itself invoked arbitration in Korea in August 2025.

What the Court held

The Bench declined to treat the overlap as a reason to refuse a reference. A Section 11 court, it said, looks only at whether an arbitration agreement prima facie exists. Here the existence of all three agreements was admitted. Whether the JVA and MoU disputes also touch on rights under the Licence Agreement is a question for the arbitrator, not a gate at the appointment stage.

The Court also took the three contracts at face value: they operate, prima facie, in different fields. Disputes arising exclusively under the JVA and the MoU go to the Mumbai clauses in those documents. The JVA (Article 25) and the MoU (Clause 10.8) each contain a supersession term for their own subject matter, which supported keeping the disputes under their respective clauses rather than collapsing them into the Seoul arbitration.

On RBIPL, the Court noted that it is a wholly owned subsidiary of a signatory and was allegedly formed to defeat the JVA. Whether it is nevertheless bound is a contentious question, and the Court left it entirely to the tribunal. Former Bombay High Court Chief Justice Ramesh Deokinandan Dhanuka was appointed sole arbitrator.

Why this matters for civil and commercial practitioners in Haryana

Most commercial arbitration clauses we see in district-level practice are not as elaborate as this one, but the pattern is familiar: a master or distribution agreement, followed by a later purchase order, MoU or supplementary contract, each with slightly different dispute-resolution wording. The temptation at the Section 11 stage is to argue that the real dispute belongs under some other document, or involves a party who never signed. This ruling reminds us that such arguments are, in the first instance, for the arbitrator.

For the party seeking reference, the practical lesson is to anchor the claim in the specific agreement that carries the clause you are invoking, plead the supersession terms, and avoid sweeping in claims that clearly belong elsewhere. For the party resisting, the lesson is equally plain: preserve your objection on the overlap and on non-signatory status, but expect it to be decided in the arbitration, under Section 16 of the Act, and not to defeat the appointment. Since the Court deliberately kept the non-signatory question open, nothing here decides that RBIPL is bound.

Note also that the Court sent the parties to mediation before deciding the petitions. Where a clause or the circumstances allow it, expect courts to nudge settlement first, and keep records of any failed attempt at amicable resolution ready.

Statutory cross-references

The arbitration framework is unchanged by the 2023 criminal law reforms and continues to rest on the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996: Section 11 (appointment of arbitrators, where the Court examines only the prima facie existence of an arbitration agreement), Section 16 (the tribunal’s power to rule on its own jurisdiction, including the existence and scope of the agreement) and Section 8 (reference where an action is brought in a matter covered by an arbitration agreement). The Verdictum report of the judgment refers to the Section 11(6-A) standard; readers should check the current text and status of that sub-section before citing it, as it has been the subject of legislative amendment. No IPC/CrPC to BNS/BNSS mapping arises in this civil matter.
