Documents Needed to File for Divorce in India: A Practical Checklist

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseGeneral procedural guide — Family & District Courts, Haryana
BenchNot case-specific
DateAs of July 2026
CitationN/A — statutory and procedural guide
Key SectionsSs. 10, 13 & 13-B, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955; Ss. 27 & 28, Special Marriage Act, 1954; Family Courts Act, 1984

What this topic is about

One of the most common questions that walks into any district court chamber is not about legal doctrine at all — it is "what papers do I need to bring?" Clients arrive expecting a divorce filing to be as simple as handing over a marriage certificate. In practice, the paperwork required depends heavily on which route is being taken: a mutual consent divorce under Section 13-B of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 (or the equivalent provision under the Special Marriage Act, 1954), or a contested divorce filed on a fault ground under Section 13.

This article sets out, in practical terms, what a petitioner in Haryana should be assembling before the first date of filing, and why each document matters — not as a substitute for consulting a lawyer on the specifics of a case, but as a starting checklist.

What the legal position requires

For a mutual consent divorce, both spouses jointly petition the court, and the paperwork is comparatively lighter but still needs to be complete and consistent between the two parties. At minimum, courts in Haryana typically expect: proof of marriage (the marriage certificate, or in its absence, wedding photographs, invitation cards, and the priest's or registrar's record); identity and address proof of both spouses (Aadhaar card, PAN card, voter ID, or passport); recent passport-size photographs of both parties, including at least one joint photograph from the wedding; proof of residence establishing jurisdiction — i.e., that the marriage was solemnised, or that the parties last resided together, or that the respondent currently resides, within the territorial jurisdiction of the court where the petition is filed; and a settlement agreement or memorandum of understanding covering alimony, custody of children (if any), and division of assets, signed by both parties. Courts will also usually require income proof (salary slips, ITRs, or a Form 16) from both sides if any alimony or maintenance figure is being recorded as part of the settlement, since the court needs to satisfy itself that the terms are not one-sided or coerced.

Under Section 13-B(2) of the Hindu Marriage Act, a mutual consent petition also requires a second joint motion after a statutory waiting period — commonly six months from the date of filing, though the Supreme Court in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, (2017) 8 SCC 746, held that this cooling-off period can be waived by the court in appropriate cases where reconciliation efforts have clearly failed and the parties have genuinely settled all issues. Practitioners should be ready to move an application for waiver with supporting affidavits if the client wants to avoid the wait.

For a contested divorce filed on a fault ground — cruelty, desertion, adultery, conversion, unsoundness of mind, or the other grounds listed under Section 13 — the paperwork is necessarily heavier, because the petitioner has to plead and eventually prove the ground being relied upon. In addition to the marriage proof, identity documents, and address proof described above, a contested petition typically needs: any documentary evidence supporting the specific ground pleaded (medical records and injury reports for cruelty; police complaints or FIR copies where relevant; call records, messages, or witness details for adultery; proof of the date the parties last cohabited, for desertion); details and, where possible, documentary proof of any children of the marriage, including their date of birth certificates, since custody and maintenance for minor children will usually be decided alongside or ancillary to the divorce petition; an affidavit of assets and income (increasingly required by Family Courts under the maintenance guidelines laid down in Rajnesh v. Neha, (2021) 2 SCC 324); and, where a maintenance or interim maintenance application under Section 24 of the Hindu Marriage Act is being filed simultaneously, salary slips, bank statements, and tax returns for at least the preceding financial year.

Where the marriage was solemnised under the Special Marriage Act, 1954 rather than personal religious law, the same broad categories of documents apply, but the grounds and specific sections change to Sections 27 and 28 of that Act, and the certificate of marriage will typically be the one issued by the Marriage Officer at the time of registration rather than a religious ceremony record.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

In district practice at Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, the single biggest cause of delay at the filing stage is not a defect in the pleadings — it is an incomplete document set that forces a client to make repeated trips to gather papers piecemeal, often over several weeks, while the marriage certificate is chased from a gram panchayat or municipal registrar, or an old ITR is requested from an employer who has since changed hands. Preparing a document checklist at the very first client meeting, before drafting even begins, saves real time later and avoids embarrassing adjournments once the petition is listed.

It is also worth flagging jurisdiction early: many rural clients in this belt married in one district but currently reside in another after relocating for work, and the choice of forum under Section 19 of the Hindu Marriage Act (place of marriage, place of last cohabitation, or respondent's current residence, and for the wife, her own current residence) directly affects which documents will be needed to prove that jurisdictional fact — a marriage registration extract from one tehsil, a ration card or electricity bill from another, and so on. Getting this wrong at filing invites a preliminary objection that can stall a case for months.

Finally, where mutual consent is genuinely on the table, pushing early for a complete, signed settlement document — rather than filing with vague or partial terms and negotiating the fine print later — materially improves the odds of a smooth Section 13-B(2) second motion and reduces the risk of one party resiling before the waiting period ends.

Statutory cross-references

Divorce proceedings under personal law are governed by the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 (Ss. 10, 13, 13-B, 19 and 24) or, for civil/inter-faith marriages, the Special Marriage Act, 1954 (Ss. 27 and 28), and are conducted before Family Courts constituted under the Family Courts Act, 1984, or before the District Court exercising equivalent jurisdiction where no separate Family Court exists. These civil statutes are unaffected by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which apply to criminal proceedings; however, related criminal complaints — for instance under Section 85 of the BNS (cruelty by husband or relatives, the successor to Section 498-A IPC) — are sometimes filed alongside a divorce petition, and documentary evidence gathered for one proceeding often needs to be cross-referenced in the other.
