State (NCT) of Delhi v. Khimji Bhai Jadeja: Supreme Court Settles When Multiple FIRs Can Be Clubbed Into One

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — State (NCT) of Delhi v. Khimji Bhai Jadeja
BenchJustices Sanjay Kumar & Alok Aradhe
Date6 January 2026
Citation2026 INSC 25 · 2026(1) RCR(Criminal) 406 · 2026 AIR SC 507 · 2026 CriLJ 625 · 2026 SCC OnLine SC 19
Key SectionsSs. 218–223 & 395(2), CrPC · Ss. 120B & 420, IPC · S. 71, IPC · Ss. 31 & 325, CrPC

What the case was about

The Delhi Police's Economic Offences Wing registered FIR No. 89 of 2009 under Sections 420 and 120B IPC on a complaint by Rajesh Kumar, alleging that Ashok Jadeja and accomplices — including respondent Khimji Bhai Jadeja — falsely claimed Ashok Jadeja possessed the divine power of "Sikotar Mata" to triple an investor's money within days. Investigation revealed 1,852 people had been induced to invest on this promise and were cheated of Rs. 46.40 crore combined. Rather than registering 1,852 separate FIRs, the police treated the other 1,851 complaints as Section 161 CrPC witness statements within the same FIR, and filed a chargesheet against 15 persons in February 2014, followed by six supplementary chargesheets between 2014 and 2025.

When Khimji Bhai Jadeja sought bail in 2014, the Additional District & Sessions Judge framed three questions of law and referred them to the Delhi High Court under Section 395(2) CrPC: whether each deposit was a separate transaction requiring its own FIR; if so, how many transactions could be clubbed into one chargesheet; and whether treating all deposits as one transaction let the accused escape with a single offence's punishment. In 2019, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court answered that each deposit was a separate transaction requiring its own FIR and final report, amalgamable only at the charge-framing stage under Section 219 CrPC, capped at three offences. The State appealed; the Supreme Court stayed the judgment and appointed Senior Advocate R. Basant as amicus curiae, and the respondent never appeared despite notice.

What the court held

Justice Sanjay Kumar, writing for the Bench, reviewed the case law on what makes offences part of the "same transaction" for a joint trial under Sections 220(1) and 223(a) & (d) CrPC — from S. Swamirathnam (1957) and Cheemalapati Ganeswara Rao (1963), through T.T. Antony (2001) and Lalu Prasad Yadav alias Lalu Prasad (2017), to the recent run of Article 142 FIR-clubbing orders including Amish Devgan and Radhey Shyam. From this, the Court distilled a non-cumulative triple test: unity of purpose and design, proximity of time and place, and continuity of action — no single element is mandatory, but their presence together points strongly toward a single transaction. It also revisited Narinderjit Singh Sahni v. Union of India (2002), the precedent the Delhi High Court had relied on to hold 250 deposit-taking FIRs were all distinct transactions, and observed "with all due respect" that it lacked an in-depth analysis of the statutory scheme, with later three-Judge Bench decisions better reflecting today's settled position.

Applying this framework, the Court held the 2014 reference had been premature — investigation was still underway, and it could not have been determined then whether the 1,852 instances of cheating formed one transaction or many. By the time the matter reached the Supreme Court, six supplementary chargesheets over more than a decade disclosed a single alleged conspiracy under Section 120B IPC, so the Delhi Police's original approach — one FIR, with the other 1,851 complaints treated as Section 161 CrPC statements — was held correct at that stage. The Court set aside the High Court's answers to questions (a) and (b), and left it to the Magistrate to decide at the charge-framing stage whether the offences form the "same transaction" — entitling a joint trial, or requiring separate trials subject to Section 219 CrPC's offence-count cap. Sentencing, if tried jointly, must still respect Section 71 IPC and Sections 31 and 325 CrPC. Drawing on Amish Devgan, the Court reaffirmed that complainants treated as witnesses retain the right to file protest petitions if a closure report is filed or discharge is considered.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Multi-victim cheating and chit-fund/Ponzi-style prosecutions are not uncommon in Haryana's district courts, and this judgment gives a clear, citable framework for a recurring dispute: should numerous victims' complaints be treated as one case or many? Defence counsel often invoke "misjoinder" to fragment a prosecution, while investigators may push to consolidate FIRs for efficiency. The triple test — unity of purpose, proximity of time and place, continuity of action — gives both sides a structured way to argue the point, at charge-framing or in revision.

Equally important is the timing lesson: a challenge to how FIRs have been clubbed is unlikely to succeed while investigation is ongoing, since "same transaction" cannot meaningfully be answered until the chargesheet's contours are settled. Counsel considering a Section 395(2) CrPC reference, or its BNSS equivalent, should first confirm investigation has concluded — a premature reference risks the fate of this one. For fraud victims fearing their complaint will be buried as a mere witness statement, this judgment is reassurance: they retain standing to file a protest petition if the case does not proceed on the merits.

Statutory cross-references

The joinder-of-charges scheme discussed here sits in Sections 218 to 223 CrPC. Section 218 CrPC, mandating a separate charge and trial for every distinct offence, now corresponds to Section 241 BNSS; Section 219 CrPC — a joint trial for offences of the same kind within twelve months — is now Section 242 BNSS, with the cap raised from three offences to five, as the judgment notes. The reference-to-High-Court mechanism under Section 395(2) CrPC is now Section 436 BNSS. On the substantive side, Section 420 IPC (cheating) is now Section 318(4) BNS, Section 120B IPC (criminal conspiracy) is Section 61(2) BNS, and Section 71 IPC — limiting punishment where a single act amounts to several offences — is Section 9 BNS. As the FIR here was registered in 2009 and investigated almost entirely under the old Code, the case is a reminder that pending matters spanning the CrPC-to-BNSS transition will keep being litigated under the old numbering for years yet.
