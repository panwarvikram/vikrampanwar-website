How to File a Domestic Violence Complaint in India: Reliefs, Procedure, and What Counts as a "Shared Household"

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing LawProtection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005
ForumJudicial Magistrate First Class / Metropolitan Magistrate
Leading CasesHiral P. Harsora v. Kusum Narottamdas Harsora, (2016) 10 SCC 165 · Satish Chander Ahuja v. Sneha Ahuja, (2021) 1 SCC 414
AppealCourt of Session, within 30 days
Key SectionsSs. 3, 12, 17–23, 28 & 29, Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005

What the topic is about

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 ("DV Act") is the statute most women in Haryana actually reach for when a marriage or domestic relationship turns abusive — not because it sends anyone to jail (it rarely does, on its own), but because it is fast, civil in character despite being tried by a criminal court, and built around what a woman in that situation usually needs first: a roof, some money, and an order that stops the abuse without waiting years for a divorce to conclude. A large share of the maintenance, residence, and protection applications filed before Judicial Magistrates in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani trace back to it.

Section 3 defines "domestic violence" broadly to cover physical, sexual, verbal, emotional, and economic abuse — the last covering things like being deprived of household necessities, being stopped from working, or having stridhan and jointly-owned assets withheld. It applies to any woman who is or has been in a "domestic relationship" with the respondent — through marriage, a relationship in the nature of marriage, consanguinity, adoption, or a joint family — reaching beyond wives to daughters, sisters, mothers, and women in live-in relationships, provided they share or have shared a household with the respondent.

What the legal position is

An application under Section 12 can be filed by the aggrieved woman herself, by a Protection Officer on her behalf, or by any other person acting for her welfare, before the Magistrate having jurisdiction where the aggrieved person or the respondent resides or works, or where the cause of action arose — a deliberately wide venue that lets a woman who has fled the matrimonial home file from wherever she has taken shelter. Before deciding, the Magistrate must consider any Domestic Incident Report (DIR) from a Protection Officer or service provider; in practice, a woman typically first approaches the local Protection Officer (appointed district-wise under Section 8), who records the DIR and helps prepare the application, though going directly to the Magistrate is equally permissible.

Chapter IV of the Act sets out the reliefs a Magistrate can grant, and this is where the statute earns its reputation as a fast, front-loaded remedy: a protection order under Section 18 restraining further violence or entry into the aggrieved person's workplace or the children's school; a residence order under Section 19 restraining dispossession from the shared household or directing alternate accommodation, which can extend even to relatives of the respondent; monetary relief under Section 20 covering loss of earnings, medical expenses, and maintenance, payable as a lump sum or periodically; a custody order under Section 21; and compensation under Section 22 for injury, including mental torture and emotional distress. Critically, Section 23 lets the Magistrate pass any of these as an interim, ex-parte order on the aggrieved person's affidavit alone, before the respondent is even heard, wherever the application discloses domestic violence has taken place or is likely to.

Two Supreme Court rulings shape how these provisions operate on the ground. In Hiral P. Harsora v. Kusum Narottamdas Harsora, (2016) 10 SCC 165, the Court struck down "adult male" from the definition of "respondent" in Section 2(q) as arbitrary and violative of Article 14 — so a complaint can now be maintained against female relatives too, such as a mother-in-law or sister-in-law, not only against men. In Satish Chander Ahuja v. Sneha Ahuja, (2021) 1 SCC 414, the Court overruled its narrower reading in S.R. Batra v. Taruna Batra and held that a "shared household" under Section 2(s) is not confined to a house owned by the husband or joint family — it covers any household where the woman has lived in a domestic relationship, regardless of whose name the property stands in. This considerably widened residence protection for daughters-in-law living in a father-in-law's self-acquired house, a pattern common in Haryana's joint-family households.

Proceedings under Sections 12 and 18 to 23, and the offence of breaching a protection order under Section 31, are governed procedurally by the code applicable at the time (the CrPC earlier, now the BNSS), though Section 28(2) lets the Magistrate lay down a simpler procedure for a Section 12 application — part of why DV Act cases move faster than a full trial. An order can be appealed to the Court of Session within thirty days under Section 29.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

In district court practice, the DV Act is rarely filed alone — it usually runs alongside a Section 125 BNSS (formerly CrPC) maintenance petition, a divorce case, or a cruelty complaint under Section 85 BNS (which replaced Section 498-A IPC). Advocates should treat the DV Act application as the first line of relief precisely because Section 23 allows an ex-parte interim order on affidavit alone, securing residence and interim maintenance within days — well before a maintenance petition or divorce suit would yield anything.

On the defence side, the two cases above come up most often: a mother-in-law or sister-in-law can no longer argue that only "adult males" can be proceeded against post-Harsora, and a respondent resisting a residence claim on the ground that the house belongs to his father will find that weakened after Satish Chander Ahuja, unless the facts show no genuine domestic relationship existed at all. Since complaints here often name the entire in-law household, both sides should plead the specific domestic relationship and specific acts with real particularity — vague, omnibus allegations invite an equally omnibus denial, and Magistrates increasingly expect applications to identify who did what, and when.

Statutory cross-references

The DV Act is a standalone civil-remedial statute, unamended and unrenumbered by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita or Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. Its procedural backbone under Section 28 now refers to the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023 in place of the CrPC, 1973, and breach of a protection order under Section 31 continues to be tried as a criminal complaint under that procedure. Where a DV Act complaint is combined with a cruelty allegation, the relevant provision is now Section 85 (with Section 86 defining cruelty) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, corresponding to the erstwhile Section 498-A IPC.
