ONGC v. Suryakand D. Lad & Ors.: Supreme Court Says a Principal Employer With No Direct Hiring Link Cannot Be Made to Pay a Contract Worker's Gratuity

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India · M/s Oil and Natural Gas Corporation Ltd. v. Suryakand D. Lad & Ors. (with connected appeals)
BenchJustices Ahsanuddin Amanullah & Manmohan
DateReported 20–21 September 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (SC) 961
Key SectionsS. 4, Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 · S. 21(4), Contract Labour (Regulation & Abolition) Act, 1970 · S. 2(vi), Payment of Wages Act, 1936

What the case was about

A group of workers engaged through a contractor to work at Oil and Natural Gas Corporation Ltd. (ONGC) sites filed claims for gratuity, naming ONGC itself as the party liable to pay. ONGC's position throughout was that it had no employer-employee relationship with these individuals at all — they had been supplied by a contractor under a job-work arrangement, and Clause 12.4.1 of ONGC's contract with that contractor expressly recorded that the arrangement did not create any direct employment relationship between ONGC and the contractor's workers. Despite that objection, and despite ONGC separately contesting whether the Controlling Authority under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 even had jurisdiction to decide who was liable to pay, the Controlling Authority ruled against ONGC and held it liable for the gratuity claimed.

ONGC appealed, and the Appellate Authority under the Gratuity Act reversed that finding in ONGC's favour. The workers then moved the Bombay High Court, which set aside the Appellate Authority's order and restored the Controlling Authority's finding that ONGC was liable. That left ONGC to approach the Supreme Court, arguing the case on what it called "the short point" of whether the Controlling Authority had any jurisdiction to adjudicate liability in the first place, rather than simply computing the amount payable to an established employee.

What the court held

A Bench of Justices Ahsanuddin Amanullah and Manmohan sided with ONGC and restored the Appellate Authority's order. Solicitor General Tushar Mehta, appearing for ONGC, built the argument on three limbs: first, that Section 4 of the Payment of Gratuity Act makes gratuity payable only to an "employee," and the claimants were never ONGC's employees; second, that Section 21(4) of the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970 fixes a principal employer's responsibility toward contract labour at wages alone, a distinct statutory component under Section 2(vi) of the Payment of Wages Act, 1936, and does not extend that responsibility to gratuity; and third, that the contract's own terms recorded the arrangement as a job contract at arm's length, not an employment relationship. The Court also drew on its own 2025 ruling in Municipal Council, Nandyal Municipality, Kurnool District, AP v. K. Jayaram & Ors., which had already held that a person supplied through a contractor cannot claim an employer-employee relationship with the entity that engaged the contractor.

On the jurisdictional point, the Bench held squarely that the Controlling Authority's statutory power under the Gratuity Act is limited to computing the amount of gratuity payable to a person who is already established as an "employee" — it has no power to first decide, as a contested question, who that employee's employer actually is and fasten liability on a third party. Since the entire proceeding before the Controlling Authority had turned on deciding ONGC's liability rather than merely quantifying a sum owed by an admitted employer, the Court held that "the adjudication by the Controlling Authority with regard to the liability was beyond its jurisdiction," and that the Bombay High Court had therefore been wrong to reverse the Appellate Authority's order. Notably, since ONGC had already paid the gratuity amounts claimed by the workers while the litigation proceeded, the Bench directed that no recovery be made from them despite ruling in ONGC's favour on the underlying question of liability.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Contract labour arrangements are common across Haryana's industrial belt and in government-linked establishments, and disputes over who actually owes statutory dues to outsourced workers — the contractor or the principal employer — recur regularly before Labour Courts, Controlling Authorities under the Gratuity Act, and in writ proceedings that follow. This judgment gives practitioners representing either side a clear two-step framework: first, whether an actual employer-employee relationship exists between the principal employer and the worker, which will usually turn on the contract's own terms and whether the principal employer directly supervises, pays, or controls the worker's engagement; and second, and separately, whether the forum being approached even has jurisdiction to decide that liability question at all, or whether it is confined to computing an amount once liability is otherwise established or admitted.

For advocates advising outsourcing agencies, contractors, or principal employers in Haryana on drafting or defending these arrangements, the judgment underscores the practical value of a clearly worded contract clause disclaiming any direct employment relationship between the principal and the contractor's workers — language the Supreme Court treated as a relevant, credible indicator rather than a mere formality. Equally, for advocates representing contract workers seeking gratuity, this ruling signals that claims should be carefully routed through the correct forum and framed to establish the employer-employee relationship as a threshold fact, rather than assuming a Controlling Authority can resolve a genuinely contested liability dispute as part of computing the payable amount.

Statutory cross-references

The Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 and the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970 remain in force as central labour legislation and have no BNS/BNSS equivalent, since both are civil and regulatory statutes rather than criminal ones. Practitioners should note that the Code on Social Security, 2020, once brought into force, is intended to consolidate gratuity provisions along with several other labour welfare statutes, though the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 continues to govern gratuity claims as of this writing.
