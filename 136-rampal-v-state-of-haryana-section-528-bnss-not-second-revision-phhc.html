Rampal v. State of Haryana: Punjab & Haryana High Court Says a Section 528 BNSS Petition Cannot Be a “Disguised Second Revision” Against a Sessions Court Order in a Land-Possession Dispute

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab and Haryana High Court — Rampal & Others v. State of Haryana & Others
BenchJustice Sumeet Goel (single judge)
DateReported as 25 September 2026 by one outlet and 29 September 2026 by another (likely the date of publication)
CitationCRM-M-56291-2024 (O&M) — no neutral citation seen; not independently confirmed
Key SectionsSection 528 BNSS, 2023 · Sections 438 and 442 BNSS (revision) · Sections 164–165 BNSS (land disputes likely to cause breach of peace)

What the case was about

The dispute concerned about 514 kanals of land in Village Gohana, Kaithal. The petitioners said they held it under a registered 99-year lease dated 6 June 1970. In 2008 they discovered that the revenue entries had been altered in favour of a samadh, which is a religious institution. They went to the civil court, and that suit did not go their way. After an attempted forcible taking of possession in 2015, the Sub-Divisional Magistrate attached the land and appointed a receiver. That order was set aside, but a fresh order in 2018 directed the receiver to hand possession to the samadh.

The petitioners challenged the 2018 order in revision before the Sessions Court, which dismissed it on 26 July 2024. They then came to the High Court under Section 528 BNSS, the successor to Section 482 CrPC, asking it to undo both orders.

What the court held

Justice Sumeet Goel dismissed the petition. The central proposition is that when a petitioner uses the inherent jurisdiction of the High Court to attack an order that has already been through revision, the petition operates in an “extraordinarily constricted domain”. If the petitioner is simply asking the High Court to take a different view of the same facts, the petition “virtually morphs into a disguised second revision”, which the statute does not permit.

To get in at that stage, the petitioner must point to an egregious failure of justice or a glaring jurisdictional defect, or a patent illegality, procedural impropriety or manifest miscarriage of justice. Disagreement with how evidence was appreciated is not enough. Here the court found that the revisional court had examined all the material, that no perversity or jurisdictional error was shown, and that the questions of possession, title and the meaning of the lease were disputed factual matters that sit poorly in summary proceedings of this kind.

The reasoning reflects a settled tension in criminal procedure. The revisional jurisdiction of the Sessions Court is itself a supervisory check on the magistrate, and the law deliberately limits a party to one revision so that litigation over a single order does not run through three tiers. Inherent power exists to prevent abuse of process and to secure the ends of justice, not to give an unsuccessful revision petitioner a back-door appeal. By insisting on a threshold showing, the High Court protects that design while keeping an exit for the rare case where the revisional order is plainly unsustainable in law. It also signals that parties who lost on possession in the summary forum should not expect the High Court to resolve questions such as the true effect of a 1970 lease, which a civil court is better placed to decide on full evidence.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Many petitions that reach the High Court from Haryana’s district courts are really civil disputes that travelled through the criminal side: proceedings over possession under the old Sections 145 and 146 CrPC, attachment, receivers, or complaints that are a continuation of a title fight. This ruling is a clear warning that going from the Sessions Court to the High Court in an inherent-power petition is not a free second bite. Counsel should assess before filing whether there is a real jurisdictional point or a legal error visible on the face of the record, and should frame the petition around that rather than around a re-argument on facts.

For practitioners resisting such petitions, the order supplies a ready citation for the proposition that Section 528 is not an appellate remedy. At the same time, it does not shut the door: a true jurisdictional defect or a gross miscarriage of justice still justifies interference, and the ruling is careful to say so. Where title is genuinely in dispute, the safer course remains a civil declaration or injunction suit and, where relevant, a properly founded challenge to the magistrate’s possession order at the first stage rather than years later.

Statutory cross-references

Section 528 BNSS corresponds to Section 482 CrPC (inherent powers of the High Court). Revision by the Sessions Court and High Court, Sections 397 and 401 CrPC, is now found in Sections 438 and 442 BNSS, and the bar on a second revision by the same party, Section 397(3) CrPC, appears as Section 438(3) BNSS. The magistrate’s power to deal with land disputes likely to cause a breach of the peace, earlier Sections 145 and 146 CrPC, is now Sections 164 and 165 BNSS.
