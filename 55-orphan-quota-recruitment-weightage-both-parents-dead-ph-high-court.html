HSSC Recruitment Case: P&H High Court Says "Orphan" Means Both Parents Dead, Not Just the Father, Settling the Fate of Thousands of Haryana Police Jobs

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — CWP-11590-2019 and 51 connected appeals (HSSC recruitment weightage matter)
BenchJustices Harsimran Singh Sethi & Amarinder Singh Grewal (Division Bench)
Date20 July 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 249
Key SectionsHSSC Advertisement No. 3 of 2018 · Haryana's 2003 Rules for compassionate assistance to families of deceased government employees

What the case was about

In 2018, the Haryana Staff Selection Commission advertised a large recruitment drive for the state police — 5,000 male constables, 1,147 female constables, 500 constables for the Indian Reserve Battalions, and 463 Sub-Inspectors. Among the eligibility conditions was a weightage of five extra marks for candidates who were "orphans" or widows. HSSC's own advertisement defined an orphan strictly: both parents dead, and specifically, the father's death must have occurred either before he turned 42 or before the child turned 15.

A number of candidates whose mothers were still alive challenged this definition, arguing that since the clause referred only to the father's death and age, a living mother should not disqualify them from the five-mark weightage. In May 2023, a Single Judge of the High Court agreed and directed HSSC to rework the merit list on that reading — a decision that put the jobs of already-selected constables and sub-inspectors at risk, since a reworked list threatened to push some of them out in favour of candidates who had claimed the wrongly interpreted orphan benefit. Both HSSC and the affected selected candidates appealed, and the resulting batch of appeals — 51 connected matters in all — came up before a Division Bench.

What the court held

The Division Bench of Justices Harsimran Singh Sethi and Amarinder Singh Grewal sided with HSSC and set aside the Single Judge's order. The Court's reasoning rested on two pillars. First, it applied ordinary principles of interpreting a recruitment advertisement: where the language of an eligibility clause is clear, courts must read it by its plain meaning and by the sense the author of the clause — here, the State — intended it to carry. Reading additional flexibility into a clause that the drafting authority did not intend, the Bench held, would amount to courts engaging in "judicial legislation," which is not a function courts are meant to perform.

Second, and more substantively, the Bench found that the HSSC's definition was not an arbitrary or ad hoc restriction invented for this recruitment. It traced back to Haryana's own 2003 rules governing compassionate assistance to the families of deceased government employees, which had defined "orphan" the same way years before the 2018 police recruitment drive: a child becomes an orphan only on losing the second parent, having already lost the first. That pre-existing statutory definition, the Court said, could not simply be brushed aside in favour of a more generous reading urged by candidates years later. The Bench was also unmoved by the argument that certain district-level recruitment committees had, in a separate Class-IV recruitment exercise, given the same five marks to candidates with a living mother — an isolated wrong interpretation by a subordinate committee, the Court held, does not bind HSSC or override the State's consistently stated position. The Single Judge's order was set aside, and HSSC's original interpretation — both parents dead, father's death before 42 or before the candidate turned 15 — was restored across all 51 connected matters.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Recruitment and service-law writ petitions are a steady part of practice before the Haryana bench of the High Court, and this judgment is a useful reference point on two fronts. The first is interpretive: when an eligibility or weightage clause in a government advertisement is challenged as ambiguous, this ruling reinforces that courts will look first to the plain language the recruiting authority used, and second to any pre-existing statutory or rule-based definition already in force, before entertaining a more expansive reading urged by an aggrieved candidate. Counsel drafting a challenge to a recruitment condition should therefore search out whether the term in dispute already has a settled definition elsewhere in the State's rules — as happened here with the 2003 compassionate-assistance rules — since that existing definition is likely to be treated as decisive.

The second, more practical lesson concerns the risk that reopening a merit list years after selections have been finalised carries for already-appointed candidates. This case shows the disruption a single writ petition can cause across an entire recruitment cycle when a beneficial clause is read too broadly at the first instance, and the multi-year uncertainty (2018 advertisement, 2023 Single Judge order, 2026 Division Bench correction) that selected candidates were left to live with in the interim. Advocates representing either a challenger to an eligibility clause or an already-selected candidate whose position is threatened by such a challenge should move quickly to get connected matters heard together, exactly as happened with these 51 appeals, rather than letting parallel petitions proceed on inconsistent timelines.

Statutory cross-references

This dispute turns on the language of an executive recruitment advertisement and a set of state compassionate-assistance rules rather than the BNS/BNSS/BSA framework, so there is no direct criminal-law cross-reference here. The broader constitutional basis for such service-law challenges remains Articles 14 and 16 of the Constitution (equality and equality of opportunity in public employment), invoked through a writ petition under Article 226 before the High Court, exactly as the original CWP-11590-2019 was filed.
