Madhu Kudapa v. Lodha Healthy Constructions: Supreme Court Says a Homebuyer Who Takes a Refund With Interest Cannot Also Claim Rent for the Same Builder Delay (2026 INSC 1131)

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India (Civil Appeals against NCDRC order)
BenchJustice Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha & Justice Alok Aradhe
Date15 October 2026 (judgment uploaded 16 October 2026)
Citation2026 INSC 1131
Key SectionsConsumer Protection Act, 1986 (deficiency in service); Section 73, Indian Contract Act, 1872 (compensation for breach); Clause 24 of the apartment buyer’s agreement

What the case was about

In 2009 a Hyderabad buyer booked an apartment in the Lodha Bellezza project for about ₹2.30 crore. He paid roughly ₹51 lakh from his own pocket, and a bank loan of ₹2 crore was sanctioned, of which about ₹1.68 crore went straight to the builder under a tripartite arrangement. By July 2011 the builder had collected close to 95 per cent of the price. Possession, however, was never delivered within the promised time.

In 2013 the buyer went to the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC) asking for a refund with interest, reimbursement of rent he had paid for alternative housing, further rent going forward, and ₹15 lakh as compensation. The NCDRC ordered a refund with 9 per cent interest (rising to 12 per cent if not paid within three months) but did not decide the rent and compensation claims. Cross-appeals by the buyer, the builder and the bank reached the Supreme Court.

What the Court held

The Bench modified the NCDRC order. The refund of ₹1,27,34,726 is to be paid with 12 per cent simple interest from the dates of the respective payments, and the Court held that interest on the refund and rent for the same stretch of delay cannot both be awarded, because that would compensate the buyer twice for one default.

On rent, the Court reasoned that the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 contains no provision like the rent-control statutes that gives a tenant a free-standing right to claim rent. Compensation has to match a loss that is actually proved and that flows from the service provider’s default. Here the buyer had raised an enlarged rent figure (₹38,000 a month up to July 2013 and ₹65,000 a month from August 2013 to October 2022) only in his rejoinder, backed by a 2021 bank statement, with no lease deed or rent receipts before the NCDRC.

The Court also leaned on Clause 24 of the agreement, which gave the buyer two choices: stay in the project and receive compensation at 12 per cent until possession, or terminate and take a refund with 12 per cent simple interest. By asking for a refund he had chosen the second route, and rent paid afterwards was an ordinary living cost rather than a loss caused by non-delivery. The Bench noted the inconsistency in first pleading rent he could have earned by letting the flat out and then claiming rent he paid for his own residence.

Even so, the Court recognised that the buyer had been kept out of both his money and the flat for over 15 years and had lost the benefit of price escalation. It therefore awarded a lump sum of ₹15 lakh as full and final compensation for rent, mental agony, harassment and litigation costs, payable within one month. The ₹2 lakh compensation against the bank for deficiency in service was left undisturbed, and the balance of the deposited amount is to stay in an interest-bearing account pending the bank’s separate appeal in the High Court.

Why this matters for Civil and consumer practitioners in Haryana

Builder-buyer complaints are a staple before the District Consumer Commissions that serve Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, and the Gurugram, Faridabad and Sonepat projects bring many Haryana buyers before the State Commission and NCDRC as well. The practical lesson is about how the prayer is drafted and proved. Interest on the refund is the Court’s default measure of compensation for the time value of money, so a separate claim for rent, or for loss of rental income, has to be pleaded in the original complaint, supported by lease deeds, receipts and bank records, and framed so it is not simply the same loss counted twice.

The ruling also warns against quantifying a claim for the first time in a rejoinder, and against pleading inconsistent theories of loss. Counsel should decide early whether the client is staying in the project (and claiming delay compensation under the agreement) or exiting for a refund, because the Court treated that election as shaping everything that follows. Where a bank has disbursed the loan directly to the builder, the order also reminds us that the refund and the lender’s share must be kept distinct while directions are drafted.

For defence counsel, the case is a useful authority against inflated, undocumented rent claims. For complainants, it shows that a well-evidenced case of long delay can still justify a lump-sum award above the bare refund and interest.

Statutory cross-references

The complaint was governed by the Consumer Protection Act, 1986, which has since been replaced by the Consumer Protection Act, 2019; the forum structure (District, State, NCDRC) and the concept of deficiency in service carry forward, so the reasoning on proof and double recovery remains relevant to pending and new complaints. Section 73 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (damages for breach, limited to loss naturally arising from the breach) was the Court’s yardstick for compensation. The Court also referred to earlier decisions such as DLF Homes Panchkula v. D.S. Dhanda (2020) on the rule against awarding interest or compensation by rule of thumb.
