Succession Certificate vs. Legal Heir Certificate in India: What Each One Actually Proves, and Which You Need

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing LawSs. 372–390, Indian Succession Act, 1925 (succession certificate); state revenue rules (legal heir certificate)
Issuing AuthorityDistrict/Civil Court (succession certificate); Tehsildar/Revenue Officer (legal heir certificate)
ProvesRight to collect debts/securities (succession certificate); relationship to deceased only (legal heir certificate)
Typical Timeline3–7+ months, contested cases longer (succession certificate); 15–45 days (legal heir certificate)
Key SectionsSs. 372, 373, 381 & 383, Indian Succession Act, 1925

What the problem actually is

Someone dies without a Will, and the family that's left behind is handed a bank account, a fixed deposit, a provident fund balance, or a share certificate they can't touch until they prove, on paper, who is entitled to it. Almost every client who walks into a district court practice with this problem starts by asking for "the certificate" — without realising there are two very different documents that go by similar names, issued by different authorities, doing different jobs, and one is not a substitute for the other.

A legal heir certificate is a revenue document. It is issued by a Tehsildar, Naib-Tehsildar, or equivalent Revenue Officer (in Haryana, typically at the sub-tehsil or tehsil office) after a local inquiry, and it simply lists who the deceased's legal heirs are under the applicable personal law. It is quick, inexpensive, and useful for a narrow set of purposes — transferring a ration card, claiming a deceased government employee's pending salary or gratuity in some departments, applying for a family pension, or processing certain smaller insurance claims where the insurer's own policy accepts it. Different states and departments treat it with different degrees of trust, and it can typically be issued within a few weeks of application.

A succession certificate is an entirely different animal. It is a judicial order passed by a competent civil court under Part X of the Indian Succession Act, 1925, and it does one specific job: it authorises the holder to collect the deceased's debts and securities — bank balances, fixed deposits, shares, mutual funds, insurance payouts, provident fund dues — and gives whoever pays out on that authority full legal protection for having done so.

What the law actually says, and how a petition proceeds

Section 372 of the Act sets out who can apply (any person claiming to be entitled to the debts and securities of the deceased, or to administer them), what the petition must contain (the deceased's time of death, family and heirs, their respective rights, the debts and securities in question, and an averment that no other application for the same relief is pending), and where it must be filed — ordinarily the District Judge within whose jurisdiction the deceased ordinarily resided at death, or, if no fixed residence, where any part of the property is found.

Once filed, Section 373 requires the court to issue notice, fix a time for hearing, and direct notice to be published and served on all persons the court considers should have it — typically the other legal heirs. If the petition is unopposed and the court is satisfied about the applicant's right, a succession certificate is granted specifying the debts and securities it covers. If it is contested — which happens routinely when siblings disagree on who should hold the certificate, or when a second family or a disputed marriage is in the picture — the matter proceeds much like a regular civil trial on the limited question of entitlement, and can run for many months before a certificate is finally granted, sometimes on furnishing security under Section 375.

Section 381 is what gives the succession certificate its real weight: it protects any person who, acting in good faith, pays out a debt or hands over a security to the certificate holder, even if it later turns out that someone else had a better claim. That statutory indemnity is exactly why banks and companies insist on a succession certificate, and not a legal heir certificate, before releasing money above a certain threshold, or where the account has no nomination and no other clear settlement mechanism in place. Section 383 also lets the court revoke a certificate later — for defective procedure, for the certificate being obtained fraudulently, or for it becoming useless or inoperative — which underlines that this is a judicial grant, subject to judicial correction, not a one-time administrative formality.

Why this matters for practitioners in Haryana

A large share of succession certificate work in district court practice in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani comes from families who first tried the legal heir certificate route at the tehsil office, only to be told by a bank or a co-operative society that it isn't sufficient for releasing a deceased account holder's balance above the bank's internal limit. Setting client expectations correctly at the first meeting — which document actually does what, and which authority to approach for which asset — saves months of back-and-forth.

It is also worth flagging to clients early that a succession certificate does not decide title to immovable property, and it does not resolve disputes about who inherits what share of agricultural land or a house. It only authorises collection of debts and securities. Where the family dispute is really about land or a house, the correct remedy is a partition suit or a suit for declaration of title, not a succession certificate petition, and running the wrong proceeding wastes both time and the client's money.

Where the succession petition is contested, the drafting has to do real work: a vague averment about "legal heirs" invites objections from every relative left out or under-described, and courts in this jurisdiction are careful about ensuring notice has actually reached everyone with a stake before granting the certificate, precisely because Section 381's indemnity depends on the grant being sound. Getting the family tree, the dates, and every heir's share right at the petition stage is worth the extra care it takes.

Statutory cross-references

This area of law sits entirely within the Indian Succession Act, 1925 (Ss. 372–390) for succession certificates, and state-specific revenue administration rules for legal heir certificates — neither has been touched by the BNS/BNSS, 2023 reforms, which apply to criminal law. Where succession disputes involve agricultural land specifically, practitioners should also read the applicable state land revenue code (for Haryana, the Punjab Land Revenue Act, 1887, as applicable) alongside the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, or the relevant personal law governing intestate succession for the family in question, since the succession certificate proceeding itself does not determine substantive inheritance shares — it only follows what the applicable personal law already says about them.
