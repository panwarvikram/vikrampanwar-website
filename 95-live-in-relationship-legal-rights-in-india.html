Live-In Relationships in India: What the Law Actually Protects, and Where It Stops Short

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Leading CasesTulsa & Ors. v. Durghatiya & Ors.; D. Velusamy v. D. Patchaiammal; Indra Sarma v. V.K.V. Sarma
CourtSupreme Court of India
Date2008 – 2013 (doctrine remains current law)
Citation(2008) 4 SCC 520 · (2010) 10 SCC 469 · (2013) 15 SCC 755
Key SectionsS. 2(f), Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 · S. 16, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 · S. 144, BNSS, 2023

What the question actually is

Live-in relationships have never been illegal in India. Two consenting adults living together without marrying is protected as an aspect of the right to personal liberty under Article 21 of the Constitution, and courts have said so repeatedly since the Supreme Court's observations in cases like S. Khushboo v. Kanniammal. But "not illegal" is a much narrower statement than "legally equivalent to marriage," and the gap between those two ideas is where most of the real disputes in this area actually live. A woman who has spent fifteen years in a live-in relationship and is then abandoned does not automatically get a wife's rights to maintenance, or a widow's rights to inherit, or a legitimate child's rights for any children born of the relationship, simply because Article 21 protected her choice to cohabit in the first place. Each of those entitlements has had to be worked out separately, through separate statutes and separate lines of Supreme Court authority, and each comes with real conditions attached. This piece maps what is actually settled, since it's one of the areas where popular belief about the law runs well ahead of what courts have actually held.

What the law holds: maintenance, property and children

On maintenance, the route that actually works is the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005. Section 2(f) of the Act defines a "domestic relationship" to include a relationship "in the nature of marriage," and in D. Velusamy v. D. Patchaiammal, the Supreme Court laid down the conditions a live-in relationship must satisfy to count as one: the couple must hold themselves out to society as akin to spouses; both must be of legal age to marry; both must otherwise qualify to enter a legal marriage, including being unmarried; and they must have voluntarily cohabited and held themselves out to the world as being akin to spouses for a significant period of time. A relationship that is casual, purely for pleasure, or one where one partner is already validly married to someone else, does not qualify. In Indra Sarma v. V.K.V. Sarma, the Court applied these conditions to deny DV Act relief to a woman who had lived with a man she knew to be already married at the time the relationship began — a "relationship in the nature of marriage" cannot be built on a foundation where one party was never free to marry the other. Where the Velusamy conditions are met, though, the woman can claim maintenance, a shared household, and protection orders exactly as a wife could. Beyond the DV Act, courts have also read "wife" in Section 125 CrPC (now Section 144 BNSS) expansively enough, in appropriate long-term cohabitation cases, to allow a maintenance claim, though this route is less settled and far more fact-dependent than the DV Act route.

Property is where live-in partners have the least protection, and where the gap with marriage is widest. There is no equivalent of a spouse's statutory succession rights for a live-in partner. Self-acquired property stays with whoever's name it is in, and a partner who contributed money, labour or care towards its acquisition has to prove that contribution independently — typically by showing a joint purchase, a joint bank account funding the purchase, or an express or clearly implied agreement to share ownership. Velusamy itself flagged this contribution-based route without guaranteeing it; it is a claim a partner has to build with documentary proof, not an entitlement that arises automatically from having lived together.

Children born of a live-in relationship stand on considerably firmer ground than either the maintenance or property questions. In Tulsa v. Durghatiya, the Supreme Court held that where a man and woman have lived together for a long period as husband and wife, the law presumes, in their favour, that they were validly married — a presumption that can be rebutted, but only by strong, cogent evidence to the contrary. Once that presumption holds, or independently of it, Section 16 of the Hindu Marriage Act treats children of a void or voidable marriage as legitimate for all purposes, including inheritance. The Supreme Court's later ruling in Revanasiddappa v. Mallikarjun went further, holding that such children can even inherit coparcenary property, not merely their parents' self-acquired assets. In short: however precarious a live-in partner's own claim to maintenance or property might be, the couple's children are not left exposed by their parents' choice not to formalise the marriage.

Why this matters for practitioners in Haryana

Live-in relationships in Haryana's district courts most often surface in one of three postures: a habeas corpus or protection petition filed by an inter-caste or inter-faith couple fearing family retaliation, a DV Act maintenance application filed once a long relationship breaks down, or an inheritance dispute where collateral relatives challenge a live-in partner's child's claim to ancestral property. For the first category, the couple's entitlement to police protection rests on Article 21 and doesn't require them to prove anything about the Velusamy conditions at all — adult, consenting cohabitation is enough. For the second category, counsel should build the record around the Velusamy factors from the outset: joint bank accounts, a shared address on identity documents, social acknowledgment of the relationship, duration, and absence of a subsisting prior marriage on either side. Thin proof on these points is exactly where DV Act maintenance claims fail in practice. For the third category, Tulsa and Section 16 HMA give a strong starting position for the child, but the presumption of marriage is rebuttable, so the opposing side's strategy is usually to attack the "long cohabitation held out as marriage" foundation directly, and defending counsel should be ready to meet that with witnesses and documentary proof of the relationship's public, marriage-like character, not just its private duration.

Statutory cross-references

The maintenance route under Section 125, Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, now corresponds to Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 and the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 are unaffected by the 2023 criminal law recodification and continue to apply exactly as before. Note also the important limitation flagged in this line of cases: the legitimacy and inheritance protections under Section 16, Hindu Marriage Act and the Tulsa presumption are worked out under Hindu personal law; whether comparable protections extend to live-in couples governed by Muslim, Christian or other personal laws remains considerably less settled, and needs to be assessed separately on the facts of each case.
