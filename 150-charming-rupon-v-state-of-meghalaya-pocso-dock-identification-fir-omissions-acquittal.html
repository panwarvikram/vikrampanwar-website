Charming Rupon v. State of Meghalaya (2026 INSC 1102): Supreme Court Says a Child Victim’s Failure to Identify the Accused in Court, Plus an Incomplete FIR, Cannot Sustain a POCSO Conviction

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Charming Rupon v. State of Meghalaya
BenchJustice Vikram Nath and Justice Sandeep Mehta (judgment authored by Justice Mehta)
Date9 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1102; Criminal Appeal arising out of SLP (Crl.) No. 11881 of 2022
Key SectionsSection 6 POCSO Act, 2012; Section 106 Evidence Act, 1872 (Section 109 BSA, 2023)

What the case was about

The appellant was accused of sexually assaulting his 11-year-old niece, a child with roughly 40% intellectual disability, at her home in East Jaintia Hills, Meghalaya, on 28 May 2015. The child’s mother had gone to work and, according to the prosecution, had seen the accused in a garden next to the house before leaving. A cousin who stayed over was said to have heard the child’s account and told the mother the next morning. The child is stated to have first spoken about it to her schoolteacher on 7 June, and the FIR followed on 8 June 2015, about ten days after the incident.

The Special Judge (POCSO) convicted the appellant under Section 6 of the POCSO Act on 7 December 2021 and sentenced him to ten years’ rigorous imprisonment with a fine of ₹1 lakh. The Meghalaya High Court dismissed his appeal on 29 March 2022. He then came to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The Supreme Court allowed the appeal, set aside the conviction and ordered his release unless wanted in another case. The Court did not say that a child’s word cannot convict; it said that on this record, the cumulative weight of the gaps made the conviction unsafe.

No identification in court. Making every allowance for the victim’s disability, she did not identify the appellant as the person who assaulted her. Her cousin also did not identify him, and both said they had learnt his name from the mother. The victim admitted that she had met the Special Public Prosecutor before deposing and had been told what to say, which fed the suspicion of tutoring. With no scientific or other independent corroboration, the Court found it unsafe to rest a conviction on her testimony.

FIR omissions and delay. An FIR, the Court reminded, is not an encyclopaedia. But a fact that forms the very foundation of the case, such as the cousin’s disclosure to the mother and the mother seeing the accused near the house, cannot be introduced for the first time at trial if the earliest version is silent and no reason is offered. Together with the ten-day gap, this weakened the story. The Court relied on its earlier rulings in Amar Nath Jha v. Nand Kishore Singh and Ram Kumar Pandey v. State of M.P. on this point.

Adverse inference. The schoolteacher, to whom the child first spoke and whose disclosure led to the FIR, was never examined and no explanation was given. Citing Takhaji Hiraji v. Thakore Kubersing Chamansing, the Court drew an adverse inference against the prosecution.

Section 106 did not help the State. The burden to explain facts “especially within the knowledge” of the accused shifts only after the prosecution proves that victim and accused were last seen together. The mother had seen him merely in the garden, not with the child.

Medical evidence and defence. Defence counsel was unavailable when one medical officer was to be cross-examined; the trial court neither arranged legal aid nor deferred the examination. The Court held that testimony could not be relied upon, and observed that medical evidence of injury, even if accepted, does not by itself show who caused it. The accused’s alibi (that he was in another village on a property transaction) had been brushed aside by both courts without proper consideration.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

POCSO trials in our district and sessions courts are often built on the child’s testimony and a medical report. This judgment is a useful reminder of the checklist that wins or loses such cases at the trial stage. Was the accused actually pointed out in the witness box (dock identification), and if the witness did not, does anything independent fill that gap? Does the FIR carry the facts the prosecution later relies upon? Were all natural witnesses, particularly the first person to whom the child spoke, produced? Did the defence get a real opportunity to cross-examine every witness, including doctors?

For defence counsel, the case supports a careful reading of the first version, a record of any contact between the child witness and the prosecutor, and a prompt request for legal aid or an adjournment when cross-examination cannot be completed. For prosecutors and the bench, it is a caution that sympathy for a vulnerable victim cannot replace proof, and that the identification gap must be dealt with at the trial, ideally by re-examining the witness, rather than left for appeal. Note that the Court acquitted on the specific record before it; it did not lay down that every witness who fails to identify the accused defeats a POCSO prosecution.

Statutory cross-references

POCSO Act, 2012, Section 6 (aggravated penetrative sexual assault) is unchanged by the 2023 codes. Section 106 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (burden of proving fact especially within knowledge) corresponds to Section 109 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, and the adverse presumption for withheld evidence under Section 114(g) corresponds to Section 119(g) BSA. The duty to provide legal aid to an unrepresented accused at State expense, formerly Section 304 CrPC, is now Section 341 BNSS, 2023.
