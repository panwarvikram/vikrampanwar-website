Common Intention vs. Common Object: Section 34 and Section 149 IPC, from Jagtar Singh v. State of Punjab to the Privy Council Classics

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Lead CaseJagtar Singh & Ors. v. State of Punjab; Hardev Singh alias Billa v. State of Punjab
Court & BenchPunjab & Haryana High Court (DB), Justices Ritu Bahri & Manisha Batra
Date of Judgment25 May 2023
Citation2023:PHHC:075694-DB
Key SectionsSections 34, 141, 147, 149, 302, 427 IPC

Why this comes up in almost every group-crime trial

Few distinctions in criminal law are cited as often, and misapplied as often, as the line between Section 34 IPC (acts done in furtherance of common intention) and Section 149 IPC (offences committed in prosecution of a common object of an unlawful assembly). Both fix criminal liability on one person for what another person actually did — but they rest on genuinely different foundations, and conflating them has overturned convictions all the way up to the Privy Council.

A recent Punjab & Haryana High Court judgment gives a clean, current illustration of Section 149 failing for want of proof of common object. Read alongside the classical authorities that built this area of law — two Privy Council rulings and two Supreme Court decisions — it forms a complete practitioner's map of the doctrine.

The recent case: Jagtar Singh v. State of Punjab (2023)

Four men were convicted by the Sessions Court, Gurdaspur of murdering two victims by deliberately ramming them with a Bolero vehicle, in a dispute linked to rivalry over a liquor vend. The trial court convicted all four under Section 302 read with Section 149 IPC, relying on eyewitness testimony from two prosecution witnesses closely related to the deceased.

On appeal, the Punjab & Haryana High Court acquitted all four accused. The bench found the prosecution's case riddled with unexplained delay in lodging the FIR, contradictory and improbable eyewitness accounts, an inadmissible site plan, and a complete failure to prove motive. But the judgment's most instructive passage, for practitioners, is its treatment of Section 149:

The Court's holding on Section 149

Three of the four accused — Aman Kumar, Shamsher Singh, and Jagtar Singh — were not attributed any specific overt act at all. They were convicted solely with the aid of Section 149. The High Court held this could not stand: "mere presence of an unlawful assembly cannot render a person liable unless it is proved that there was a common object and they were actuated by that common object." Since the prosecution failed to produce any satisfactory evidence that all the appellants had formed an unlawful assembly with murder as their common object, the constructive liability under Section 149 collapsed along with the rest of the case.

This is Section 149 doctrine working exactly as intended: constructive liability under the section is not a shortcut around proving intent — it still requires the prosecution to establish, from the facts and circumstances, what the assembly's common object actually was.

The foundational cases behind the doctrine

Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. Emperor (1924) — the Privy Council settles Section 34

In this famous post office dacoity case, the appellant was caught fleeing the scene where a sub-postmaster was shot dead by one of several assailants. His defence argued that Section 34 could only apply if he had personally fired the fatal shot. The Privy Council rejected this narrow reading, holding that "a criminal act" in Section 34 means the whole unity of criminal behaviour — the joint transaction of several persons acting in furtherance of a common intention — and each participant is liable for the whole result as if he had done it alone. The phrase "they also serve who only stand and wait" from this judgment remains one of the most quoted lines in Indian criminal jurisprudence, capturing that even a person who does not strike the fatal blow can be liable under Section 34 if he participated in furtherance of the common intention.

Mahbub Shah v. Emperor (1945) — common intention needs a pre-arranged plan

Two decades later, the Privy Council drew the crucial limiting principle. The appellant and his companion rushed to the rescue of a relative during a scuffle, and one of them fatally shot a member of the opposing party. The prosecution argued that a common intention to kill arose the moment both men ran up with guns. The Privy Council disagreed, holding that common intention presupposes a prior meeting of minds and a pre-arranged plan — it is not enough that two people happen to have the same or similar intention independently of each other. The judgment's most cited line is a caution to courts: "Care must be taken not to confuse same or similar intention with common intention; the partition which divides their bounds is often very thin... but if overlooked will result in miscarriage of justice."

Mizaji v. State of U.P. (1958) — how far can common object extend?

Here, a group went armed with a spear, a pistol, and lathis to forcibly take possession of disputed agricultural land. When resisted, one member fired the pistol, killing a complainant. The Supreme Court upheld the conviction of all members under Section 149, holding that even though the common object was merely to take forcible possession — not explicitly to commit murder — the composition of the assembly, the weapons carried, and their conduct (threatening to "finish" the opposing party) showed they knew murder was likely to be committed in prosecution of that object. This illustrates the second limb of Section 149: liability attaches not only for offences committed to directly achieve the common object, but for any offence the members knew was likely to result from pursuing it.

Pandurang, Tukia and Bhillia v. State of Hyderabad (1954) — no common intention without proof of concert

Five men were prosecuted for a fatal assault; the Supreme Court accepted that two of them individually inflicted specific, provably fatal blows. But for a third accused, Pandurang, who inflicted only a minor injury with an axe, no case under Section 34 or Section 149 could be sustained. The Court held there was no evidence of any prior meeting of minds — the accused were not even all from the same caste or background — and reduced his conviction to the lesser offence actually proved against him individually. This case is the practical companion to Mahbub Shah: it shows a court applying the "no common intention without pre-arranged plan" principle to acquit a co-accused of constructive liability while still holding him liable for his own individual act.

Tukaram Ganpat Pandare v. State of Maharashtra (1974) — Section 34 in property offences

Though arising from a burglary rather than a violent offence, this Supreme Court judgment usefully illustrates that Section 34's principle of joint criminal responsibility is not confined to murder cases. An accused found in possession of duplicate godown keys and present near the stolen goods at a weighbridge was convicted with the aid of Section 34, even without direct evidence of his presence at the actual break-in, because the totality of circumstances showed him acting in concert with the others toward the joint criminal project.

Why this matters for Criminal practitioners in Haryana

For defence counsel before the District Courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, this line of authority is essential whenever a chargesheet names multiple accused under Section 149 (now Section 190 BNS) without attributing a specific overt act to each. The Jagtar Singh judgment is directly citable: where the prosecution cannot establish, from evidence, what the assembly's actual common object was, or that a particular accused shared it, a conviction resting solely on group membership cannot survive appeal.

Equally, when the prosecution instead invokes Section 34 (now Section 3(5) BNS) against multiple accused, Mahbub Shah and Pandurang together give counsel the tools to press for proof of an actual pre-arranged plan — not merely that several accused happened to act around the same time with similar intent. This distinction is frequently the difference between a client facing a murder conviction by constructive liability and a client liable only for his own individually proven act, often a much lesser offence.

For prosecutors, Mizaji and Barendra Kumar Ghosh remain the standard citations for establishing that liability under both sections extends beyond the person who struck the fatal blow, provided the evidentiary foundation — common object or common intention — is properly laid and proved, not merely assumed from the fact of a joint appearance at the scene.

Statutory cross-references

Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, the principle of common intention formerly in Section 34 IPC now appears in Section 3(5) BNS, while unlawful assembly and its constructive liability provision, formerly Sections 141 and 149 IPC, now correspond to Sections 189 and 190 BNS respectively. Murder under old Section 302 IPC is now Section 103 BNS, and rioting under Section 147 IPC now sits at Section 191 BNS. The substantive doctrine developed across these decades of case law — the requirement of a pre-arranged plan for common intention, and proof of a genuine shared object for unlawful assembly liability — continues to apply without modification under the renumbered BNS provisions.

This body of case law forms the backbone of Section 149 IPC unlawful assembly and Section 34 IPC common intention jurisprudence in India, and remains essential reading for any advocate handling group-crime trials, rioting cases, or multi-accused murder appeals before the district courts of Haryana.
