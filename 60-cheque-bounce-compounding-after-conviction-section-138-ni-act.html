Convicted, Appeal Dismissed, Case Still Alive: P&H High Court Says Cheque Bounce Offences Can Be Compounded Even at the Revision Stage

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court; illustrative precedent — Mukesh Sharma v. State of Haryana & Anr.
BenchJustice Sumeet Goel (ruling reported 5 August 2026); Justice Subhas Mehla (Mukesh Sharma, 7 May 2026)
Date5 August 2026 (reported); precedent decided 7 May 2026
CitationReported by LiveLaw, 5 August 2026 · CRM-M-58269-2025 (Mukesh Sharma)
Key SectionsS. 138 & S. 147, Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 · S. 359 & S. 528, BNSS, 2023

What the case was about

A cheque bounce complaint under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act typically follows a familiar arc: the accused is convicted by a Magistrate, files an appeal before the Sessions Court, and if that appeal fails too, the matter can end up in revision before the High Court. By the time a case reaches that third stage, most litigants assume the die is cast — either the conviction is confirmed, or it is overturned on some legal infirmity. The Punjab & Haryana High Court has, in a fresh ruling reported on 5 August 2026 before Justice Sumeet Goel, reaffirmed a principle that keeps surprising litigants and, occasionally, their own lawyers: even at this late stage, if the complainant and the accused genuinely settle their dispute, the offence can still be compounded and the conviction wiped out.

This is not a one-off holding. It is the third time in under a year that this High Court has applied the same reasoning to a fresh set of facts. In Mukesh Sharma v. State of Haryana & Anr. (CRM-M-58269-2025), decided by Justice Subhas Mehla on 7 May 2026, a cheque-bounce accused had been convicted by a Magistrate, lost his appeal before the Additional Sessions Judge in 2019, and only reached a compromise with the complainant in February 2023 — years after both the trial and the first appeal had run their course. The High Court still permitted compounding, holding that once the complainant had been paid in full, keeping the criminal machinery running any further "would serve no purpose."

What the court held

The legal foundation for this position rests on reading three provisions together. Section 147 of the NI Act itself declares every offence under the Act to be compoundable. Section 359 of the BNSS, 2023 (which carries forward the compounding scheme earlier found in Section 320 of the CrPC) sets out the general procedure for compounding offences with the consent of the person aggrieved. And Section 528 of the BNSS, 2023 — the provision preserving the High Court's inherent powers, in the place of the old Section 482 CrPC — supplies the residual authority to step in and quash a conviction in the interest of justice even where no other provision expressly covers the situation.

Reading these together, the High Court has consistently held that a Section 138 offence can be compounded "at all stages of litigation, including when the matter has reached the High Court after having been conclusively dealt with by the Magisterial as also the Sessions Court." The court has been emphatic that its inherent jurisdiction under Section 528 BNSS is not a mere procedural convenience but, in its own words, "intrinsic to the high court, forming its very life-blood, its very essence, its immanent attribute. Without such powers, a high court would retain form but lack substance." The underlying reasoning traces back to well-settled Supreme Court authority holding that Section 138 proceedings are fundamentally compensatory rather than punitive: the object of the provision is to make the payee whole, not to extract retribution once that has happened.

Courts applying this principle typically retain one safeguard: while the conviction can be set aside once a genuine settlement is placed on record, the discretion to waive the costs that are ordinarily imposed as a condition of compounding at a late stage is used sparingly, and only in exceptional circumstances. The message to litigants is that compounding after conviction is not a free pass — it usually still costs something, even if it saves the criminal record.

Why this matters for practitioners in Haryana

For advocates handling Section 138 matters in the district courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, this line of rulings is worth keeping in a client's back pocket at every stage of the case, not just before trial. It is common for litigants to believe that once a Magistrate has convicted their client and the Sessions Court has dismissed the appeal, the only route left is a full-blown revision fought on the merits. That is not correct. A negotiated settlement remains available even after two rounds of adverse findings, and can end the matter faster and more cheaply than continuing to litigate a revision whose odds may not be favourable.

This also changes the calculus for complainants. A payee who has secured a conviction and an appellate affirmance still holds real leverage to negotiate a lump-sum settlement, because the accused facing a live revision has a strong incentive to pay up and compound rather than gamble on the High Court's view of the merits. Advocates representing complainants should not assume that a favourable judgment forecloses settlement talks; if anything, it often opens the door to a better one, since the accused now has more to lose by continuing to litigate.

For accused persons, the practical lesson is to actively pursue settlement even after losing at two levels, rather than treating the case as a lost cause. A revision petition can be an opportunity to place a fresh compromise on record, and the High Court has shown a consistent willingness — across at least three reported rulings in the last year — to accept it and close the matter, provided the settlement is real and not a device to escape a properly earned conviction.

Statutory cross-references

Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 remains unchanged in its own numbering under the new criminal law framework, since the NI Act was not itself replaced. What has changed is the procedural scaffolding around it: Section 320 of the CrPC, 1973 (compounding of offences) now corresponds to Section 359 of the BNSS, 2023, and Section 482 of the CrPC, 1973 (inherent powers of the High Court) now corresponds to Section 528 of the BNSS, 2023. Advocates drafting compounding applications or revision petitions after the 1 July 2024 cut-over should cite the BNSS provisions directly rather than their CrPC predecessors, particularly where the application itself is being filed after that date regardless of when the original complaint was lodged.
