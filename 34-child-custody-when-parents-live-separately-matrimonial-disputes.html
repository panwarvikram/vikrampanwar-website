Child Custody When Parents Live Separately: Jurisdiction, the Welfare Test, and Visitation Rights Explained

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing StatutesGuardians and Wards Act, 1890 · Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 · Family Courts Act, 1984
Leading CasesGaurav Nagpal v. Sumedha Nagpal, (2009) 1 SCC 42 · Rosy Jacob v. Jacob A. Chakramakkal, (1973) 1 SCC 840 · Nil Ratan Kundu v. Abhijit Kundu, (2008) 9 SCC 413
Key SectionsS. 9, Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 · S. 6, Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956
Governing PrincipleWelfare of the child is paramount, not the legal right of either parent

What the topic is about

When a marriage breaks down and parents begin living separately — whether during a contested divorce, judicial separation proceedings, or even an informal separation with no case yet filed — the question of who the children live with, and how much access the other parent gets, becomes one of the most emotionally charged and legally complex issues in family litigation. Unlike property or maintenance disputes, custody law in India is not governed by a single, comprehensive statute. It draws from personal law (the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 for Hindus, and analogous principles under Muslim, Christian, and Parsi personal law), the secular Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 that applies to all communities as a residual and procedural framework, and a substantial body of Supreme Court and High Court case law that has, over decades, built the operating principles courts actually apply on the ground.

Which court has jurisdiction

The starting point in any custody dispute is jurisdiction, and Indian law is unusually clear on this: Section 9(1) of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 provides that a guardianship or custody petition must be filed in the District Court (or Family Court, where one is constituted, as is the case in most of Haryana's district headquarters) within whose local limits the minor ordinarily resides — not where either parent resides, not where the marriage was solemnised, and not where a divorce petition happens to be pending. "Ordinary residence" means the child's habitual, settled place of living, not a temporary stay with grandparents, a short visit, or a recent relocation engineered specifically to create a favourable forum. Courts have consistently held that a parent cannot manufacture jurisdiction by whisking a child away to a new city shortly before filing a custody petition there; the inquiry looks at where the child was, in substance, actually living and settled before the dispute arose. This becomes especially contentious in cases involving NRI parents or a parent who relocates a child across state lines or internationally — in such cases, Indian courts retain jurisdiction over a child physically present in India regardless of a foreign custody order, though they will weigh the existence and reasoning of any foreign order as a relevant (not binding) factor under the doctrine of comity of courts.

It is worth noting that a custody claim can also be raised as an ancillary prayer within a pending matrimonial case (divorce, judicial separation, or restitution of conjugal rights) before the Family Court, rather than as a standalone Guardians and Wards Act petition — most Family Courts in Haryana permit this consolidated approach, which avoids parallel proceedings in two different fora.

The welfare principle: the only test that ultimately matters

Indian custody law is unambiguous on one point above all others: no parent has an absolute right to custody merely by virtue of being the natural guardian. In Rosy Jacob v. Jacob A. Chakramakkal, (1973) 1 SCC 840, the Supreme Court held that the dominant consideration is the welfare of the minor, not the legal rights of the parents under personal law. This was reaffirmed and elaborated in Gaurav Nagpal v. Sumedha Nagpal, (2009) 1 SCC 42, where the Court held that even statutory preferences (such as the father being the natural guardian under Hindu law) must yield entirely to the child's welfare, and that courts exercise a parens patriae (state-as-guardian) jurisdiction that overrides technical entitlements. Nil Ratan Kundu v. Abhijit Kundu, (2008) 9 SCC 413 further clarified that "welfare" is to be read in the widest possible sense — encompassing not just material and physical well-being but moral, intellectual, and emotional welfare too.

In practice, this means custody litigation is not decided by asking "whose right is stronger" but by asking a fact-intensive question: with which parent, and under what arrangement, is this particular child likely to grow up safest, most stable, and most well-adjusted?

The yardsticks courts actually apply

Over decades of case law, certain recurring factors have crystallised into the practical checklist Family Courts and High Courts use, even though no single provision lists them exhaustively.

Age and gender of the child. Section 6 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 creates a statutory presumption that custody of a child below the age of five ordinarily vests with the mother, regardless of who the "natural guardian" is otherwise. This is a rebuttable presumption, not an absolute rule — it can be displaced where the mother is shown to be unfit or wholly unsuitable, but courts require clear, cogent evidence of unsuitability before departing from it, rather than mere allegations. For older children, no comparable statutory presumption operates, and gender or age alone carry progressively less weight as independent factors.

The child's own wishes. Where a child is of an age and maturity to form an intelligent preference (courts generally treat this as beginning somewhere around 9–12 years, though there is no fixed cut-off), the court will typically interview the child privately, in chambers, without either parent or counsel present, to ascertain the child's wishes free of pressure. This preference is an important factor but not conclusive — a child's stated wish can be overridden where it conflicts with the child's actual welfare or appears to be the product of parental influence or an unhealthy attachment to material comforts.

Continuity, stability, and the status quo. Courts are generally reluctant to disturb a child's existing, settled environment — school, friends, home, routine — without strong reason, on the view that repeated custody shuffling is itself harmful to a child's psychological stability. This is why interim custody orders, once passed, tend to acquire practical weight even before the final hearing.

The primary caregiver and quality of parenting. Courts look closely at who has actually been performing day-to-day caregiving, and assess each parent's character, conduct, financial stability, home environment, and capacity (including the support of extended family) to provide a nurturing upbringing. Allegations of cruelty, addiction, instability, or a history of neglect toward the child weigh heavily; allegations relevant only to the failed marriage but irrelevant to parenting capacity carry far less weight, and courts are cautious not to conflate marital fault with parental unfitness.

Financial capacity is a factor, not a trump card. A wealthier parent does not automatically get preference — courts routinely award custody to a financially weaker parent (commonly the mother) while directing the other parent to pay child maintenance, precisely so that financial disparity does not distort the welfare assessment.

Visitation rights: the working compromise

Where sole physical custody is awarded to one parent, Indian courts almost invariably grant the non-custodial parent structured visitation rights, on the settled principle that a child's welfare is ordinarily best served by maintaining a meaningful relationship with both parents, not just the custodial one. Courts have made clear that visitation cannot be indefinitely denied or used as leverage in unrelated disputes — for instance, withholding access to pressure the other parent over maintenance or property is impermissible, and the Supreme Court has cautioned that visitation arrangements must never be allowed to compromise the child's health, routine, or emotional stability. In practice, Family Court orders specify visitation with real granularity: which weekends, which school holidays, festival time-sharing, video-call access for a parent living far away, and handover logistics (often at a neutral location or through a court-appointed mechanism where the parents' relationship is acrimonious enough that direct handovers risk conflict in front of the child). Many Family Courts in Haryana now also refer contested custody and visitation disputes to court-annexed mediation centres before framing final orders, recognising that a negotiated parenting plan is usually more durable and less damaging to the child than an imposed order.

Custody and visitation orders are never treated as permanently final in the way a decree of divorce is — courts retain continuing jurisdiction to modify custody or visitation arrangements on a genuine, material change in circumstances (relocation, remarriage, deterioration in a parent's conduct, or the child's own evolving needs and wishes), since the underlying welfare test is, by its nature, an ongoing inquiry rather than a one-time determination.

Finally, where a parent is deprived of a child's custody in violation of an existing order, or where a child is being illegally retained by a parent or relative without any custody order at all, the remedy is not limited to a slow-moving guardianship petition — a writ of habeas corpus can be maintained before the High Court to secure the child's production and, ultimately, custody, particularly where the child is very young and the detaining party's claim to custody is legally weak.
