One Composite Question, 424 Words Long: P&H High Court Sets Aside Death Sentences for Two Convicts Over Defective Section 313 Questioning

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court, murder reference and connected appeal against a trial court death sentence
BenchJustice Anoop Chitkara & Justice Sukhvinder Kaur
Date4 August 2026
CitationReported by LiveLaw, 4 August 2026 · full-text citation not yet available in open-access sources
Key SectionsS. 313, CrPC, 1973 (S. 351, BNSS, 2023) · rape-murder charges, exact IPC sections not specified in available reporting

What the case was about

A trial court had convicted two men for the rape and murder of a 12-year-old girl and sentenced both to death. Because a death sentence cannot become final without confirmation, the matter reached the Punjab & Haryana High Court as a murder reference, heard alongside the convicts' own appeal against conviction. Ordinarily, a bench confirming or reviewing a capital sentence scrutinises the evidence on guilt and the aggravating and mitigating circumstances relevant to sentence. This bench, comprising Justice Anoop Chitkara and Justice Sukhvinder Kaur, instead found a problem that neither side had even argued: the trial court's own questioning of the accused under Section 313 of the CrPC was so badly done that the conviction itself could not stand, whatever the strength of the evidence.

Section 313 CrPC exists for a narrow but vital purpose. After the prosecution closes its evidence, the trial court must personally examine the accused and put to him, question by question, every circumstance in the evidence that points to his guilt. The idea is simple: an accused should not be convicted on the strength of evidence he was never given a fair chance to explain. According to LiveLaw's report on the ruling, the trial court in this case had failed to forward the FSL report to the doctors who conducted the post-mortem examination for their final opinion on the cause of death — a lapse that fed directly into how incomplete the eventual Section 313 questioning turned out to be. The High Court found that the medical evidence establishing rape was never specifically put to either accused at all.

What the court held

The Bench held that since neither accused was ever confronted with the medical evidence of sexual assault, "on this score alone, this is a highly defective questioning under Section 313 CrPC." That single omission mattered enormously because, as the Bench reasoned, the rape was the motive for the murder: absent the sexual assault, there would have been no reason to kill the child to conceal what had happened. A Section 313 examination that skips the very fact that supplies the motive for the killing, the court said, cannot be treated as a fair or complete examination.

This is not the first time this particular Bench has confronted this exact failure. In two other recent Punjab & Haryana matters — a Jhajjar case involving the rape-murder of a five-year-old, and a Ludhiana case involving a seven-and-a-half-year-old victim referred to by the court as "Laadli" — the same two judges set aside trial court convictions and death sentences for the identical reason: incriminating evidence, including DNA and medical reports, was clubbed into single sprawling questions running hundreds of words, rather than put to the accused as short, specific, comprehensible questions. In the Ludhiana matter, the Bench had observed that expecting an accused to answer a 424-word composite question, followed by a one-line addition that a second witness "also stated in similar terms," defeats the entire purpose of Section 313: to give the accused a real, meaningful opportunity to understand and respond to the case against him.

Rather than order an acquittal, the Bench in each of these cases drew a careful line between defects that are incurable and irregularities that can be cured without prejudice to either side. A trial court that omitted incriminating material altogether, or that asked the right questions in the wrong words, falls into the second, curable category. The remedy is not to let the accused walk free, and it is not to let a flawed conviction stand either — it is to send the case back to the point where the error occurred and do it properly. Here too, the High Court quashed the conviction and the death sentence "without commenting on the case's merits," and remanded the matter to the Sessions Court with directions to reopen proceedings from the Section 313 stage: every incriminating circumstance is to be put separately, in short and specific questions the accused can actually understand, the accused is to be given a fresh opportunity to lead defence evidence, and only then should a new judgment follow.

Why this matters for practitioners in Haryana

For defence counsel, this line of rulings is a reminder that Section 313 is not a formality to be waived through in five minutes at the fag end of trial. Every accused's counsel should independently check, before final arguments, whether each piece of incriminating evidence — medical reports, forensic reports, recoveries, and every material prosecution witness's testimony — has actually been put to the client in an intelligible, standalone question. A defect here can unwind a conviction years after the trial concludes, but it does not by itself win an acquittal; it wins a retrial, which is a mixed result for an accused who has already spent years in custody.

For prosecutors and for judicial officers conducting trials in the district courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, the lesson runs the other way. A Section 313 statement should never be built by pasting a witness's entire deposition into one question, nor should one witness's evidence be disposed of with a one-line "similarly stated" shortcut for another. Each circumstance needs its own short, plain-language question. This is especially critical in cases carrying capital or life sentences, where the appellate and reference courts will scrutinise the Section 313 record with exacting care, and where a shortcut taken to save an afternoon in the trial court can cost years in delay by the time the matter is corrected on reference.

The pattern across these three rulings by the same Bench also signals that the Punjab & Haryana High Court is treating defective Section 313 examinations as a structural problem in how murder trials are being conducted in the subordinate courts of the two states, not as an isolated slip. Trial courts handling grave offences would do well to build a Section 313 questionnaire, circumstance by circumstance, at the close of prosecution evidence, rather than drafting it hurriedly once arguments have concluded.

Statutory cross-references

Section 313 of the CrPC, 1973, corresponds to Section 351 of the BNSS, 2023, and the requirement to put every incriminating circumstance to the accused in an intelligible form carries over unchanged into the new code. Trials that began under the old CrPC continue to be governed by it; the principle laid down here will, however, apply with equal force to Section 351 examinations under BNSS trials once those start reaching appellate scrutiny in similar numbers.
