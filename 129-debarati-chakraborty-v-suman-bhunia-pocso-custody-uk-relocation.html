Debarati Bhunia Chakraborty v. Suman Sankar Bhunia: Supreme Court Says a Parent Facing POCSO Prosecution Cannot Get Custody, Clears the Way for the Children to Move to the UK

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Debarati Bhunia Chakraborty v. Suman Sankar Bhunia
BenchJustices Vikram Nath & Sandeep Mehta
Date23 September 2026 (interim order; final hearing listed January 2027)
Citation2026 INSC 1043 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 981 · C.A. Nos. 12774-12775/2026
Key SectionsGuardians and Wards Act, 1890 · Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 · POCSO Act, 2012

What the case was about

The couple separated in 2018. Their son, born in 2013, and daughter, born in 2019, stayed on with the mother, who holds a doctorate in engineering and has since moved cities — and jobs — several times, eventually landing a lecturer's position at the University of Hull in the United Kingdom. Somewhere in the middle of the custody litigation, the son told a psychiatrist at NIMHANS that his father had sexually abused him. That disclosure led to a POCSO FIR and, in time, a chargesheet against the father.

The Family Court, in July 2024, still gave sole custody to the father, with visitation to the mother, and turned down her plea to take the children abroad. It treated her repeated relocations as instability rather than as a career trying to find its footing. On appeal, the Delhi High Court agreed with the Family Court in January 2026 — and went a step further, making observations that cast doubt on whether the abuse allegations were even credible, while the criminal case was still pending trial.

The mother carried the matter to the Supreme Court, arguing that a court hearing a custody dispute has no business pre-judging a POCSO trial that hasn't concluded, and that her willingness to build a stable, well-paying career for her children was being read backwards as a flaw.

What the court held

The Bench of Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta reversed both lower courts on the central point: as the Court put it, "under no circumstances" can a parent facing an active POCSO prosecution be considered entitled to custody, whatever the eventual outcome of that trial. The pendency of the case, and the risk it poses to the child's safety and to the integrity of his testimony, was itself enough to disqualify the father for now — the Family Court and High Court had, in effect, decided a live criminal allegation in a civil custody proceeding, which was not theirs to do.

The Court was equally firm about the mother's job changes. Frequent moves in pursuit of better pay and a more secure future for her children, it held, is not a "nomadic life" — it is exactly what a solo parent providing for two children is expected to do. On that footing, the Supreme Court permitted her to take both children to the UK, subject to conditions: she must file an affidavit within six weeks detailing their school enrolment, and furnish an undertaking to produce the children before the Court whenever directed. Because this is an interim order with the final appeal still listed for hearing in January 2027, the stay on the Family Court's and High Court's findings continues to operate in the meantime.

Notably, the Bench also expunged the Delhi High Court's observations questioning the truthfulness of the abuse allegations, calling them premature and uncalled for at a stage when the criminal trial was still running its course. A custody bench, the Court signalled, should not be in the business of pronouncing on the credibility of a pending criminal case — that is the trial court's job, once evidence is actually led.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Custody disputes routinely arrive in Haryana's family courts tangled up with an ongoing criminal matter — sometimes a genuine POCSO or domestic-violence complaint, sometimes one that a suspicious opposing counsel will call retaliatory. This ruling gives practitioners a clean, workable rule for the more straightforward half of that problem: where a parent is actually facing prosecution under a child-protection statute, that pendency alone is a sufficient, independent ground to withhold custody from them while the case is alive, without the family court needing to first assess guilt or innocence. Advocates defending a custody claim on behalf of the accused parent should expect this reasoning to be cited squarely against them, and should focus argument instead on getting the underlying criminal case resolved quickly, rather than trying to relitigate its merits inside matrimonial proceedings.

The judgment is equally useful on the relocation side, which comes up often when one spouse works abroad or takes up employment outside India. Courts in Haryana have sometimes treated a parent's repeated job or city changes as a red flag when weighing custody or a plea to relocate children. This ruling pushes back on that instinct: a career trajectory built around better opportunities for the children is evidence of responsibility, not rootlessness, and counsel representing a working parent — especially a mother — should be building that narrative affirmatively rather than playing defence against it. It is also a reminder to keep any relocation order tethered to enforceable safeguards: school-enrolment affidavits and undertakings to produce the child on direction, exactly as the Supreme Court imposed here, are the standard conditions worth requesting or resisting on behalf of a client.

Statutory cross-references

Custody itself continues to be governed by the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, and, for Hindu parties, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 — neither has a BNS/BNSS equivalent since these are civil, not criminal, statutes. The underlying criminal prosecution proceeds under the POCSO Act, 2012, which likewise remains untouched by the 2023 criminal-law overhaul, though its procedural steps now run through the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023: the police report that used to be filed as a "chargesheet" under Section 173 of the CrPC is now filed under Section 193, BNSS, and it is the completion (or non-completion) of that stage which will decide how quickly the pending prosecution against the father can actually move to trial.
