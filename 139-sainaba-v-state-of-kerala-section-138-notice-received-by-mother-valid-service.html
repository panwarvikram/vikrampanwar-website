Sainaba v. State of Kerala (2026 INSC 1069): Cheque Bounce Notice Received by the Drawer’s Mother Is Valid Service Under Section 138 NI Act

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Sainaba v. State of Kerala & Anr.
BenchJustice Ahsanuddin Amanullah and Justice Manmohan
Date30 September 2026 (as listed in the Verdictum weekly overview; reported 1 October 2026)
Citation2026 INSC 1069
Key SectionsSection 138 and proviso (b), Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881; Section 27, General Clauses Act, 1897; Section 313 CrPC; Article 141

What the case was about

The complainant, Sainaba, said the accused had borrowed ₹3 lakh from her and handed over a cheque in repayment. The cheque bounced. She then sent the statutory demand notice by registered post to the accused’s correct address. It was his mother who received the envelope. The accused neither paid nor replied, and a complaint under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act followed.

At trial the accused took a different line. In his statement under Section 313 CrPC he claimed he had borrowed ₹7 lakh, repaid all of it, and that the ₹3 lakh cheque was a blank signed cheque left with the complainant. The Trial Court and the Appellate Court both rejected that story and convicted him. In revision, the Kerala High Court overturned the concurrent findings on a narrow point: the notice, it said, had not been served on the accused personally but on his mother, so the proviso to Section 138 was not satisfied.

What the Supreme Court held

The Supreme Court allowed the complainant’s appeal, set aside the High Court order and restored the conviction. The sentence reinstated is six months’ simple imprisonment and a fine of ₹3 lakh, with further imprisonment in default.

The central ruling is that proviso (b) to Section 138 speaks of the giving of notice, not of personal receipt by the drawer. Once the notice is sent by registered post to the drawer’s correct address, Section 27 of the General Clauses Act raises a presumption that it was served. That presumption can be rebutted, but the burden is on the drawer. He must show that the address was wrong, that the notice never reached the household, or that he was on bad terms with the family member who signed for it. This accused proved none of these. He had used the very same address in his vakalatnama, his appeal and his revision petition, and he never alleged any hostility with his mother.

The Court also dealt with a Kerala Division Bench ruling, M.D. Thomas v. P.S. Jaleel (2009), which had set aside a conviction because the notice was received by the drawer’s wife. The Bench declared that decision per incuriam, because it ignored binding Supreme Court authority, and said it cannot bind courts under Article 141. The Court followed the three-Judge Bench line in C.C. Alavi Haji v. Palapetty Muhammed (2007), and the earlier decisions in K. Bhaskaran and D. Vinod Shivappa on deemed service. It also reasoned that the opposite rule would be illogical: a notice that is never delivered at all is presumed served, so a notice that is actually delivered to a family member at the correct address cannot be treated worse.

Finally, the Court said the High Court had wrongly shifted the burden onto the complainant and had no good reason to disturb concurrent findings of two courts merely because the envelope was signed by someone else.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Cheque bounce complaints form a large part of the docket in the courts at Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, and “notice was not received by the accused personally” is one of the most common defences. This judgment closes much of that ground. For complainants, the practical lesson is to keep the registered post receipt, the tracking report and the postal acknowledgment (or the “refused” or “left” endorsement), and to check that the address on the envelope matches the address the drawer has used in bank records, agreements and earlier correspondence.

For the defence, a bare plea that the mother, wife or a servant signed for the notice will not succeed. The accused will need positive material: a different residential address at the relevant time, proof that the signatory lived elsewhere, or evidence of estrangement. Pleading the same address in later court filings, as this accused did, will count against him. Defence counsel should also remember that the 15-day payment window under the proviso runs from receipt, so the date of delivery on the postal record still matters.

The ruling also repeats a broader point for revisional courts: a High Court exercising revisional jurisdiction should be slow to upset concurrent findings of conviction on a technical objection that the record does not support.

Statutory cross-references

Section 138 and Section 142 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 continue to apply unchanged. Section 27 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 supplies the presumption of service by post. The accused’s examination under Section 313 CrPC is now Section 351 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, and the revisional power in the High Court is now found in Sections 438 and 442 BNSS (earlier Sections 397 and 401 CrPC). Presumptions under Sections 118 and 139 of the NI Act continue to operate against the drawer once the signature on the cheque is admitted.
