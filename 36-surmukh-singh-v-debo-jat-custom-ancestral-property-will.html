Surmukh Singh v. Smt. Debo: P&H High Court Says Custom Lets an Issueless Jat Will Ancestral Property to a Caregiver

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSurmukh Singh and others v. Smt. Debo and others
BenchJustice Harkesh Manuja, Punjab & Haryana High Court
DateReported 13 July 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 229
Key SectionsCustomary law of Jat agriculturists; presumption of marriage from cohabitation; ancestral property alienation rules

What the case was about

This second appeal reached the Punjab & Haryana High Court out of a dispute that is depressingly familiar in rural Haryana and Punjab: an ancestral landholding, an issueless proprietor, and collateral relatives who surface only after his death to challenge how the land was disposed of. The proprietor, a Jat agriculturist named Bishan Singh, had no children. During his lifetime, a woman named Debo lived with him and, according to the record, had been described as his wife in a decree passed as far back as 1978 — a decree that was never independently challenged by anyone at the time. Long after Bishan Singh's death, his collateral relatives (agnates further removed than a son or near heir) filed suit contesting the disposition of his ancestral property, arguing that custom did not permit him to give it away outside the recognised line of succession.

Both the trial court and the first appellate court ruled against the collaterals, upholding the disposition in favour of the person who had rendered services to Bishan Singh — understood in the judgment to include care, companionship, and support of the kind a spouse or close family member would ordinarily provide. The collaterals then filed a regular second appeal before the High Court, essentially asking it to re-open findings that two courts below had already concurrently arrived at.

What the court held

Justice Harkesh Manuja dismissed the appeal, reiterating a settled but often-litigated principle of customary law governing Jat tribes in this region: an issueless proprietor is not powerless over his own ancestral property merely because he has no direct descendants. Custom permits him to bequeath that property in favour of a person who rendered services to him during his lifetime. For this limited purpose, the Court held, such a beneficiary is treated on a footing analogous to a near agnate — meaning the ordinary customary restriction against alienating ancestral property outside the family line does not operate as an absolute bar where the beneficiary has, in substance, stood in that caregiving role.

The second, and arguably more consequential, finding concerned the 1978 decree describing Debo as Bishan Singh's wife. The High Court held that since this decree had never been independently challenged by any party at the relevant time, it could not be reopened decades later through a collateral attack dressed up as a succession dispute. More significantly, the Court invoked the well-established evidentiary presumption that prolonged and continuous cohabitation between a man and a woman as husband and wife raises a rebuttable presumption of a valid marriage. On the facts before it, this presumption was not displaced by the collaterals, who offered no cogent evidence to the contrary beyond bare assertion. With both the customary right to bequeath and the marital status of the beneficiary affirmed, the High Court found no illegality in the concurrent findings of the two courts below and declined to interfere.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

For advocates handling succession and property disputes in the district courts of Charkhi Dadri, Bhiwani, and across rural Haryana, this judgment is a useful reaffirmation of two doctrines that come up constantly in agricultural land litigation. First, the "services rendered" exception to the customary rule against alienating ancestral property outside the family line is not a technicality — courts will recognise a caregiver, including a de facto spouse, as functionally equivalent to a near agnate where the facts support it. Second, and just as important procedurally, is the reminder that a decree recording marital status, if left unchallenged for decades, hardens into a fact that later litigants cannot casually reopen through a fresh civil suit years after the event. Litigants (and their lawyers) who suspect a decree affecting property rights or marital status is questionable must challenge it promptly, not wait for a succession dispute to surface after the principal parties are dead.

This also has practical value for drafting. Advocates advising clients on Wills or family settlements involving elderly, issueless landowners should document the caregiving relationship contemporaneously — medical records, revenue entries, ration cards, or affidavits from neighbours showing cohabitation and care — so that the "presumption of marriage" and the "services rendered" custom are both easier to establish if the disposition is challenged after the proprietor's death, as so often happens once land values rise.

Statutory cross-references

This dispute was decided on customary law principles specific to Jat agriculturists in Punjab and Haryana, which operate independently of, but alongside, the codified Hindu Succession Act, 1956. Where custom is silent or inapplicable, Section 22 of the Hindu Succession Act (preferential rights of co-heirs) and the general rules of testamentary succession under the Indian Succession Act, 1925 remain relevant. The presumption of marriage from long cohabitation is a rule of evidence developed through a consistent line of Supreme Court authority and is not tied to any single statutory provision; it operates under the general framework of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (now the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023) on presumptions as to facts.
