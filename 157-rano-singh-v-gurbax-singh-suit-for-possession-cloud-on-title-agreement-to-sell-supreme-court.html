Rano Singh v. Gurbax Singh: Supreme Court Says an Agreement to Sell Gives No Title and a Bare Suit for Possession Cannot Stand Against a Registered Sale Deed (2026 INSC 1137)

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Smt. Rano Singh & Ors. v. Gurbax Singh (D) through LRs & Ors.
BenchJustice Aravind Kumar and Justice Vipul M. Pancholi
Date16 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1137 · Civil Appeal No. 7025 of 2012
Key SectionsTransfer of Property Act, 1882: Sections 53A, 54; Limitation Act, 1963: Articles 54, 59, 64; Evidence Act, 1872: Section 115

What the case was about

The dispute concerned Plot No. 8 in Friends Colony, New Delhi, about 4,132 square yards. In January 1952 a cooperative housing society agreed to sell the plot to Sohan Singh on a condition: he must build a house within three years or lose the right to buy. In October 1954, before that period ran out, Sohan Singh wrote to the society saying the plot should instead go to Gurbax Singh, whom he described as his nephew. The society transferred the plot, Gurbax Singh built a house at his own cost, and a registered sale deed was executed in his favour in December 1960.

In 1975, Sohan Singh’s heirs filed a suit for possession. They did not ask for cancellation of the 1960 sale deed, and they did not ask for specific performance of the 1952 agreement. The trial court found the suit maintainable but dismissed it on estoppel. The Delhi High Court agreed, adding that it was time-barred and that Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act did not help the heirs. The heirs came to the Supreme Court in a civil appeal pending since 2012.

What the Supreme Court held

The Bench of Justice Aravind Kumar and Justice Vipul M. Pancholi dismissed the appeal, with no order as to costs, on several independent grounds. First, an agreement to sell does not create title. Only a registered sale deed conveys ownership of immovable property under Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, and Sohan Singh never obtained one because the construction condition was never met. His heirs therefore had no title to stand on.

Second, a bare suit for possession was the wrong remedy. Relying on Anathula Sudhakar v. P. Buchi Reddy (2008) 4 SCC 594, the Court held that where a registered sale deed already stands against the plaintiff, it casts a cloud on title and the plaintiff must first seek a declaration, along with cancellation of the deed or specific performance. A plain possession suit does not meet that need.

Third, limitation. The Court treated the claims as barred under Articles 54, 59 and 64 of the Limitation Act, 1963: specific performance expired long before 1975, a cancellation suit was barred whether time ran from 1960 or from 1963, and the twelve-year period for recovering possession had also run out. Fourth, the 1954 letter amounted to a representation relied on by the society and by Gurbax Singh, so Section 115 of the Evidence Act estopped Sohan Singh, and his heirs stood in his shoes. Finally, Section 53A failed because the heirs were out of possession and Sohan Singh had himself abandoned the construction obligation, so he was not ready and willing to perform. The Court did not decide the allegation of fraud, as no cogent evidence was led.

Why this matters for Civil practitioners in Haryana

Property suits in the civil courts of Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri are often drafted as simple possession or injunction suits, even though a registered sale deed, a mutation entry or a family settlement stands against the plaintiff. This judgment is a clear warning. If the opposite side holds a registered instrument, the plaint should ask for a declaration of title, and for cancellation or specific performance as appropriate, instead of relying on possession alone. Leaving the deed untouched can sink the case at the threshold, however good the underlying story is.

The ruling also shows why old agreements to sell and receipts, which are common in village land transactions, must be converted into registered deeds, or enforced by a timely suit. A bare agreement gives a right to sue for performance, not ownership. Defence counsel can use the same reasoning, together with limitation under Article 54 and the estoppel principle, when a long-delayed claim is built on a family member’s earlier written consent or letter. For clients, the practical advice is to act early: delay was fatal here, with a suit filed more than two decades after the 1954 letter.

Note that the decision turns on its particular facts, including a letter written by the original allottee. It does not say possession suits are never maintainable; the line is drawn where there is a serious, concrete dispute over title.

Statutory cross-references

The relevant provisions are Sections 53A and 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882; Articles 54, 59 and 64 and Section 27 of the Limitation Act, 1963; Section 115 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (estoppel); and the Specific Relief Act. For the evidence law, note that the Indian Evidence Act has since been replaced by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, where estoppel appears at Section 121; the Court applied the 1872 Act because the suit long predates the change.
