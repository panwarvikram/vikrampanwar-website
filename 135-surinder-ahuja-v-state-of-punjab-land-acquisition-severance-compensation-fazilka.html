Surinder Ahuja v. State of Punjab (2026 INSC 1068): Supreme Court Restores ₹5 Lakh Per Acre and 50% Severance Compensation for Fazilka Farmers, Rejects High Court’s “Radial” Pricing Model

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Surinder Ahuja & Anr. v. State of Punjab & Anr. (47 connected civil appeals arising from the Punjab & Haryana High Court)
BenchJustice Dipankar Datta and Justice Sheel Nagu
Date29 September 2026 (some outlets report it on 1 October 2026, the date it was uploaded)
Citation2026 INSC 1068 · Civil Appeal Nos. 3800–3846 of 2026
Key SectionsSection 23(1), First, Second and Thirdly, Land Acquisition Act, 1894 · Order VI and the rule that evidence cannot travel beyond pleadings

What the case was about

Around 228 acres of agricultural land in six villages of Fazilka district, Punjab, were acquired for the Aspal Extension Drain, an 18.42-kilometre drainage channel. The drain is a wide cut, roughly 100 to 130 feet across with raised banks on either side, and it ran straight through existing holdings. Farmers were left with fragments on both sides of a channel that could be crossed only at narrow bridges placed about two kilometres apart, which in practice made heavy farm machinery useless on the leftover plots.

The Land Acquisition Collector fixed an award, the Reference Court enhanced it, and the matter went to the Punjab and Haryana High Court. The High Court did something unusual. Instead of treating the project as one block of similar agricultural land, it adopted what the Supreme Court described as a “radial hub-and-spoke” model, treating two villages, Awa and Kotha, as hubs and grading compensation downward with distance. Rates came out between about ₹2.99 lakh and ₹3.39 lakh per acre. It also capped severance compensation at a flat 40 per cent of the value of the acquired land, relying on Tehal Singh v. State of Punjab (1987). Forty-seven appeals followed.

What the Supreme Court held

Market value. The Court restored a uniform ₹5,00,000 per acre across all the villages. It reasoned that the land was acquired for a single project, shared common characteristics, and showed suburban potential, a point it supported by noting that the urban stamp-duty rate of 9 per cent was in play. A distance-based gradation could not be justified where the land was acquired for one scheme in one compact belt.

Severance. The bench revived severance compensation at 50 per cent, and made an important clarification on what that percentage is calculated on. Severance damages under the third clause of Section 23(1) are an independent head, compensating for injury to the land that is left behind. They are therefore assessed on the value of the remaining, unacquired land, not on the value of what was taken. Applying a flat 40 per cent to the acquired land, as the High Court had done, mixed up the two. On the facts, a massive physical barrier had made the severed parcels agriculturally inaccessible, which justified the higher figure.

Where the farmers lost. The Court did not allow everything. It upheld the High Court on fruit trees, confining compensation to the 280 trees actually pleaded (₹5,38,300), because evidence cannot be led beyond pleadings. It also upheld the denial of solatium and additional interest on auxiliary assets, following State of Punjab v. Amarjit Singh (2011), and a flat ₹50,000 per tubewell. The precedents discussed included Tribeni Devi v. Collector of Ranchi (1972) and Walchandnagar Industries v. State of Maharashtra.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Land acquisition references still fill the dockets of Additional District Judges across Haryana and Punjab, especially for canals, drains, roads and the older acquisitions that continue to work their way through appeal. Three practical points emerge. First, a project-wide uniform rate is the starting point when the land is similar and acquired for one purpose; a party arguing for village-wise or distance-wise grading must lead evidence for it. Second, severance is a separate claim and must be specifically pleaded and proved, with the basis of calculation stated as the value of the retained land. Third, the pleadings rule is unforgiving: if a claim covers 400 trees but the pleading says 280, the Court will pay for 280.

For counsel appearing for landowners, the case is a template for proving severance: the width of the cut, the distance between crossings, the inability to move machinery, and the loss of access to wells or irrigation. For counsel appearing for the State, it is a reminder that a flat percentage of the acquired land’s value is a risky shorthand.

Statutory cross-references

The acquisition here was governed by the Land Acquisition Act, 1894, with market value, severance and injurious-affection heads sitting in Section 23(1) and solatium in Section 23(2). Acquisitions initiated after 1 January 2014 fall under the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, where the market-value multiplier, the factors for assessing the award and the 100 per cent solatium appear in Sections 26 to 30; the factor of damage caused by severing land continues in that scheme. Lawyers should confirm which Act governs the acquisition before relying on this ruling’s clause-wise reasoning.
