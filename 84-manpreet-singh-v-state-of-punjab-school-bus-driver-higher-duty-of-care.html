Manpreet Singh v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Says a School Bus Driver Owes Children a "Higher Duty" to Drive Safely, Refuses to Cut His Sentence

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Shalini Singh Nagpal
Date1 September 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 305
Key SectionsSs. 279, 337 & 338, IPC (Ss. 281 & 125(a)/(b), BNS, 2023) · S. 397/401, CrPC (revision)

What the case was about

The petitioner, Manpreet Singh, drove a school bus for Little Flower Model School in Hoshiarpur. In 2018, the bus he was driving overturned near the Rest House in Hoshiarpur while carrying schoolchildren, teachers and a conductor. Several occupants suffered injuries, some of them grievous. An FIR followed under Sections 279 (rash driving), 337 (causing hurt by an act endangering life or personal safety), 338 (causing grievous hurt by an act endangering life or personal safety) and 427 (mischief causing damage) of the Indian Penal Code, on a complaint by a nearby e-rickshaw driver who said the bus had come at high speed and lost control before overturning.

At trial, the account held up. The complainant identified the accused as the driver. A Class 10 student who suffered a fractured arm gave a detailed narration of the rash driving. A teacher who suffered shoulder and leg injuries corroborated it. The bus conductor, who fractured his leg, inculpated the accused as well, and other student and teacher witnesses gave matching versions. Medical evidence proved the injuries, simple and grievous, and a mechanical inspection report ruled out the defence's claim that the accident was caused by a mechanical fault in the bus rather than by how it was driven.

The trial court convicted Manpreet Singh under Sections 279, 337 and 338 IPC and sentenced him to rigorous imprisonment of three months, three months and six months respectively. The Sessions Judge, Hoshiarpur, upheld both the conviction and the sentence in appeal. Before the High Court, the petitioner did not challenge the conviction itself — he sought only a reduction of sentence to the period he had already served in custody.

What the court held

Justice Nagpal first examined, on her own, whether the conviction itself called for any interference, even though only the sentence was under challenge before her in revision. Finding the evidence of the complainant, the injured student, the injured teacher and the injured conductor consistent and corroborated by medical and mechanical evidence, she found no reason to disturb the finding of guilt.

On sentence, the Court's reasoning turned on the nature of the petitioner's role rather than on the bare facts of a road accident. The judgment records the Court's view in terms that go beyond the ordinary rash-driving case: "Petitioner herein was not an ordinary driver of a vehicle, who caused an accident and resultant injuries. He was a professional driver employed by a School to carry young children and teachers from home to school and back. Being entrusted with the transportation of minor school going children, he was under a higher duty to drive the vehicle safely and responsibly, to maintain safe and reasonable speed, keep proper look out for turns, maintain proper control of the vehicle, while observing all applicable traffic rules and safety regulations."

The Court held that sentencing is not a formality that automatically follows conviction but a judicial exercise that must weigh the manner in which the offence was committed, the circumstances of the accused, and the harm actually caused. Applying that standard, it found that the sentence already imposed by the trial court, and affirmed on appeal, already reflected leniency, given that the offence involved injuries to schoolchildren who had been entrusted to the petitioner's care. A further reduction, the Court held, would not serve the interest of society. The revision petition was accordingly dismissed and the conviction and sentence under Sections 279, 337 and 338 IPC were maintained in full.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

School transport is a fact of daily life across Haryana's towns and district headquarters, and rash-driving prosecutions arising out of school bus and van accidents are a recurring category of work before Magistrates in Charkhi Dadri, Bhiwani and neighbouring districts. This ruling gives that category of case a doctrinal anchor that did not exist as clearly before: a school bus or van driver is not to be sentenced as an "ordinary" motorist who caused an accident. The occupational relationship — a professional driver entrusted with the safety of minors travelling to and from school — is itself a sentencing-relevant fact, capable of justifying a harsher view of culpability even where the injuries and the statutory provisions invoked would otherwise look identical to any other Section 279/337/338 case.

For defence counsel, the practical lesson is that a revision confined to sentence, without challenging conviction, still exposes the entire record to scrutiny; the Court here independently reviewed the sufficiency of evidence before turning to sentence, and an accused cannot assume that an unchallenged conviction will simply be left alone. For complainants and for schools defending civil liability claims arising from the same accident, the judgment is also useful as an authority on standard of care: it recognises, in terms a civil court assessing negligence could readily borrow, that transporting schoolchildren carries an elevated duty distinct from ordinary road use. Advocates handling parallel MACT claims arising from school-transport accidents may find this reasoning useful in urging a stricter negligence standard against the driver and, through vicarious liability, the school or transport contractor.

The ruling also reflects a broader institutional concern that recurs in Punjab and Haryana High Court orders on school transport safety — compliance with the Motor Vehicles Act's specific safeguards for school vehicles, such as speed governors, trained attendants and fitness certification. While this particular judgment did not turn on those regulatory provisions, prosecutors and complainants in future cases would do well to plead them where relevant, since the Court has now signalled a willingness to treat violations connected with child safety as aggravating rather than incidental.

Statutory cross-references

The FIR in this case was registered in 2018 and prosecuted under the Indian Penal Code, which was in force at the time. Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, applicable to offences committed on or after 1 July 2024, the corresponding provisions are: Section 279, IPC (rash driving or riding on a public way) corresponds to Section 281, BNS; Section 337, IPC (causing hurt by act endangering life or personal safety of others) corresponds to Section 125(a), BNS, which also increases the maximum fine from ₹500 to ₹5,000; and Section 338, IPC (causing grievous hurt by act endangering life or personal safety of others) corresponds to Section 125(b), BNS, which extends the maximum term of imprisonment from two years to three years and raises the maximum fine from ₹1,000 to ₹10,000. Section 427, IPC (mischief causing damage) corresponds broadly to the mischief provisions in Chapter XV of the BNS, principally Section 324. A revision petition of the kind filed here, earlier maintainable under Sections 397 and 401 of the CrPC, now proceeds under the corresponding provisions of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.
