Sameem Begum & Ors. v. K. Venkat Swamy & Anr.: Supreme Court Says Consortium Is an Indispensable Head of Motor Accident Compensation, Not an Afterthought

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSameem Begum & Ors. v. K. Venkat Swamy & Anr., Supreme Court of India
BenchJustices Nongmeikapam Kotiswar Singh & N.V. Anjaria
Date14 August 2026
Citation2026 INSC 864 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 809
Key SectionsSs. 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

On 23 June 2012, Shaik Janimiya, a 48-year-old man employed as private security personnel, was walking in Malkajgiri when he was struck by a car being driven rashly and negligently. He later died of his injuries. His widow and three children filed a claim petition before the Motor Accidents Claims Tribunal seeking compensation for the loss of their breadwinner.

The Tribunal assessed the deceased's monthly income at ₹7,000, relying on the testimony of the Director of his employer, and awarded a total of ₹8,44,000 with interest. On appeal, the High Court for the State of Telangana at Hyderabad enhanced this to ₹11,00,672 — but it granted only a collective, lump-sum amount under the conventional heads of compensation, without separately awarding parental consortium to the three children. The appellants approached the Supreme Court, questioning both the income assessment and, more significantly, the absence of a proper consortium award for the children, two of whom were between 18 and 21 years of age and no longer minors.

Although the Supreme Court had issued notice on the limited question of parental consortium, the Bench of Justices N. Kotiswar Singh and N.V. Anjaria took the opportunity to examine the law on consortium comprehensively — covering not just parental consortium for children but spousal consortium for the widow as well.

What the court held

On the income question, the Court found no infirmity in the Tribunal's reliance on direct testimonial evidence from the employer's Director that the deceased earned ₹7,000 a month, and declined to disturb that finding in the appellants' favour.

The more consequential part of the judgment deals with consortium. The Court traced the doctrine's evolution through Gujarat State Road Transport Corporation v. Ramanbhai Prabhatbhai (1987), Rajesh v. Rajbir Singh (2013), National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Pranay Sethi (2017), and Magma General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Nanu Ram (2018), and reaffirmed that any person falling within the wide statutory expression "legal representative" is entitled to maintain a compensation claim — a right that does not depend on financial dependency on the deceased. Citing Manjuri Bera v. Oriental Insurance Co. Ltd. (2007) and National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Birender (2020), the Bench held that even a major, earning child who was not fully dependent on the deceased remains a "legal representative" entitled to compensation, including consortium.

The Court explained the distinct strands of the doctrine in plain terms: spousal consortium compensates a surviving spouse for the loss of companionship, society, cooperation and affection that comes with a conjugal relationship, while parental consortium compensates children for the loss of a parent's aid, protection, affection, guidance, discipline and training. Holding that "consortium in different categories… is an important and indispensable head to award accident claim compensation to make the compensation amount to be just compensation," the Court found that both the Tribunal (which gave the widow a token ₹5,000 and nothing to the children) and the High Court (which lumped everything into an undifferentiated conventional-heads figure) had failed to apply the law correctly. Since the children's dependency on their deceased father was never actually disputed, they too qualified as legal representatives entitled to parental consortium in their own right.

Applying Pranay Sethi's escalation formula — a base consortium figure enhanced by 10% at every three-year interval — the Court fixed ₹40,000 as the base amount payable to each of the four claimants (the widow for spousal consortium, and each of the three children for parental consortium), working out to ₹48,400 per claimant after escalation. It also enhanced the conventional heads of funeral expenses and loss of estate to ₹15,000 each, while retaining the High Court's figure for loss of dependency. The total compensation was recalculated at ₹12,47,272, with the insurer directed to deposit the additional ₹1,46,600 together with 7.5% interest from the date of filing the claim petition, within six weeks, for release to the claimants in equal shares.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

Consortium claims are routinely under-pleaded and under-argued at the Tribunal stage in Haryana's MACT courts, particularly where the deceased leaves behind adult children who are themselves earning or married. This judgment is a direct rebuke of the practice of treating consortium as a single, undifferentiated lump sum tucked into "conventional heads" rather than a distinct entitlement calculated per claimant. Advocates drafting claim petitions on behalf of a deceased's family should now specifically plead spousal consortium for the surviving spouse and parental consortium separately for each surviving child, regardless of the child's age, earning status, or marital status, since dependency in the traditional sense is not the test — qualification as a "legal representative" is.

The ruling is equally useful on appeal or revision where a Tribunal award has bundled consortium into a token figure: this judgment, together with Pranay Sethi and Magma General Insurance, gives counsel a clean basis to argue that the award must be recalculated claimant-by-claimant, with the appropriate three-year escalation applied from the relevant baseline. Given how often MACT claims in Haryana's district courts involve joint families with several surviving children, getting the consortium math right can meaningfully move the final compensation figure, as it did here.

Statutory cross-references

The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 continues to govern MACT claims and has not been affected by the 2023 recodification of the criminal law. Sections 166 and 168, under which this claim was filed and decided, remain unchanged. Where a motor accident also gives rise to a criminal prosecution for rash or negligent driving causing death, the erstwhile Section 304-A, IPC now corresponds to Section 106, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, and the procedural provisions for such trials fall under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 — though, as this judgment illustrates, the outcome of any parallel criminal case has no bearing on a claimant's separate and independent right to full and just compensation before the Tribunal.
