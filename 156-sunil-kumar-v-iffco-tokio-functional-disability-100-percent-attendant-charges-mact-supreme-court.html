Sunil Kumar v. IFFCO Tokio General Insurance: Supreme Court Treats a 75.5% Medical Disability as 100% Functional and Doubles a MACT Award With Proper Attendant Charges (2026 INSC 1145)

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Sunil Kumar (Totally Disabled, represented by wife) v. IFFCO Tokio General Insurance Co. Ltd. (with connected appeal by Sreejith K.S.)
BenchJustice S.V.N. Bhatti and Justice N.V. Anjaria
Date15 October 2026 (one report mentions a judgment PDF dated 17 October 2026)
Citation2026 INSC 1145 · Civil Appeal Nos. 13542 & 13543 of 2026
Key SectionsMotor Vehicles Act, 1988: Sections 166 and 168 (functional disability, attendant charges, loss of earning capacity)

What the case was about

On 22 October 2016, two young men on a scooter were hit near the Paliyekkara toll plaza in Kerala. Sunil Kumar, then 43 and riding pillion, suffered a severe head injury, including an open diffuse axonal injury and a midbrain contusion, along with a penetrating knee wound. He is now totally dependent on others and the appeal was pursued through his wife. The scooter’s driver, Sreejith K.S., aged 22, had hand fractures and a brachial plexus injury. Both claimed compensation from IFFCO Tokio General Insurance Co. Ltd.

The Motor Accident Claims Tribunal at Irinjalakuda awarded Sunil Kumar about ₹19.92 lakh after taking his monthly income at ₹9,000. The Kerala High Court raised the income to ₹15,000 and the award to about ₹36.23 lakh, but it stayed with the Medical Board’s 75.5% disability and allowed only a token sum for attendant charges. Both claimants went to the Supreme Court.

What the Supreme Court held

The Bench of Justice S.V.N. Bhatti and Justice N.V. Anjaria allowed both appeals. The central point is the gap between medical disability (what the board certifies for the limb or organ) and functional disability (what the injury does to the claimant’s ability to earn). For Sunil Kumar, the Court treated a 75.5% medical disability as 100% functional disability, because he needs a lifelong attendant and has lost all working capacity. For Sreejith, a 62% medical disability was treated as 80% functional disability.

On the numbers for Sunil Kumar, the Court took income at ₹15,000 a month, added 25% for future prospects, applied the multiplier of 14 and arrived at ₹31.50 lakh for loss of earning capacity. The larger correction was attendant charges: the lower courts had given a nominal amount (reported as ₹30,000 in one source and ₹1 lakh in another), and the Court replaced it with ₹25.20 lakh calculated by the multiplier method, citing Kajal v. Jagdish Chand (2020), together with ₹10 lakh for future medical expenses. The total came to ₹77,84,394, an increase of ₹41,61,750. Sreejith’s total rose to ₹69,60,880, built on notional income of ₹10,500, a multiplier of 18, attendant charges of ₹22.68 lakh and further heads for pain, amenities and lost marriage prospects. The additional sums carry 6.5% interest from the date of the claim petition and must be deposited by the insurer within six weeks.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

Many injury claims before the Tribunals at Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri are argued on the disability certificate alone. This judgment is a useful reminder that the certificate is evidence, not the final answer. Where the injured person cannot work in their own occupation, or cannot look after themselves, counsel should lead evidence on the nature of the work, the daily dependence on others and the medical board’s own comments, and then argue functional disability separately. The Court has long supported this approach (the report cites Jithendran and a 2026 decision on the same distinction), and the present ruling shows how much it can add to the award.

The second practical lesson is attendant charges. A flat annual or one-time figure is easy to under-prove and easy to under-award. Presenting a monthly cost of a full-time attendant, then multiplying it by a multiplier, gives the Tribunal a ready method with Supreme Court support. Likewise, a claim for future medical expenses should be backed by a doctor’s estimate rather than left as a general plea. Insurers, for their part, should expect appeals on low attendant awards to be taken seriously.

One caution: the award here rests on facts that are severe, a brain injury with permanent total dependence. The ruling does not say functional disability will be 100% for every claimant above a certain medical percentage. Each case still turns on its own evidence of earning capacity and dependence.

Statutory cross-references

The claim arose under Section 166 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (just compensation), with compensation assessed under Section 168. The accident predates the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, so the criminal-law side is not engaged in this civil appeal; if a rash-driving FIR accompanies a present-day accident, the equivalent of IPC Section 279 is BNS Section 281 and of IPC Section 338/337 is BNS Section 125. The assessment follows the structure laid down in National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Pranay Sethi (future prospects) and Sarla Verma (multiplier), which Tribunals already apply, here read with the medical-versus-functional disability line of cases.
