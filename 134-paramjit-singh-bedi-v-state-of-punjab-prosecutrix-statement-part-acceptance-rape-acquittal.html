Paramjit Singh Bedi v. State of Punjab (2026 INSC 1054): Supreme Court Says a Prosecutrix’s Statement Cannot Be Accepted in Part and Rejected in Part, Acquits Accused of Rape After 17 Years

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Paramjit Singh Bedi v. State of Punjab (appeal from P&H High Court)
BenchJustices Ujjal Bhuyan and Atul S. Chandurkar
Date25 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1054 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 992
Key SectionsS. 376 IPC (rape); Ss. 363, 366, 366A IPC (kidnapping and abduction); S. 114A Evidence Act

What the case was about

In June 2009, a Class VIII student in Ludhiana was approached by a man who said he would take her to meet his daughter. According to her, he took her by car to a flat, where they stayed for about two days, then moved her by scooter to an abandoned factory in Malerkotla. Police and her father found them near a railway colony around 25 June 2009. Paramjit Singh Bedi was charged under the IPC provisions on kidnapping or abduction and on rape.

The trial ended in a split verdict. The Sessions Judge at Ludhiana acquitted him of the abduction-related charge, holding that the girl was in fact a major and had accompanied him voluntarily, since she did not try to escape or raise an alarm though she had chances to do so. Yet the same judge convicted him of rape on her word that she was confined and forced, backed by the presence of spermatozoa, reasoning that going along willingly is not the same as consenting to sex. The Punjab and Haryana High Court affirmed, adding the statutory presumption against consent that applies once sexual intercourse is shown. The accused then spent years fighting the case, and the appeal reached the Supreme Court well over a decade later.

What the Supreme Court held

A bench of Justices Ujjal Bhuyan and Atul S. Chandurkar set aside both convictions and acquitted him. The central proposition is that a court cannot accept a prosecutrix’s statement for one purpose and reject it for another. In the bench’s words as reported, there cannot be part acceptance and part rejection of her evidence. If her account of voluntary travel was believed to defeat the abduction charge, the same account could not be treated as unreliable on the question of voluntariness when the rape charge was considered.

The Court found the two allegations factually inseparable. The story of forced confinement was undermined by her silence during multi-day travel through crowded places, by contradictions in her testimony (she said in her examination-in-chief that she came back by car, but in cross-examination said by train), and by the absence of injuries on medical examination. The presumption in the Evidence Act, the bench indicated, cannot rescue a case where the foundational evidence is not credible; it operates only once the prosecution has established the basic facts. A charge of this gravity, the Court said, must rest on hard evidence rather than surmise and a value judgment about the accused’s conduct. Moral impropriety, even if suspected, is not proof beyond reasonable doubt.

Reports give the date of the ruling as 25 September 2026 (Verdictum’s weekly digest and LiveLaw) while one Verdictum article gives 26 September; I have used the former.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Sessions courts in Haryana frequently deal with cases where a complainant’s account is partly accepted and partly doubted. This judgment gives defence counsel a clear argument against such internal inconsistency in findings. If the trial court has already disbelieved the prosecutrix on any material point connected to the same episode, the defence can argue that it cannot then lean on the remainder to convict. The argument is strongest where, as here, the acquittal on one charge rests on the very facts that the conviction on the other charge needs.

Prosecutors should take note in the opposite direction. Charge-sheet strategy matters: where the evidence of voluntary movement is strong, the case on the sexual offence needs independent corroboration, such as injuries, prompt complaint or consistent testimony. Reliance on the presumption alone is risky. Judges at the trial and High Court level should also record why they treat a witness as credible on one charge and not another.

It is equally important to read the ruling narrowly. The Supreme Court did not say that an absence of injuries or a failure to raise an alarm will defeat every rape case, and it did not dilute the protection of minors under the special statute. The outcome turned on the specific contradictions and the finding on the prosecutrix’s age and voluntariness in this record.

Statutory cross-references

Section 376 IPC corresponds to Section 64 BNS, 2023. Section 363 IPC (kidnapping) corresponds to Section 137(2) BNS, and Section 366 IPC (abducting a woman to compel marriage or intercourse) to Section 87 BNS. Section 366A IPC has no direct counterpart; procuring a child is now dealt with under Section 96 BNS. The presumption in Section 114A of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 is now Section 120 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. The case arose under the IPC because the events are from 2009.
