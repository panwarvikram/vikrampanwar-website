Sanjay Badaya v. State of Rajasthan (2026 INSC 1065): Supreme Court Says Pre-Trial Incarceration Cannot Masquerade as Punitive Detention, Grants Bail in a ₹979 Crore Tender-Fraud Case

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Sanjay Badaya v. State of Rajasthan (with Shubhanshu Dixit v. State of Rajasthan)
BenchJustice Dipankar Datta and Justice Sheel Nagu (judgment authored by Justice Datta)
Date29 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1065
Key SectionsPrevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (ss. 7(c), 9, 10, 12, 13(1)(a) r/w 13(2)); IPC ss. 409, 467, 468, 471, 120-B; bail under the CrPC/BNSS; Article 21

What the case was about

The case arises from an FIR registered by the Anti-Corruption Bureau, Jaipur, on 30 October 2024. The prosecution alleges that two private firms used forged work-experience and completion certificates, purportedly issued by IRCON International, to win about 104 Jal Jeevan Mission tenders worth roughly ₹979.45 crore in Rajasthan's Public Health Engineering Department.

Two men were before the Supreme Court. Shubhanshu Dixit, then Secretary of the Rajasthan Water Supply and Sewerage Management Board, is accused of sitting on complaints about the forged certificates and issuing Finance Committee minutes that allegedly helped ineligible bidders. Sanjay Badaya, a private individual, is described as an intermediary for the then PHED Minister who collected cash bribes. Dixit was arrested on 17 February 2026 and Badaya on 11 May 2026. The Rajasthan High Court refused Dixit bail on 1 June 2026 and Badaya on 13 August 2026. Both came to the Supreme Court.

What the Court held

The Bench allowed both appeals and granted bail. Its central line, which will be quoted in bail arguments for some time, is that pre-trial incarceration cannot masquerade as punitive detention when the commencement and conclusion of the trial are not in sight.

The Court did not wave away the State's case that economic offences involving a deep-rooted conspiracy deserve serious treatment. It accepted that. But it weighed that against the actual state of the proceedings. Investigation against the appellants was complete and chargesheets had been filed (April 2026, with a supplementary chargesheet in June 2026). Charges had not been framed. The prosecution cited 124 witnesses, sanction against six other public servants had arrived only on 6 September 2026, two accused were absconding, and investigation against some others was still open. On those facts a long trial was inevitable.

The Court also noted that the case rests largely on documents and electronic records already seized and lying with the investigating agency, so the risk of tampering was substantially diminished. Parity weighed too: a co-accused had been granted bail by the High Court, and the former PHED Minister had been granted bail by the Supreme Court in the connected PMLA matter in December 2025. Continued custody, the Bench said, would serve no fruitful purpose.

Bail terms were left to the trial court, with directions that the appellants join the investigation when called, attend the trial diligently, and face cancellation for any breach. The Court expressly gave no opinion on the merits or the evidentiary value of the records; the appellants remain presumed innocent.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Trial courts and the Punjab & Haryana High Court regularly see bail pleas in cases involving forged documents, tender manipulation, cooperative-society and bank frauds, and Prevention of Corruption Act prosecutions. The usual State response is that the offence is economic, the sums are large, and the conspiracy runs deep. Badaya gives defence counsel a clean structure for answering it: (1) show that the investigation against the client is complete and the chargesheet filed; (2) show that the evidence is documentary and already in the agency's custody, which answers the tampering objection; (3) show that the trial will not start soon, using the number of witnesses, pending supplementary investigation, absconding accused and pending sanctions; and (4) show parity with co-accused who have already been released.

Equally, prosecutors should note what the judgment does not do. It does not say economic offences are bailable as a class, and it does not touch the merits. The ruling turns on delay and the state of evidence. Where the accused is alleged to hold influence over witnesses, or evidence is still being collected, the same reasoning will not carry the day. For practitioners at Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, the practical takeaway is to build the bail petition around a dated chronology of the case (arrest, chargesheet, supplementary chargesheet, sanctions, framing of charge) rather than leaning only on the generic Article 21 argument.

The order also fits a visible pattern: the Supreme Court has been repeatedly reminding courts below that the length of custody has to be measured against the realistic length of the trial, particularly where the accused is not a flight risk and the evidence is secured.

Statutory cross-references

The FIR was registered under the IPC and the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, which continues to apply unchanged. For new cases, IPC s. 409 (criminal breach of trust by a public servant or agent) corresponds to s. 316(5) BNS; s. 467 (forgery of a valuable security) to s. 338 BNS; s. 468 (forgery for the purpose of cheating) to s. 336(3) BNS; s. 471 (using a forged document as genuine) to s. 340(2) BNS; and s. 120-B (criminal conspiracy) to s. 61(2) BNS. Regular bail, formerly under s. 439 CrPC, is now governed by s. 483 BNSS.
