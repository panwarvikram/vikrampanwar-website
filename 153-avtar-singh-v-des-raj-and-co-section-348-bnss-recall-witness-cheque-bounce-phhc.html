Avtar Singh v. M/s Des Raj & Co.: P&H High Court Says Section 348 BNSS Cannot Be Used to Reopen and Rebuild a Defence After Evidence Is Closed in a Cheque Bounce Case

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — Avtar Singh v. M/s Des Raj & Co.
BenchJustice Sumeet Goel (Single Judge)
Date1 October 2026 (as reported by Courtbook; LiveLaw report dated 6 October 2026)
CitationCRM-M-54995-2026 (case number as reported by Courtbook; neutral citation not confirmed)
Key SectionsSection 348 BNSS (earlier Section 311 CrPC); Section 138 NI Act; Section 528 BNSS

What the case was about

A firm, M/s Des Raj & Co., filed a complaint under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act alleging that Avtar Singh had taken ₹10.30 lakh in instalments between December 2019 and June 2020 and had issued a cheque dated 27 August 2021 that bounced for insufficient funds. In his Section 313 CrPC statement the accused denied the loan. He said the cheque was a security cheque and that the money was in fact owed to him for crops he sold in 2018-19 and 2019-20 through connected firms.

The accused cross-examined the complainant’s representative at length and examined a Market Committee official as DW-1. When another proposed defence witness declined to depose, the trial court closed the defence evidence on 18 May 2026 and fixed the case for arguments. Only then did the accused apply to reopen the defence, recall DW-1, bring on record J-Forms and the account books of another firm, and examine a handwriting expert. The Judicial Magistrate at Malerkotla refused the application on 8 September 2026 as belated and likely to delay the trial, and the accused came to the High Court.

What the court held

Justice Goel dismissed the petition. Applying the settled principles on Section 311 CrPC, now Section 348 BNSS, he said the power is wide but is to be exercised judiciously and with cogent reasons, not routinely. Evidence can be summoned or a witness recalled only where it is essential to a just decision, and not because a party wants to improve, supplement or reconstruct its case once the evidence has closed.

On the facts, the court found that the accused had not been denied a fair opportunity. His crop-sale defence and the J-Forms had already been put to the complainant’s witness in cross-examination, and a Market Committee official had already been examined. He had also not challenged the 18 May order closing his evidence when it was passed. The court added that the mere relevance of a document to the defence does not justify reopening the whole defence evidence, and that a handwriting comparison was not necessary at that stage. Exercising its inherent jurisdiction under Section 528 BNSS, it declined to substitute its own view merely because another view was possible, and described the application as an afterthought. The observations were expressly stated not to affect the merits of the pending complaint.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Section 138 complaints make up a large share of the docket in the Magistrate courts at Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, and recall applications are a common late-stage tactic. This order gives complainants a clean authority to resist an application filed after the defence has been closed, and it warns accused persons that the time to lead documents, call banks or firms, or seek expert opinion is before closure, not after.

For the defence, the practical lessons are specific. Decide at the Section 313 stage which witnesses and documents are needed, summon them early, and challenge an order closing evidence immediately if it is wrongly passed. If a recall is genuinely needed, the application should explain why the material could not be produced earlier and why it bears directly on the outcome. For the complainant, the order is a reminder to oppose such applications on the record, pointing to the cross-examination already conducted, rather than relying on delay alone. Note that other P&H benches have allowed additional evidence to cure genuine, unintentional omissions in NI Act cases, so the outcome will still turn on the facts.

Statutory cross-references

Section 348 BNSS corresponds to Section 311 CrPC (power to summon material witnesses or examine persons present). Section 313 CrPC (examination of the accused) corresponds to Section 351 BNSS, and Section 528 BNSS to Section 482 CrPC (inherent powers). Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 is unchanged.
