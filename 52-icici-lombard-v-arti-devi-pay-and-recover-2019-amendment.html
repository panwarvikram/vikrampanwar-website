ICICI Lombard General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Arti Devi & Ors.: Allahabad High Court Says the 2019 Motor Vehicles Amendment Didn't Kill the "Pay and Recover" Principle

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseICICI Lombard General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Arti Devi & Ors., Allahabad High Court (First Appeal From Order Nos. 1780, 1776, 1777 & 1789 of 2024)
BenchJustice Kshitij Shailendra
Date31 January 2025
Citation2025:AHC:14110 · MANU/UP/0194/2025
Key SectionsS. 149 (pre-2019) & S. 150 (post-2019), Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 · S. 147(6), MV Act

What the case was about

On 29 May 2022, a road accident in Kanpur Dehat involved a bus insured with ICICI Lombard and a car in which one Pradeep Kumar was travelling; he died on the spot. His legal heirs filed a claim before the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal, Kanpur Dehat, which awarded Rs. 20,11,800 against the owner and driver of the bus, while directing that the insurer pay the amount first and recover it later from the owner. ICICI Lombard appealed to the High Court, along with three connected appeals raising the identical point of law.

The insurer's argument went to the root of a question that had, until this judgment, not been squarely decided anywhere in the country: does the "pay and recover" doctrine — built up over two decades of Supreme Court rulings starting with National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Swaran Singh (2004) 3 SCC 297 — survive the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019? The 2019 amendment replaced the old Chapter XI of the Act with effect from 1 April 2022, and in doing so replaced Section 149 with a new Section 150. Crucially, the proviso to the old Section 149(4), which had expressly given insurers the right to recover from the owner any sum paid to discharge the owner's liability, was not carried over into the new Section 150(4). ICICI Lombard argued that this deletion was deliberate, that Parliament's Statement of Objects and Reasons showed an intent to curb fraudulent claims and tighten insurer liability, and that every precedent built on the old proviso — including Swaran Singh itself — simply had no application to accidents occurring after 1 April 2022.

What the court held

Justice Kshitij Shailendra rejected that argument in a detailed judgment that walks through the old and new provisions side by side. The Court's reasoning turns on a distinction it drew between the insurer's liability to pay and its ultimate, final liability to bear the loss. Section 147(6) of the amended Act (renumbered from the old Section 147(5)) still requires an insurer to indemnify the persons covered by a policy "notwithstanding anything contained in any other law for the time being in force." That indemnification obligation, the Court held, was never touched by the 2019 amendment. What Section 150(2) does is give the insurer defences that let it avoid having an award passed against it directly — it does not extinguish the insurer's underlying duty to indemnify the owner in the first instance.

The judgment also draws a careful textual distinction between the words "paid" and "payable" as used in the old proviso, holding that the insurer's right to recover was always tied to sums it had actually paid to satisfy an award, a right that flows from the general law of indemnity and unjust enrichment rather than from the proviso alone. On that basis, the Court held that even without an express proviso, the recovery right survives, because nothing in the new Section 150 or in Section 147(6) says otherwise, and courts cannot read an implied abolition of a well-settled protective doctrine into a bare legislative silence, particularly in welfare legislation. The Court noted pointedly that the Motor Vehicles Act's compulsory-insurance scheme exists to protect third-party accident victims, who are strangers to the insurance contract and should not be made to suffer, or wait years, over a dispute between the insurer and the vehicle owner. On this reasoning, the Court dismissed all four appeals and upheld the Tribunal's pay-and-recover direction, holding that Swaran Singh and the long line of authority following it remain good law for accidents occurring after 1 April 2022, exactly as they did before.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

This is the first reasoned judgment to confront, head-on, an argument insurers had been quietly preparing to run in Tribunals across the country: that the 2019 recodification gave them a clean exit from pay-and-recover. Advocates for claimants in Haryana's MACTs, several of which see this exact objection raised by insurer counsel in written statements, now have a detailed, citable answer rather than having to reconstruct the argument from first principles each time. Advocates for insurers, for their part, should note that the door is not entirely shut — the Court's reasoning rests on statutory interpretation rather than a Supreme Court ruling directly on the 2019 text, and the point is squarely capable of reaching the Supreme Court in a future appeal.

Locally, this closes a gap this blog flagged only recently: our previous piece on Reliance General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Om Parkash involved a 2009 accident governed entirely by the old Section 149, so it never had occasion to address whether pay-and-recover survives for post-2022 accidents. Given how much of Haryana's MACT docket now involves accidents on the Kundli-Manesar-Palwal Expressway, NH-48, and other high-traffic corridors occurring well after April 2022, this is precisely the fact pattern practitioners in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani will increasingly encounter, and the Allahabad High Court's reasoning is currently the most detailed judicial word on the point available to cite before the Punjab & Haryana High Court.

Statutory cross-references

The comparison at the heart of this case is entirely internal to the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988: Section 149 as it stood before 1 April 2022 (with its proviso to sub-section (4)) against Section 150 as substituted by the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019, together with Section 147(5) (old) and its renumbered equivalent, Section 147(6) (new). These are civil/regulatory provisions and are unaffected by the 2023 criminal law recodification (BNS/BNSS/BSA), which has no bearing on motor accident compensation claims under the Motor Vehicles Act.
