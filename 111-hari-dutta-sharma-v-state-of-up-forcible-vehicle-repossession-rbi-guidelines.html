Hari Dutta Sharma v. State of U.P.: Supreme Court Says a Financier Can't Repossess a Hypothecated Vehicle by Force or Without Notice

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseHari Dutta Sharma v. State of U.P. & Ors.
BenchJustices P.S. Narasimha & Alok Aradhe, Supreme Court of India
Date16 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 998 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 942
Key SectionsS. 35A, Banking Regulation Act, 1949 · Arts. 14 & 21, Constitution of India · RBI Fair Practices Code for Lenders, 2003

What the case was about

A truck bought on a commercial vehicle loan is a working asset, not a luxury — the owner's entire livelihood usually depends on it staying on the road. Hari Dutta Sharma had taken a loan of roughly Rs. 10.4 lakh in 2019 from a non-banking finance company for exactly this kind of truck, repayable over 75 monthly instalments and secured by hypothecating the vehicle itself. When repayments fell behind, the company had already repossessed the truck once in 2022 and released it after a part-payment. In April 2023, while the vehicle sat parked under CCTV surveillance at a consignor's premises, four unidentified men broke open its steering lock at around 1 a.m. and drove it away. No notice had gone out beforehand. Sharma lodged a police complaint the same day believing his truck had been stolen; it hadn't been — his own financier had taken it, and had it sold within months for Rs. 4.5 lakh while still treating him as liable for the shortfall.

His criminal complaint before the Magistrate went nowhere once the police reported that the vehicle had simply been "confiscated for default." A writ petition before the Allahabad High Court fared no better: it was dismissed on the footing that he had approached the court too late and had, in any event, defaulted on the loan. That combination — a defaulting borrower and a delay in filing — is exactly the kind of reasoning financiers rely on to treat repossession as a closed chapter once a sale has gone through. The Supreme Court was asked to decide whether it really is.

What the court held

The bench of Justices P.S. Narasimha and Alok Aradhe set aside the High Court's order and drew a sharp line between a financier's right to recover its security and the manner in which that right can be exercised. A hypothecation agreement gives the lender an interest in the vehicle, but it does not license self-help through force, deceit, or a procedure that leaves the borrower with no real opportunity to respond. Drawing on the Court's own 2007 ruling in ICICI Bank Ltd. v. Prakash Kaur, which had already condemned the use of "goondas" to seize vehicles, and on the Reserve Bank of India's Fair Practices Code and successive guidelines on debt collection, the bench held that recovery of loans and seizure of vehicles can only happen through lawful means.

The Court examined the loan agreement's own repossession clause and found it legally defective on four counts: it let the borrower's rights over the vehicle lapse "ipso facto without any notice" the moment a default occurred; it authorised recovery agents to enter "any place or places" in search of the asset, which itself offends RBI's guidelines; it prescribed no actual procedure for taking possession or for sale; and it let the company waive even the notice period at its own discretion. A clause that lets one party unilaterally strip away the very safeguards meant to protect the other, the Court said, is not a valid repossession clause at all — whatever it might say on paper.

Applying that framework to the facts, the Court found that no seven-day notice had gone out before the truck was taken, so the right to repossess had never even accrued to the company in the first place. Breaking open a steering lock at 1 a.m., without a signed possession memorandum, was, in the Court's words, a mode of taking possession that "bears every mark of the very 'goondaism'" it had condemned in Prakash Kaur. The Court also brushed aside the delay objection: Sharma had filed a police complaint the very day his truck went missing and had continued receiving traffic challans for it long after the company claimed to have sold it — hardly the conduct of someone sleeping over his rights. Calling him "a man of modest means" whose livelihood depended on the vehicle, the Court held the seizure violated his rights under Articles 14 and 21, directed the company to refund the Rs. 4.5 lakh sale price with 6% interest, awarded him Rs. 10 lakh in compensation, and asked the RBI to actually enforce guidelines that, until now, had "existed only on paper."

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Vehicle-loan defaults and midnight repossessions are not a rare curiosity in Haryana's district courts — they are a routine fact of practice, particularly around Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri where commercial vehicles, tractors and two-wheelers financed through NBFCs are common collateral for small transport and agricultural livelihoods. Clients frequently arrive after the vehicle is already gone, unsure whether they have any remedy once a "confiscation" has taken place. This judgment gives practitioners a concrete framework to test any repossession against: was a notice period actually built into the contract and actually followed; was possession taken peacefully and with a signed memorandum; and did the financier's own contractual clause meet the four-fold test the Court has now laid down.

It also matters for drafting and advisory work, not just litigation after the fact. A repossession or hypothecation clause that mirrors the "ipso facto, without notice" language the Court struck down is now vulnerable to challenge regardless of which financier uses it, and advocates reviewing loan documents for clients — whether borrowers or local financiers — should flag such clauses before they become the subject of a writ or a civil suit for damages. For a borrower whose vehicle has already been taken, this ruling supports a claim for both restitution of sale proceeds with interest and separate compensation for loss of livelihood, framed as a violation of Articles 14 and 21, rather than treating the matter as a closed civil dispute over an unpaid loan.

Statutory cross-references

The judgment turns on Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, which empowers the RBI to issue binding directions to banks and NBFCs, and on Articles 14 and 21 of the Constitution, both of which remain unchanged under the current framework. The borrower's original criminal complaint had been filed under Section 156(3) of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, seeking a police investigation into what he believed was theft — that provision now corresponds to Section 175(3) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which practitioners should cite when filing an equivalent complaint today. The underlying repossession clause was tested against the general fairness requirements of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, particularly the principle that a contractual term cannot be so one-sided as to be illusory.
