Ku. Chetna Sharma v. Pawan Sharma & Ors.: Supreme Court Says a Permanently Paralyzed Child's Disability Must Be Treated as 100% for Compensation, Not the Certified Percentage

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Ku. Chetna Sharma v. Pawan Sharma & Ors.
BenchJustices N. Kotiswar Singh & K. Vinod Chandran
Date8 July 2026 (order); reported by LawBeat and cross-verified via Supreme Court Cases (EBC), 31 July 2026
Citation2026 INSC 676 · Civil Appeal @ SLP(C) No. 20897 of 2024
Key SectionsSs. 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

On 23 June 2018, a five-year-old girl travelling with her mother and grandmother suffered grievous spinal injuries when the car they were in met with an accident caused by rash and negligent driving. The injuries left the child permanently paralyzed. A disability certificate placed her permanent disability at 80%. The Motor Accident Claims Tribunal awarded ₹14,84,233, which the High Court enhanced by a further ₹6,00,000 in appeal. Still dissatisfied with the quantum given the lifelong nature of the child's condition, the claimant approached the Supreme Court seeking further enhancement.

The appellant's counsel leaned on the Supreme Court's 2024 decision in Baby Sakshi Greola v. Manzoor Ahmad Simon (2024 SCC OnLine SC 3692), which had dealt with a strikingly similar fact pattern — a seven-year-old left paralyzed in a motor accident. The submission was that the same method of computation used in that case should apply here, with an upward adjustment for the rise in cost of living between the two accidents (2009 in Baby Sakshi Greola, 2018 in this case). The claimant also pressed for recurring future expenses: physiotherapy, diapers, and the occasional use of a catheter, all flowing from the child's incontinence.

What the court held

The Bench of Justices N. Kotiswar Singh and K. Vinod Chandran agreed that Baby Sakshi Greola applied squarely, subject to a reasonable upward revision for the intervening rise in living costs. The judgment's most consequential holding, however, concerns how "disability" itself should be measured for compensation purposes. The medical certificate on record assessed the child's permanent disability at 80%, not 100%. But the treating doctor, examined as a witness, testified that whatever the certified percentage, the disability would last the child's entire lifetime. On that basis, the Court held: "we are of the opinion that the disability of the child has to be taken at 100%." In other words, once medical evidence establishes that a disability is permanent and lifelong, the Tribunal or Court computing compensation is not bound by a lower disability percentage recorded on a certificate — the practical, functional loss of earning capacity for a child who will never be able to work independently is total, regardless of what the certificate says.

Following Baby Sakshi Greola, both notional income and attendant charges were computed on the minimum wages payable to a skilled labourer (₹9,560 per month in 2018), since a permanently paralyzed child would need a skilled attendant for life. The Court added 40% for future prospects and applied a multiplier of 18. It also trimmed the claim for recurring future-care expenses from the ₹10,000 a month sought to ₹5,000 a month, to be multiplied by the child's average life expectancy, while deleting a separate ₹25,000 award for "discomfort and inconvenience" as duplicative of the pain-and-suffering head. The final award, totalling ₹87,15,137, broke down across eight heads: loss of earning capacity, medical expenditure, hospitalization-period incidental expenses, attendant expenses, pain and suffering, loss of marriage prospects, future care expenses, and transportation and special diet — payable with interest at the Tribunal's originally awarded rate, within two months, after adjusting sums already disbursed.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

Child pedestrian and passenger injuries are a recurring category before the MACT at Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, given the volume of traffic on the Rohtak-Bhiwani and Hisar-Charkhi Dadri stretches. This judgment gives claimants' counsel a concrete argument to resist a Tribunal pegging compensation strictly to a disability certificate's stated percentage when the medical evidence otherwise establishes permanence. The practical lesson is evidentiary: it is not enough to file the disability certificate and rest. Counsel should ensure the treating doctor is examined and specifically asked to confirm, on record, that the disability is lifelong and irreversible — that single line of testimony was what allowed the Supreme Court to treat an 80%-certified disability as 100% for compensation purposes.

The judgment is equally useful on the computation side. It reaffirms that for a child with no proven income, notional income should be pegged to the prevailing minimum wage of a skilled labourer (checked against the relevant state's minimum wage notification for the year of the accident), with the standard 40% future-prospects addition and an age-appropriate multiplier drawn from the Sarla Verma/Pranay Sethi table. It also separately recognises future recurring-care costs as a distinct head from one-time attendant charges, and treats loss of marriage prospects as a legitimate head in cases of severe, visible permanent disability in a minor. Advocates drafting Detailed Accident Reports and claim petitions in Haryana's MACTs should plead each of these heads separately, rather than clubbing them under a generic "pain and suffering" claim, since the Supreme Court here specifically pruned an overlapping head for exactly that reason.

Statutory cross-references

The compensation framework under Sections 166 and 168 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 is unaffected by the 2023 recodification of criminal law, since MACT proceedings remain civil in character. Where the same accident also gives rise to a prosecution for rash or negligent driving, the erstwhile Section 279 IPC and Section 304-A IPC offences now correspond to Section 281 and Section 106 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 respectively; a conviction or acquittal in that parallel criminal case does not bind the Tribunal's independent assessment of compensation, though the underlying evidence of rashness or negligence is often common to both proceedings.
