Akbar Khan v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Says a Muslim Couple's Marriage After Puberty Cannot Be Undone by a Family's Disapproval

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / CaseAkbar Khan & Anr. v. State of Punjab & Ors., Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Vikram Aggarwal
Date15 September 2026
CitationReported by LiveLaw, 15 September 2026
Key SectionsArt. 195, Mulla's Principles of Mohammedan Law · Art. 21, Constitution of India

What the case was about

Two petitioners, both Muslim and governed by Muslim Personal Law, approached the Punjab & Haryana High Court seeking protection of their life and personal liberty. The first petitioner was 26 years old. The second, the young woman he had married, was 17 years and eight months old at the time of filing. The couple said they had known each other for some time, developed a liking for one another, and decided to marry. When the woman's father opposed the match, she left her parental home in the district of Ludhiana on 22 August 2026 and performed Nikah with the first petitioner that same day, as per Muslim rites.

Apprehending danger from the woman's family members, named as respondents in the petition, the couple submitted a representation to the police on 30 August 2026 seeking protection. That representation went unanswered. With no response forthcoming and the threat of harm still live, the couple moved the High Court under its writ jurisdiction, invoking Article 21's guarantee of life and personal liberty.

The State did not seriously dispute either the facts placed before the Court or the legal position the petitioners' counsel relied upon — leaving the Court to decide the petition largely on settled law rather than a contested record.

What the court held

Justice Vikram Aggarwal grounded the ruling in a specific and long-settled proposition of Muslim Personal Law: a person governed by that law who has attained puberty is competent to enter into a valid marriage of their own choosing, without needing a guardian's consent. The Court noted that under Article 195 of Sir Dinshah Fardunji Mulla's Principles of Mohammedan Law (10th Edition, 1933), puberty is presumed, absent evidence to the contrary, on completion of fifteen years of age — and a marriage contracted after that point is void only if it was brought about without the person's own consent.

Applying that principle to the facts, the Court observed that although the woman was 17 years and eight months old, "the fact remains that the petitioners, being Muslims, are governed by the Muslim Personal Laws," and that under those laws, a boy or girl who has attained the presumed age of puberty "is at liberty to marry anyone of his or her liking." The Court drew on two Coordinate Bench rulings to anchor this position: Kammu v. State of Haryana (2010), which held that a person who has attained puberty may marry whom they choose, with a guardian having no right to interfere where the match is otherwise an equal one, and Gulam Deen v. State of Punjab (2022), which clarified that in petitions of this kind, the Court is not being asked to certify the marriage's validity but only to address a genuine apprehension of danger to life and liberty under Article 21.

Importantly, the Court was careful to keep those two questions separate. It did not pronounce on whether the marriage itself was valid — that remains a question for another forum, in another proceeding, if it is ever raised. What it did was protect the couple's constitutional right not to be threatened or harmed simply because their families disapproved of a marriage entered into by consenting adults, or by a minor found competent to consent under her personal law. Disposing of the petition, the Court directed the Senior Superintendent of Police (Rural), District Ludhiana, to consider the couple's pending representation dated 30 August 2026 and take whatever action the facts warranted, in accordance with law.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Protection petitions of this kind are a recurring feature of practice before the Punjab & Haryana High Court, and they surface regularly out of Haryana's district courts too, wherever a couple marries against family wishes and the local police are slow, or reluctant, to act on a complaint of threatened harm. This ruling is a useful reminder that such petitions are decided on a narrow and specific footing: the couple does not need the Court to bless their marriage, only to protect them while that marriage — valid or not — stands unchallenged in any competent forum. Drafting a protection petition around Article 21 and the couple's immediate safety, rather than around proving the marriage's validity, keeps the relief squarely within what courts are actually willing to grant at this stage.

For Muslim clients specifically, the ruling is also a working reminder of where Personal Law and the general law diverge. The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, sets eighteen as the general marriageable age for women across communities and criminalises marriages below that threshold; Muslim Personal Law, as this and earlier P&H rulings recognise, treats puberty — presumed at fifteen absent evidence otherwise — as the threshold for capacity to marry of one's own choice. Courts across India remain divided on how these two regimes interact, and this judgment does not purport to resolve that tension; it simply follows the line of Punjab & Haryana precedent that has, for over a decade, protected a couple's safety without adjudicating the underlying validity dispute. Practitioners advising Muslim clients in this age band should flag this unsettled area candidly, and should not read a protection order of this kind as a guarantee that the marriage itself will survive a properly framed challenge.

Statutory cross-references

This ruling turns on the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937, and the customary sources it preserves, rather than on any provision of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. Where a family's opposition to a marriage of this kind escalates into actual threats, coercion, or confinement, the relevant criminal provisions would now fall under the BNS — principally Sections 115 and 351, BNS, 2023 (voluntarily causing hurt, and criminal intimidation) in place of the corresponding Sections 323 and 506 of the erstwhile Indian Penal Code, 1860. The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, remains the relevant general-law statute against which the personal-law position discussed above continues to be tested.
