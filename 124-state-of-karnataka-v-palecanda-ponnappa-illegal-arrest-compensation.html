State of Karnataka v. Palecanda Ponnappa @ Vishal: Supreme Court Refuses to Disturb ₹5 Lakh Compensation for an Illegal Arrest

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseState of Karnataka v. Palecanda Ponnappa @ Vishal
BenchJustices Vikram Nath & Sandeep Mehta
Date22 September 2026 (SC order); 15 July 2026 (Karnataka HC judgment, Nagaprasanna, J.)
CitationSLP (Crl) No. 16734/2026 (dismissed)
Key SectionsArt. 21, Constitution of India · S. 35, BNSS, 2023 (Ss. 41 & 41-A, CrPC, 1973) · Ss. 238, 239 & 351, BNS, 2023

What the case was about

The respondent ran a homestay in Kodagu, Karnataka, and was arrested in connection with a case in which a foreign national guest alleged she had been sexually assaulted by a staff member on the property. He was named as accused no. 2, but on the record he was not present when the alleged assault took place and was not himself accused of committing it; the case against him rested instead on allegations bearing on what he did, or failed to do, once the complaint surfaced — the kind of conduct that BNS provisions on screening an offender, on omitting to inform authorities of an offence, and on criminal intimidation are meant to capture. He was picked up on the evening of 18 April 2026, kept in informal custody overnight, formally shown as arrested only at 5 a.m. the next day, and was not produced before a magistrate until 11:30 p.m. that same day — a gap of roughly eighteen hours between arrest and production that would itself invite scrutiny under the constitutional requirement to produce an arrested person "without unnecessary delay." He remained in judicial custody for thirteen days before being released on bail on 2 May 2026.

He challenged the arrest before the Karnataka High Court. Justice M. Nagaprasanna held the arrest illegal, reasoning that the offences actually attributed to him were non-cognizable in character, so the police had no unilateral power to arrest him without a warrant or a magistrate's order in the first place, and that an arrest is not something to be reached for as a routine step in an investigation merely because the power exists on paper. Drawing on the Supreme Court's own line of authority on arrest — including the landmark guidelines in D.K. Basu, the caution against mechanical arrest for offences carrying lesser punishment in Arnesh Kumar, and more recent reiterations in cases involving press freedom and undertrial safeguards — the High Court traced the source of the power to award compensation to Article 21 itself and the Supreme Court's earlier ruling in Sube Singh v. State of Haryana, and directed the State to pay ₹5,00,000 to the respondent. Karnataka carried the matter to the Supreme Court by way of a special leave petition, without disturbing the ongoing investigation into the underlying offence.

What the court held

A Bench of Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta declined to interfere with the High Court's order and dismissed the State's petition. The Supreme Court did not disturb the finding that the arrest itself was unlawful, nor the quantum the High Court had fixed as compensation. In doing so, the Court kept two questions carefully separate — whether an arrest was lawfully made, and whether an investigation may lawfully continue — and treated them as answerable independently of each other. The underlying FIR was not quashed and the police retained the liberty to carry the investigation forward on its merits; what the Supreme Court affirmed was narrower and, for that reason, sharper: that an arrest carried out in breach of the settled safeguards on cognizability and prompt production before a magistrate attracts a compensatory remedy under Article 21, regardless of how the investigation into the substantive offence eventually turns out. The order is a reminder that a constitutional tort for illegal arrest is not swallowed up by, and does not depend on, the final outcome of the criminal case that gave rise to the arrest.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Claims for compensation arising out of police excess are less commonly litigated before the district courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani than they perhaps should be, and this judgment gives practitioners fresh, current Supreme Court authority to build such claims on. Two features of the ruling are directly usable. First, the cognizability of the specific offence alleged is a threshold question that must be checked before assuming an arrest was even lawful in the first place; counsel defending a client arrested for what turns out to be a non-cognizable offence, without a warrant or a magistrate's prior order, has a straightforward basis to challenge the arrest and pursue compensation, independent of whatever defence is later run in the criminal case itself. Second, the roughly eighteen-hour gap between this respondent's arrest and his production before a magistrate is exactly the kind of delay that Haryana practitioners should be alert to; production "without unnecessary delay" is not a formality, and unexplained delay strengthens a compensation claim considerably.

For advocates drafting a writ petition or a civil suit seeking damages for wrongful arrest or detention, this case is useful precedent for pleading Article 21 as the constitutional source of the remedy, distinct from any statutory compensation scheme, and for arguing that the investigation's eventual outcome — a chargesheet, a discharge, or an acquittal — is irrelevant to whether the arrest itself was lawful at the moment it was made. It is equally useful on the other side of the bar, for counsel representing the State or the police, as a reminder that resisting such a claim on the ground that "the investigation is still pending" or "the accused was ultimately not exonerated" will not, by itself, answer a properly framed challenge to the legality of the arrest.

Statutory cross-references

The offences originally invoked against the respondent — causing disappearance of evidence or giving false information to screen an offender, intentional omission to give information of an offence by a person bound to inform, and criminal intimidation — now sit at Sections 238, 239 and 351 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, corresponding respectively to the erstwhile Sections 201, 202 and 506 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860. The arrest-discretion and notice safeguards that the Karnataka High Court invoked, previously found in Sections 41 and 41-A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, now correspond to Section 35 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. Practitioners in Haryana relying on this judgment in a fresh proceeding should plead the BNS and BNSS numbering where the arrest in question post-dates 1 July 2024, and the corresponding IPC/CrPC provisions only where the arrest itself predates that transition.
