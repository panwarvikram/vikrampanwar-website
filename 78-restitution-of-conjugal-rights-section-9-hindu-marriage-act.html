Restitution of Conjugal Rights Under Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act: What a Decree Actually Does, and Why It's Rarely the Whole Answer

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing ProvisionS. 9, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 · S. 22, Special Marriage Act, 1954
Leading AuthoritySaroj Rani v. Sudarshan Kumar Chadha
CitationAIR 1984 SC 1562 · (1984) 4 SCC 90
Related ProvisionS. 13(1A)(ii), Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 (divorce on non-resumption after decree)
ForumDistrict Court / Family Court having jurisdiction

What the remedy is about

Restitution of conjugal rights is one of the older and more misunderstood remedies in Indian matrimonial law. Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 says that where either the husband or the wife has, without reasonable excuse, withdrawn from the society of the other, the aggrieved spouse may petition the District Court for restitution of conjugal rights, and the court, if satisfied of the truth of the petition and finding no legal ground to refuse it, may pass a decree accordingly. An identical remedy exists for civil and inter-faith marriages under Section 22 of the Special Marriage Act, 1954.

In everyday district court practice, this petition tends to surface in one of two situations. The first is a marriage where one spouse, typically the wife, has moved back to her parental home after a dispute and the husband genuinely wants her to return, with no divorce or separation yet contemplated by either side. The second, more common in contested matters, is defensive or strategic: a husband facing a maintenance claim under Section 144 BNSS or a domestic violence complaint under the DV Act, 2005, files for restitution to signal to the court that he is willing to cohabit and that the separation is the wife's doing, not his. Understanding which of these two postures a case falls into matters enormously for how the petition is likely to be received.

To succeed, the petitioner must show three things: a valid, subsisting marriage; that the other spouse has withdrawn from the petitioner's society; and that this withdrawal is without reasonable excuse. "Withdrawal from society" does not require the couple to be living in separate cities — it can exist even under one roof, if there is a settled refusal of matrimonial companionship, including intimacy, without justification. The burden of proving a reasonable excuse for withdrawal then shifts to the respondent, who can raise cruelty, desertion by the petitioner, or any other matrimonial fault as a defence to defeat the petition.

What the courts have held

The remedy's constitutional validity was seriously tested in the 1980s. The Andhra Pradesh High Court, in T. Sareetha v. T. Venkata Subbaiah, AIR 1983 AP 356, struck down Section 9 as unconstitutional, holding that a decree compelling a wife to return to a husband's company amounted to state-enforced sexual cohabitation, violating her right to privacy and human dignity. The Delhi High Court, in Harvinder Kaur v. Harmander Singh, AIR 1984 Del 66, took the opposite view and upheld the provision. The conflict was resolved by the Supreme Court in Saroj Rani v. Sudarshan Kumar Chadha, AIR 1984 SC 1562, which upheld Section 9's constitutional validity, disagreeing with the Andhra Pradesh view. The Court reasoned that the object of a restitution decree is to offer an inducement for estranged spouses to reconcile and resume cohabitation, not to force an unwilling spouse into physical intimacy; the decree cannot be enforced by any process that touches the body of the unwilling spouse, and its real teeth lie elsewhere, in what happens if the decree is simply not complied with.

That "elsewhere" is Section 13(1A)(ii) of the Hindu Marriage Act, and it is the single most important thing to understand about this remedy in current practice. Once a decree for restitution of conjugal rights has been passed, either party can seek divorce if there has been no resumption of cohabitation between the parties for a period of one year or more after the decree. In other words, filing a restitution petition and letting the year run without reconciliation is, for many petitioners, less a genuine attempt at reunion and more the fastest lawful route to a contested divorce, since it converts mere separation into a statutory ground. This dual character — nominally a reconciliation remedy, practically a fault-neutral pathway to divorce — explains why restitution petitions remain common even in marriages where neither side seriously expects the other to move back in.

More recent Supreme Court decisions have also used restitution proceedings as a lens on marital conduct rather than a genuine attempt at cohabitation. In Suman Singh v. Sanjay Singh, (2017) 4 SCC 85, the Court observed that a spouse's persistent refusal to fulfil marital obligations without reasonable cause can itself amount to cruelty, relevant in a subsequent divorce petition. Family courts today routinely see restitution petitions filed alongside, or shortly before, cruelty-based divorce petitions, precisely because a documented, unanswered restitution decree becomes evidence of the other spouse's unwillingness to reconcile.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

For a district court practice in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, three practical points are worth flagging to clients before filing or responding to a Section 9 petition. First, a restitution decree is not a warrant — no bailiff will physically deliver an unwilling spouse back to the matrimonial home, and advising a client that the decree guarantees resumption of married life sets up unrealistic expectations. Its real value is procedural: it starts a one-year clock after which non-compliance becomes an independent, no-fault ground for divorce under Section 13(1A)(ii), available to either spouse regardless of who filed the original petition.

Second, restitution petitions interact directly with maintenance litigation. A wife who is respondent to a genuine restitution petition, and who refuses to return without reasonable cause, risks the husband later arguing that her refusal amounts to desertion or unreasonable withdrawal, which can be raised to resist or reduce a maintenance claim under Section 144 BNSS. Conversely, a wife with a real and provable reasonable excuse — cruelty, non-payment of dower or streedhan issues, threats to safety, or the husband's own matrimonial misconduct — should ensure that excuse is squarely pleaded and evidenced in her written statement, because a restitution decree passed against her can later be used to undercut her position in a maintenance or domestic violence proceeding.

Third, counsel should candidly assess with the client, at the outset, which of the two postures described above actually fits their situation. If reconciliation is a real possibility, the petition should be drafted and conducted in a manner that keeps the door open, and counsel should encourage genuine mediation, which family courts in Haryana routinely direct in Section 9 matters before framing issues. If reconciliation is not realistically on the table and the client's real goal is an eventual no-fault divorce, that should be discussed openly, since pursuing a restitution decree purely as a mechanical first step toward Section 13(1A)(ii) divorce, without any real intention of resuming married life, invites the very criticism the Supreme Court in Saroj Rani was alert to — using a reconciliation remedy as a tactical instrument.

Statutory cross-references

Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 has no counterpart procedural offence in the BNS/BNSS, since it is a purely civil remedy adjudicated by the District Court or Family Court under the Hindu Marriage Act and, where applicable, the Family Courts Act, 1984. The parallel maintenance provision, formerly Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, is now Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. For inter-religious or civil marriages, the equivalent restitution remedy is Section 22 of the Special Marriage Act, 1954, and the corresponding divorce-on-non-compliance ground is Section 27(1A) of that Act, mirroring Section 13(1A)(ii) of the Hindu Marriage Act.
