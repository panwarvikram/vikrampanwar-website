Sobaran Singh v. Gordhan Singh: Supreme Court Says a Completed Specific Performance Sale Can't Be Undone by "Settling Equities" Decades Later

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSobaran Singh (Dead) Thr. Lrs. v. Gordhan Singh (Dead) Thr. Lrs.
BenchJustice J.B. Pardiwala & Justice K. Vinod Chandran, Supreme Court of India
DateReported 20 September 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (SC) 962
Key SectionsS. 20, Specific Relief Act, 1963 · S. 100, CPC, 1908

What the case was about

The dispute traced back to a registered agreement to sell dated 16 June 1975, under which roughly five bighas of agricultural land near Agra were to be sold within two years for ₹20,000, with ₹5,000 paid as advance. When the sale did not go through as agreed, the buyer sued for specific performance, and the Trial Court decreed the suit in his favour on 28 February 1979. Pursuant to that decree, the sale was executed through the court itself on 7 June 1979, and the plaintiff took possession of the land that same year — possession he held continuously for more than four decades afterward.

The matter did not end there. The First Appellate Court later reversed the trial court's decree, holding there was insufficient material to show the plaintiff had been ready and willing to perform his part of the bargain — a standard precondition for a specific performance decree. The dispute reached the Allahabad High Court in second appeal, where the High Court found that the Trial Court had in fact been correct on the merits: the agreement was genuine, and the defendant's defence did not hold up. Yet instead of simply restoring the sale that had already been executed and acted upon for decades, the High Court tried a different route. Reasoning that so many years had passed since 1979, it directed the defendant to pay the plaintiff ₹15 lakh with interest, in effect substituting the original ₹20,000 sale consideration and the already-completed conveyance with a much larger cash payment, to "settle equities" between the parties.

What the court held

The Supreme Court, in a bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran, set aside both the First Appellate Court's reversal and the High Court's "equities" solution, and restored the Trial Court's original 1979 decree. The Court's reasoning rested on a straightforward but consequential point: once a decree for specific performance has actually culminated in the execution of a sale deed and delivery of possession, the transaction is complete. It is not open to a court, years or decades later, to unwind that completed sale and replace it with an enhanced monetary award simply because time has elapsed and land values have risen in the interim.

The Bench held that equity, if anything, ran the other way — in the plaintiff's favour, not the defendant's. He had parted with his ₹20,000 more than four and a half decades earlier, had obtained ownership and possession pursuant to a court-executed sale, and had held that possession, undisturbed, ever since. As the Court put it: "Equity, hence, has to be applied in favour of the plaintiff who parted with Rs.20,000/- more than four decades and a half back, and obtained the ownership and possession of the property." Allowing the appeal, the Court restored the Trial Court's decree, confirmed that the plaintiff's possession could not now be disturbed, and directed that the ₹15 lakh the defendant had deposited pursuant to the High Court's judgment be refunded to the defendant, with any interest accrued on it, within one month.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Specific performance suits over agricultural land are a recurring feature of civil litigation before the district courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, and this ruling addresses a scenario that comes up more often than it should: a decree that was passed and fully acted upon years ago, only to be reopened in appeal long after the fact. The judgment draws a firm line against appellate courts treating a completed, decree-driven sale as something that can be casually re-priced or reversed into a cash settlement merely because the litigation dragged on and property values moved. For counsel defending a decreed sale on second appeal, the message is that possession taken pursuant to a court-executed conveyance, held openly for years, is itself powerful equity working in the decree-holder's favour, not a factor that erodes his position over time.

For counsel appearing for a party resisting specific performance, the case is a reminder that arguments framed around "changed circumstances" or "unfairness given how long ago the price was fixed" carry little weight once execution of the decree is complete and possession has changed hands. The proper stage to contest valuation, hardship or delay is before the decree is executed — not afterward, dressed up as an appeal to equity. The ruling is also a useful citation wherever a second appeal under Section 100 CPC attempts, in substance, to reopen concluded relief rather than confine itself to a genuine substantial question of law.

Statutory cross-references

The suit was governed by Section 20 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, as it stood before the 2018 amendment, which then vested courts with discretion — guided by settled principles — over whether to grant specific performance at all. The 2018 amendment has since narrowed that discretion considerably, making specific performance the rule rather than the exception in most contracts for the transfer of immovable property, subject to the specific exceptions carved out in the amended Sections 10 and 14. That change does not affect this case, which concerned a decree already passed and executed in 1979, but it is directly relevant to any fresh specific performance suit filed in Haryana's district courts today. The second appeal itself proceeded under Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, which confines a High Court's jurisdiction in second appeal to a substantial question of law, and does not entitle it to re-appreciate settled findings of fact or to fashion equitable relief the trial court itself did not grant.
