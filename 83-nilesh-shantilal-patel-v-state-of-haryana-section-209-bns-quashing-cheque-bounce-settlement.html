Nilesh Shantilal Patel v. State of Haryana: P&H High Court Quashes a Section 209 BNS Absconder FIR Once the Underlying Cheque-Bounce Case Was Settled

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseNilesh Shantilal Patel & Anr. v. State of Haryana & Anr.
BenchJustice Manisha Batra, Punjab & Haryana High Court
DateDecided 31 August 2026
Citation2026 NCPHHC 121132 : 2026 Law Finder (P&H) 26138 · CRM-M-38448-2026 & connected petitions (O&M)
Key SectionsS. 209, BNS, 2023 (corresponding to S. 174A, IPC) · S. 195, CrPC · S. 138, NI Act, 1881 · S. 528, BNSS, 2023

What the case was about

Adgel Corporation had filed five cheque-bounce complaints under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 against the petitioners before the Judicial Magistrate First Class, Faridabad, bearing NACT Nos. 212/2019, 315/2019, 316/2019, 455/2019 and 456/2019. The petitioners did not appear despite proclamation proceedings, and on 22 May 2025 the Magistrate declared them proclaimed persons and directed the Station House Officer to take action for their failure to appear. Acting on that direction, the police registered FIR No. 157 dated 6 May 2026 at Police Station Faridabad Kotwali for the offence of failing to appear in response to a proclamation.

Before the police could take the matter further, the underlying dispute resolved itself the way most Section 138 cases eventually do: the parties settled. Under a Settlement Deed dated 8 May 2026, Adgel Corporation agreed to withdraw all five complaints after receiving the disputed amount, and the Magistrate dismissed all five as withdrawn on 8 June 2026. That left the petitioners with a live FIR for non-appearance attached to complaints that no longer existed. They moved the Punjab and Haryana High Court under Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 — the renumbered successor to Section 482 CrPC — to quash both the 22 May 2025 proclamation order and the FIR itself.

The State opposed the petitions, arguing there was no illegality either in the proclamation order or in the FIR that followed from it. Counsel for the complainant, however, filed appearance and confirmed the settlement had genuinely taken place, leaving the matter for the Court to decide purely on the legal question of whether an absconder-type FIR can survive the compromise of the case it was born out of.

What the court held

Justice Manisha Batra allowed all six connected petitions and quashed both the proclamation order and FIR No. 157, along with all consequential proceedings, resting the decision on three independent grounds.

First, and most centrally, the Court held that once the principal Section 138 complaints stood withdrawn on compromise, continuing the FIR for non-appearance served no purpose and amounted to abuse of the process of law. The judgment traced this through a consistent line of authority — Mohammad Hanif Attari v. State of Haryana, Ashok Madaan v. State of Haryana, Smriti v. State of Haryana, and Narinder Pal Singh v. State of Punjab — all holding that where the main Section 138 complaint has been settled, a parallel non-appearance FIR loses its reason for existing. It also invoked the Supreme Court's ruling in Daljit Singh v. State of Haryana, which had clarified that the non-appearance offence is technically an independent, substantive offence capable of surviving even after the underlying proclamation is extinguished — yet the Supreme Court itself quashed the FIR on the facts before it once the originating Section 138 complaint was settled. Justice Batra read that as confirming the principle rather than undercutting it: independence in theory does not save an FIR that, on the facts, no longer serves the purpose it was registered for, which was simply to secure the accused's presence in proceedings that have since ceased to exist.

Second, the Court found the FIR procedurally vitiated at birth. Relying on a coordinate Bench's detailed examination in Pardeep Kumar v. State of Punjab, the judgment holds that cognizance of the non-appearance offence cannot be taken except on a written complaint by the concerned public servant, because the offence falls within Section 195(1)(a)(i) of the CrPC, which covers offences under Sections 172 to 188 of the IPC — a bar that survives, in substance, under the new code. Here, the FIR had been registered merely because the trial court orally directed the SHO to act after declaring the petitioners proclaimed persons; no formal complaint was ever filed. That, the Court held, is not how the criminal law machinery is supposed to be set in motion for this class of offence, and the omission by itself vitiated the FIR.

Third, the Court noted that the underlying proclamation proceedings had not been conducted strictly as required under Section 82 CrPC (now Section 84 BNSS), adding a further layer of infirmity to the order that had declared the petitioners absconders in the first place.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Cheque-bounce litigation is a staple of practice in the district courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, and it is common for an accused — often out of station, unaware of the summons, or simply negligent about tracking a NACT complaint — to be proclaimed an absconder and to find a fresh, standalone FIR registered against them for non-appearance. Once the main cheque case settles, as a large proportion do given how strongly Section 147 of the NI Act favours compromise, clients are often surprised to learn that a second criminal proceeding survives independently. This judgment gives a clean, current precedent for closing that loop through a single Section 528 BNSS petition rather than leaving a client to fight two fronts.

The Section 195 CrPC point is worth flagging separately, because it is a procedural defect that recurs quietly across many such FIRs. Magistrates frequently direct the SHO to "register a case" against a proclaimed person without themselves filing the written complaint the law actually requires. Defence counsel should specifically check, in any non-appearance FIR, whether it originated from a bare oral or written direction to the police rather than a proper complaint — because if it did, this judgment supplies a second, independent ground for quashing even where a settlement angle is unavailable.

Statutory cross-references

The offence of failing to appear despite a proclamation, earlier Section 174A of the Indian Penal Code, now corresponds to Section 209 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. Readers should note that the reported order text itself is internally inconsistent on this point — it correctly identifies the FIR as registered "under Section 209 of BNS, 2023 (which corresponds to Section 174A IPC)" while narrating the facts, but the discussion in the later paragraphs repeatedly refers to "Section 290 BNS." Section 290 of the BNS in fact deals with negligent conduct in building construction and has no bearing on this case; this appears to be a numbering slip in the judgment or its digitisation, and this article treats Section 209 BNS, 2023 as the correct and operative provision throughout, consistent with the facts recorded in the order and with the settled IPC-to-BNS correspondence table. Section 82 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (proclamation of a person absconding) corresponds to Section 84 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. Section 195 CrPC (bar on taking cognizance of certain offences without a complaint by a public servant) corresponds to Section 215 BNSS, 2023. Section 482 CrPC (inherent powers of the High Court) corresponds to Section 528 BNSS, 2023, under which the present petitions were filed.
