P & Anr. v. State of West Bengal (2026 INSC 1087): Supreme Court Quashes a Section 498A Case Against a Husband’s Parents, Holding Omnibus Allegations With No Overt Act Cannot Sustain a Criminal Trial

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — P & Anr. v. State of West Bengal & Anr.
BenchJustice N. Kotiswar Singh and Justice N.V. Anjaria
Date5 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1087
Key SectionsSections 498A, 406, 506 IPC; Sections 3 and 4, Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961; Section 482 CrPC

What the case was about

The complainant married the appellants’ son on 1 February 2021 and lived with his parents after he was transferred to Siliguri. A daughter was born on 17 July 2023. After she left the matrimonial home she alleged a dowry demand (including a four-wheeler), taunts after the birth of a girl child, and an incident of 11 November 2023 in which she says her husband and her father-in-law made her sign a paper acknowledging that her streedhan had been returned.

An FIR was registered on 21 November 2023 at the Siliguri Women Police Station and a chargesheet followed a month later under Sections 498A, 406 and 506 IPC and Sections 3 and 4 of the Dowry Prohibition Act. The parents-in-law asked the Calcutta High Court (Jalpaiguri Circuit Bench) to quash the case under Section 482 CrPC. On 12 November 2024 the High Court quashed only the Section 323 IPC charge and left the rest to go to trial. The in-laws then came to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

A bench of Justices Kotiswar Singh and Anjaria allowed the appeal and quashed all proceedings against the two appellants. The Court began from a simple principle: criminal law should not be stretched to cover every member of a husband’s family without a clear factual basis. Its reading of the record was that neither the complaint nor the investigation attributed any specific or overt act to either appellant, and no independent material had been gathered against them.

Charge by charge, the bench found the following. On Section 498A, there was nothing to show harassment of the kind that drives a woman to injury or is aimed at forcing a property demand. Under the Dowry Prohibition Act, the allegations lacked particulars: no dates, no occasions, no independent witness to any demand. On Section 406, the property said to have been entrusted was never specified. On Section 506, the only support for the alleged threat was the complainant’s own account and the statements of her parents. The oral allegations were not backed by documents or witnesses.

The Court drew on State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal, the standard reference for the categories in which a court may quash a prosecution, and on its recent decisions in Geddam Jhansi v. State of Telangana (2025) and Arti Mehta v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2026), both of which warn against general and omnibus allegations in matrimonial complaints. It added an important safeguard: the husband was not a party to the appeal, so the observations do not affect the case on its merits against him, nor any civil remedy the complainant may have.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Most 498A and dowry complaints that reach the district courts in Bhiwani, Charkhi Dadri and neighbouring districts name the husband, his parents, and often siblings and their spouses. This judgment gives defence counsel a clean, recent authority to argue that a bare mention of a family member’s name, with no date, place or act attached, is not enough to continue a trial. It is particularly useful at the stage of discharge or framing of charge, where the question is whether the material discloses a prima facie case against each accused individually, and in quashing petitions before the High Court.

For counsel acting for complainants, the lesson is about drafting. A complaint or statement that sets out who demanded what, when, in whose presence, and what was said or done, stands on much firmer ground than one that repeats the same allegation against every in-law. Where possible, corroborate with messages, call records, bank entries, a list of articles given at marriage, or statements of neutral witnesses. If streedhan is the grievance, identify the articles and the person to whom they were entrusted.

Equally, this is not a licence to treat every in-law as immune. The ruling turns on its own record, and the Court left the husband’s trial untouched. Where specific acts are alleged against a parent or relative and are supported by material, courts will still let the case proceed. In practice, advise both sides early: a precise complaint with supporting material is the best protection against both a failed prosecution and a misuse allegation.

Statutory cross-references

Section 498A IPC (cruelty by husband or relatives) corresponds to Section 85 BNS, with the explanation defining cruelty in Section 86 BNS. Section 406 IPC (criminal breach of trust) corresponds to Section 316(2) BNS, and Section 506 IPC (criminal intimidation) to Section 351(2) and (3) BNS. Section 482 CrPC, under which quashing was sought, is now Section 528 BNSS, 2023. Sections 3 and 4 of the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 (giving, taking and demanding dowry) continue to apply. The offences in this case predate the new codes, so the IPC and CrPC provisions governed.
