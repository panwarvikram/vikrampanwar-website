Manoj @ Munna v. State of Chhattisgarh: Supreme Court Says "Last Seen Together" Is Weak Evidence That Cannot, By Itself, Sustain a Murder Conviction

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtSupreme Court of India
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & Prashant Kumar Mishra
Date18 December 2025
Citation2025 INSC 1466 · 2026 AIR SC 241 · 2025 SCC OnLine SC 2858 · 2026(1) RCR (Criminal) 323
Key ProvisionsSs. 302 & 201, IPC · S. 106, Indian Evidence Act, 1872

What the case was about

On 6 June 2004, in Chhattisgarh, tractor driver Yuvraj Singh Patle was last seen alive in the company of the appellant, Manoj @ Munna, and a co-accused, Bhagwandas. According to two witnesses, Bedram and Chamru Singh, the deceased had been asked by the accused to take the tractor to Salhevara; there, the accused left Chamru Singh behind at the house of one Mangal Patle, saying they were going to Banjari with the deceased to collect cable wire and would return shortly. Neither the deceased, the accused, nor the tractor returned that night or the next day. The following day, the deceased's badly burnt and decomposed body was recovered, bearing ligature marks on the neck, lacerations, and extensive burn injuries; the post-mortem doctor certified the death as homicidal, caused by shock and burn injury.

Six persons, including the appellant, were charged with murder, dacoity with murder, criminal conspiracy, and causing disappearance of evidence. The prosecution's theory was that the appellant needed money to redeem his impounded jeep, and killed the driver to loot the tractor. Of the six accused, the Trial Court acquitted five and convicted only Manoj, sentencing him to life imprisonment under Section 302 IPC plus a further term under Section 201 IPC (causing disappearance of evidence) for allegedly burning the body. The Chhattisgarh High Court affirmed this conviction in 2011, relying on the last-seen-together evidence and on the appellant's failure to explain how and when he parted ways with the deceased. The appeal reached the Supreme Court in 2013 and was finally decided in December 2025 — over two decades after the incident.

What the court held

The Supreme Court allowed the appeal and acquitted Manoj, holding that the case rested purely on circumstantial evidence — motive and last seen together — with no direct or ocular evidence of the crime. Reaffirming the five "golden principles" for circumstantial evidence cases laid down in the classic authority Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra, (1984) 4 SCC 116, the Court reiterated that circumstances relied upon must be fully established, must point only to guilt (not merely be compatible with it), must be conclusive in nature, must exclude every other reasonable hypothesis, and must form a complete, unbroken chain leaving no room for any conclusion consistent with innocence.

Applying these principles, the Court found the prosecution's case fell at two of its own load-bearing pillars. On motive, the theory that the appellant needed money to recover his impounded jeep was left unproved — there was no evidence he ever attempted to sell the looted tractor, which was in fact recovered from an entirely different location a full month after the incident, undermining the theft-for-money narrative. On the last-seen theory itself, while the Court accepted that the testimony of Bedram and Chamru Singh did establish that the deceased was last seen alive in the appellant's company on the evening of 6 June 2004, it held — tracing a consistent line of its own precedent including Rambraksh v. State of Chhattisgarh, (2016) 12 SCC 251, Krishnan @ Ramasamy v. State of Tamil Nadu, (2014) 12 SCC 279, Arjun Marik v. State of Bihar, 1994 Supp (2) SCC 372, Kanhaiya Lal v. State of Rajasthan, (2014) 4 SCC 715, and its own very recent decision in Padman Bibhar v. State of Odisha, 2025 INSC 751 — that last seen together is, by itself, a weak piece of evidence. It raises suspicion, even a strong one, but cannot found a conviction unless corroborated by other evidence completing the chain of circumstances.

The Court then addressed the High Court's reliance on the appellant's silence about when and how he parted from the deceased, undertaking a careful analysis of Section 106 of the Evidence Act. It held that Section 106 — which places the burden of proving facts especially within a person's knowledge on that person — is an exception to the general rule under Section 101 that the prosecution bears the burden of proof, and does not dilute or displace that primary burden. An adverse inference from an accused's silence can only be drawn as an additional link once the prosecution has already established a reasonable inference of guilt through other evidence; it cannot be the load-bearing beam that fills a gap the prosecution's own evidence leaves open. Since here the only real circumstance — last seen together — remained uncorroborated, there was no foundation for the Court to even reach the stage of drawing an adverse inference under Section 106.

On this basis, the Court set aside both the Trial Court's and the High Court's judgments, holding that circumstantial evidence which merely raises a doubt of guilt, without being so conclusive as to exclude every other hypothesis, entitles the accused to the benefit of that doubt. Manoj was acquitted and, being on bail, his bail bonds were discharged — bringing to a close a case that had spanned more than 21 years from the alleged incident to final acquittal.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Last-seen-together cases are a recurring category in Sessions trials across Haryana, especially in rural homicide cases where there are no independent eyewitnesses to the actual killing and the prosecution leans on whoever was seen with the deceased shortly before death. This judgment is valuable for defence counsel because it collects, in one place, the entire modern lineage of Supreme Court authority cautioning against convictions built on last-seen evidence alone, and it draws a sharp, usable line on Section 106: the provision supplements a prosecution case that has already cleared the threshold of a reasonable inference of guilt, it does not manufacture that inference out of an accused's silence. Defence counsel facing a last-seen prosecution should therefore always ask two questions in sequence — has the prosecution's independent evidence (motive, recovery, forensic proof, time-gap analysis) already established a reasonable inference of guilt; and only if so, does the accused's failure to explain become a further, corroborating link, rather than the primary proof itself.

The case is also a reminder that alleged motive evidence must be followed through to its logical conclusion — here, the "theft for money" motive collapsed because the prosecution never proved any attempt to monetise the stolen property, and the recovered tractor's late discovery, from an unconnected location, actively cut against the theory rather than supporting it. Advocates prosecuting or defending such cases should scrutinise whether every limb of an alleged motive is actually followed up with corroborating evidence, rather than left as an assumed inference from circumstances.

Statutory cross-references

Sections 302 and 201 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860, under which the trial and appeals in this case proceeded (the offence predating the 2023 recodification), now correspond to Sections 103 and 238 respectively of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. Section 106 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 is carried forward in substantially identical terms as Section 109 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. The doctrinal framework discussed in this judgment — the golden rules of circumstantial evidence and the limited scope of adverse inference from an accused's silence — applies equally to prosecutions and trials now conducted under the new codes.
