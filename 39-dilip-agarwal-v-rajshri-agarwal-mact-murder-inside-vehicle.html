Dilip Agarwal v. Rajshri Agarwal: Supreme Court Says There Is No Motor Accident Compensation Where a Death Inside a Vehicle Is Not Causally Linked to Its Use

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseDilip Agarwal v. Rajshri Agarwal & Ors.
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & Augustine George Masih
Date22 July 2026
Citation2026 INSC 736 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 709
Key SectionsSs. 165, 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

On 29 November 2009, Anand got into a car being driven by his friend Dilip. Three days later, on 2 December 2009, Anand's body was found near a village in Raigarh district, Chhattisgarh. His wife Rajshri lodged an FIR, and the investigation led to Dilip and two others being chargesheeted for kidnapping, murder and criminal conspiracy under Sections 365, 302, 201 and 120B of the Indian Penal Code, 1860. A trial court convicted Dilip in 2012, but the High Court acquitted him in 2015, holding that the prosecution had failed to prove the "last seen together" theory on which its case rested.

While the criminal case was still winding through the courts, Rajshri and her children filed a separate claim petition before the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal at Raigarh under Section 166 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, seeking roughly Rs. 26 lakh in compensation. Their case was that Anand had been killed inside Dilip's car, and that the vehicle was therefore "used" in a manner that attracted liability under the Act. The Tribunal accepted this, holding the death covered under Sections 165 and 166 of the Act and awarding about Rs. 5.64 lakh with interest; the Chhattisgarh High Court agreed and enhanced the award to roughly Rs. 8.6 lakh. Dilip appealed to the Supreme Court, framing a narrow but important question: does a death "arise out of" the use of a motor vehicle merely because a vehicle was somewhere in the chain of events that led to it?

What the court held

A bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and Augustine George Masih answered no. Writing for the Court, Justice Karol held that the Tribunal and High Court had simply assumed that Anand's injuries were inflicted while he was inside the car, without any evidentiary basis for that assumption. There was no forensic material recovered from the vehicle — no blood, skin cells or hair — and no suggestion that the car had been involved in any collision. All that had been established was that Anand died from injuries caused by hard and blunt objects; where, exactly, those injuries were inflicted was never shown.

The Court accepted that claimants in a motor accident case need not prove the precise manner in which injuries were sustained, but held that this lighter evidentiary standard cannot substitute for a causal link altogether. Distinguishing the case from Rita Devi v. New India Assurance Co. Ltd., (2000) 5 SCC 113 — where a car theft led to the driver's murder, and the death was held to have arisen "in relation to" the vehicle because the primary felonious intent (theft) was itself connected to the vehicle — the Court found no such link here. The primary object of the alleged crime was to kill Anand, not to use, steal or otherwise engage the vehicle; the car's presence in the narrative did not, by itself, satisfy the phrase "arising out of the use of a motor vehicle" in Section 166. The concurrent findings of the Tribunal and High Court were set aside and the appeal allowed, though the Court directed that any compensation already paid would not be recovered from the claimants.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

MACT practice in Haryana runs on a steady diet of claim petitions where the facts are not always a clean, witnessed road accident — bodies found near vehicles, disputed sequences of events, and claimants understandably reaching for whatever provision offers a route to compensation. This judgment draws a firm line: Section 166 is not a general compensation scheme for any death in which a vehicle happens to appear; it requires a demonstrable causal or proximate relationship between the vehicle's use and the death. Advocates representing claimants in factually ambiguous cases — where the cause of death is disputed, or where a criminal case running parallel to the MACT claim has resulted in acquittal for want of proof — should treat this ruling as a caution against relying on inference alone; some evidentiary thread connecting the vehicle itself (forensic material, a collision, an established felonious use of the vehicle as in Rita Devi) will now be expected.

The judgment is equally useful for advocates defending owners and insurers, who can now cite Dilip Agarwal directly to resist claims built on circumstantial narratives that treat "a vehicle was involved somewhere" as sufficient. It also reinforces something practitioners on both sides already know from handling parallel criminal and MACT proceedings out of the same incident: a criminal acquittal does not automatically defeat a civil compensation claim, since the standards of proof differ, but the underlying facts pleaded in a MACT petition still have to establish, on a preponderance of probabilities, an actual link between the vehicle and the death — not merely gesture at one.

Statutory cross-references

The judgment turns on Sections 165, 166 and 168 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, which remain unchanged in their numbering and have no BNS/BNSS equivalent since the Act sits outside the criminal codes; the underlying criminal charges in the case — then Sections 302, 365, 201 and 120B of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 — would today be prosecuted under Sections 103, 137, 238 and 61 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 respectively, for any comparable case arising after 1 July 2024.
