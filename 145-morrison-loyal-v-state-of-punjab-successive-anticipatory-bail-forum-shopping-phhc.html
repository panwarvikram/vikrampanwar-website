Morrison Loyal v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Says a Second Anticipatory Bail Plea Without a Substantial Change in Circumstances Is Forum Shopping

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab and Haryana High Court — Morrison Loyal @ Morison @ Nishu v. State of Punjab
BenchJustice Sumeet Goel
DateReported 9 October 2026 (first petition dismissed 1 October 2026)
CitationCRM-M-57958-2026 (printed citation not yet available)
Key SectionsS. 482 BNSS · S. 356(2) BNS · S. 66E, 67A IT Act

What the case was about

Morrison Loyal alias Nishu approached the Punjab and Haryana High Court under Section 482 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 for anticipatory bail. The FIR, registered after a DSP-level inquiry, alleged that he circulated a woman’s private photographs and videos on social media and had threatened her with it. The sections invoked were Section 356(2) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (defamation) and Sections 66E and 67A of the Information Technology Act. The complainant said her phone had been accessed while the petitioner’s wife was staying at her house during a marital dispute.

This was his second attempt. His first anticipatory bail petition had been dismissed on merits on 1 October 2026. In the new petition he said the case was a product of neighbourhood rivalry, earlier inquiries had found nothing, the number used for circulation was not his, and, as a changed circumstance, that he had since handed over the phone password and lock pattern, which he could not rebut when the Investigating Officer claimed otherwise earlier.

What the High Court held

Justice Sumeet Goel dismissed the petition. Following the High Court’s earlier decision in Bhisham Singh v. State of Haryana (2024), the Court accepted the legal position that a second anticipatory bail plea is not barred as such, whether the first was withdrawn or dismissed on merits. But it is maintainable only if the applicant shows a substantial change in circumstances, and a cosmetic one will not do.

Measured against that test, the petitioner failed. Not being able to answer the IO’s version at the first hearing is not a new circumstance with bearing on the merits; the grounds now urged were all available earlier. Entertaining them would let a litigant keep returning to the same court for the same relief. The Court called the filing forum shopping: where a party is unhappy with an order, the remedy is to go up the judicial hierarchy, not to try the same court again hoping for a different outcome. It also stressed the finality of judicial orders. Separately, it found custodial interrogation necessary given the nature of the allegations. The Court made clear that its observations are not an opinion on the merits of the case.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Successive bail petitions are a daily feature of criminal practice, and the High Court has been tightening its approach lately, including refusing to treat length of custody alone as a substantial change. This order extends the same discipline to anticipatory bail. Before filing a second petition, counsel should be able to point to a concrete development after the first order: a fresh fact, a change in the investigation, a new document, a co-accused’s bail or a change in law. Re-arguing points that were open earlier, or explaining away what was not said earlier, will invite the forum-shopping label and possibly costs.

The ruling also guides how to prepare the first petition. If the first plea is going to be argued on merits, all available grounds, including cooperation with the investigation and willingness to hand over devices, should be placed before the Court at that time. Where a first plea fails, the better course may be a challenge before the Supreme Court rather than a fresh petition. In district courts, the same logic is used by prosecutors to oppose second applications before the Sessions Court, so the standard is worth keeping in mind there too. For technology-related offences, as here, offering passwords and devices at the outset matters.

Statutory cross-references

Section 482 BNSS (anticipatory bail) corresponds to Section 438 of the old CrPC, and regular bail provisions in Sections 480 and 483 BNSS correspond to Sections 437 and 439 CrPC. Defamation under Section 356(2) BNS corresponds to Section 500 IPC. Sections 66E (violation of privacy) and 67A (publishing sexually explicit material) of the Information Technology Act, 2000 are unchanged by the new codes.
