Dr. Shyam Bihari v. State of Haryana: P&H High Court Says a Doctor Cannot Be Prosecuted Merely Because His Name Stayed on a Clinic's Registration Panel

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseDr. Shyam Bihari v. State of Haryana · Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Yashvir Singh Rathor
DateDecided 3 July 2026 (reported 14 July 2026)
CitationCRR-3912-2018 (O&M)
Key SectionsPre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act, 1994; Rule 3(3)(3), PCPNDT Rules

What the case was about

The case travels back to July 2016, when a joint team of health department officials raided the Janta Diagnostic Centre in Hansi, Haryana, acting on a tip-off about illegal prenatal sex determination. A decoy operation was run, and marked currency was recovered from the clinic's owner. An FIR followed, alleging violations of the PCPNDT Act along with provisions of the Indian Penal Code.

Dr. Shyam Bihari, a radiologist, was roped in as an accused during investigation — even though his name did not appear in the original FIR or in the spot proceedings at the time of the raid. The sole basis for implicating him was that his name still figured on the diagnostic centre's registration panel, even though the prosecution's own record showed he had stopped working there years earlier and was, on the date of the raid, employed as a consultant radiologist at a different centre in Gurugram.

The trial court discharged him in April 2017, finding no material connecting him to the offence. But the Additional Sessions Judge, Hisar, set aside that discharge in October 2018, reasoning that since his name was still on the registration panel, he could not escape the charge. Dr. Bihari challenged that revisional order before the High Court.

What the court held

Justice Yashvir Singh Rathor went through the investigation record and found it actually supported the doctor's case rather than the prosecution's. Attendance records and registration documents confirmed he was working at a Gurugram centre on the date of the Hansi raid. The seized ultrasound report and the mandatory Form ‘F’ identified a different radiologist as the person associated with the Hansi clinic on the relevant date, and witness statements confirmed Dr. Bihari had left that clinic years before.

On the legal question, the Court read Rule 3(3)(3) of the PCPNDT Rules — which restricts a radiologist's registration to clinics within the same area — to mean that the restriction applies only within a single district. Since Dr. Bihari's registrations were in different districts, the Court found no statutory violation on that count, also noting that the operation of this rule had in any case been stayed by various High Courts.

The judgment is emphatic that administrative untidiness cannot be dressed up as a crime. In the Court's words, a prima facie case requires "some material of a substantive nature linking the accused to the offence," and "administrative omissions or delayed updates in registration records cannot, by themselves, amount to a criminal offence." A clinic's failure to formally remove a doctor's name from its panel after he has left is a lapse attributable to the clinic and the licensing authority — not a criminal act by the doctor.

The High Court accordingly allowed the revision petition, set aside the Additional Sessions Judge's order of 29 October 2018, and restored the trial court's original discharge order dated 12 April 2017.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

PCPNDT prosecutions are common across Haryana's district courts given the state's historically skewed sex ratio and the resulting regulatory vigilance, and defence counsel repeatedly encounter cases where a doctor is implicated on the thinnest of institutional links — a name on a panel, a signature on an old registration form, an association that ended years before the alleged offence. This judgment gives defence lawyers a clean, citable authority for the proposition that criminal liability under a beneficial, strict-liability-leaning statute like the PCPNDT Act still requires actual, substantive material connecting the individual accused to the specific offence charged, not mere documentary residue.

It is also a useful precedent at the discharge stage generally. Sessions Courts exercising revisional jurisdiction sometimes reverse a considered discharge order on the strength of a single administrative fact, without asking whether that fact establishes involvement in the offence. This judgment reinforces that a revisional court must test whether the material actually shows participation, physical presence, or conspiracy — and that discharge orders resting on a genuine absence of such material should not be casually overturned. For practitioners defending doctors, paramedical staff, or clinic employees in PCPNDT matters in Hisar, Bhiwani, Charkhi Dadri and elsewhere in the state, the ruling is a template for the kind of documentary defence — attendance records, alternate registration proof, Form F entries — that can secure discharge even after an FIR has been registered.

Statutory cross-references

The PCPNDT Act, 1994 remains a standalone special statute and is unaffected by the transition from the Indian Penal Code to the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023 or from the Code of Criminal Procedure to the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023 — its offences and penalties under Sections 23 and 26 to 28 continue unchanged. Procedurally, however, complaints, cognizance, and discharge applications in pending and fresh PCPNDT prosecutions in Haryana's district courts now follow the BNSS framework rather than the CrPC: for instance, the discharge process itself (equivalent to the old Section 227 CrPC) is now governed by Section 251, BNSS, and revisional jurisdiction against such orders by Sections 438 to 442, BNSS (formerly Sections 397 to 401, CrPC).
