Union of India v. Dharmender Singh: P&H High Court Says Breaking a Railway Level-Crossing Gate Needs Intent, Not Just Rash Driving

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court, Chandigarh
BenchJustice Sukhvinder Kaur
Date22 December 2025
Citation2026(1) RCR (Criminal) 472 · 2025 NCPHHC 176900 · CRM-A-462 of 2025 (O&M)
Key SectionsS. 160(2), Railways Act, 1989 · Ss. 25 & 26, Indian Evidence Act, 1872

What the case was about

On 29 May 2021, a gateman on duty at Gate No. 97-C was in the process of closing the level-crossing boom to allow a goods train to pass when a vehicle, allegedly driven by respondent Dharmender Singh, struck the upside gate boom at high speed and broke it. A criminal case was registered the same day under Section 160(2) of the Railways Act, 1989, and the respondent was arrested. The Railway Protection Force recorded what the prosecution described as a confessional statement from him, and the matter went to trial before the Special Railway Magistrate, Ambala Cantt.

The prosecution examined five witnesses, including the gateman, a mechanic who prepared a mechanical examination report on the vehicle, an inquiry officer, and a Senior Section Engineer who dealt with the damage report. No defence evidence was led. Despite this, the Trial Court acquitted the respondent by judgment dated 25 July 2023, holding that the prosecution had not proved its case beyond reasonable doubt. The Union of India, represented by the Railways, appealed to the High Court — but only after a delay of 347 days, which the Court condoned at the threshold before hearing the appeal on merits.

Before the High Court, the Railways' counsel argued that the Trial Court had ignored consistent, unshaken witness testimony, that the respondent's own confessional statement admitted his role in the incident, and that intention was, in any event, immaterial to the offence — rash and negligent driving that damaged the gate was enough to make out the case.

What the court held

The High Court disagreed, and its reasoning turned on four distinct planks — each independently sufficient to sustain the acquittal.

First, and most significantly, on the mental element required by Section 160(2). The Court held, following an earlier Co-ordinate Bench decision in Union of India v. State of Punjab, 2007(4) RCR (Criminal) 102, that Section 160(2) — which punishes breaking a gate, chain, or barrier at a level crossing closed to road traffic — is meant to punish intentional misconduct, not rash or negligent conduct. The Court reasoned by comparison with Section 160(1), which punishes unauthorised opening of a gate and is plainly aimed at deliberate conduct; reading the two sub-sections together, the whole of Section 160 was held to exclude rashness or negligence from its scope. On the facts, the gateman's own testimony showed the vehicle came at high speed and rammed into the boom — at most establishing rashness, not a prior intention to damage railway property. Even the respondent's own statement recorded that he tried to brake but could not stop in time given the speed, which if anything corroborated an accident rather than a deliberate act.

Second, on the confessional statement. The Court accepted, relying on the Supreme Court's ruling in Bal Kishan A. Devi Dayal v. State of Maharashtra, 1981 AIR (SC) 379, that a statement recorded by RPF officials is not automatically excluded by Sections 25 and 26 of the Evidence Act (which bar confessions to police officers) since RPF personnel are not "police officers" for this purpose. But admissibility was not the end of the inquiry — due execution and reliability still had to be established. The Trial Court had referred to the RPF's own Crime Manual, which requires a confessional statement to be recorded, where possible, in the accused's own handwriting and attested by two independent witnesses. Here, the statement was neither in the respondent's handwriting nor attested by two independent witnesses, and his signature was in Punjabi rather than the language the rest of the document was recorded in — unexplained irregularities that the Court held rendered the statement unreliable as a basis for conviction.

Third, on whether the gate was actually "closed to road traffic" at the relevant moment — an express statutory ingredient of Section 160(2). The gateman's own account was that the incident occurred while he was in the process of closing the gate for the train's passage, meaning the gate was not yet fully closed to road traffic when the collision occurred. This alone meant the ingredients of the offence were not complete.

Fourth, on proof of damage and repair. The mechanical examination report noted rubbing marks on the vehicle's bumper, but the mechanic who prepared it admitted he never visited the accident spot and could not say how or when those marks were formed. Separately, none of the railway witnesses could produce a movement certificate (showing who attended to repair the gate) or a damage certificate, meaning even the fact and extent of damage — let alone its cause — remained unproved.

On this combination of findings, the High Court held there was no infirmity, perversity, or illegality in the Trial Court's acquittal, and dismissed the Railways' appeal.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Level-crossing incidents are a routine feature of prosecutions in Haryana's railway magistracies, given the state's dense rail network and heavy road traffic at crossings in districts like Ambala, Kurukshetra, and Yamunanagar. This judgment gives defence counsel a clear, two-tier line of attack in Section 160(2) prosecutions: first, contest the mental element by pointing to any evidence of speed, inattention, or accidental collision rather than deliberate targeting of the gate; second, independently attack the RPF's confessional statement on procedural compliance grounds, since RPF's own Crime Manual sets a documented, checkable standard (handwriting of the accused, attestation by two independent witnesses) that investigating officers frequently fail to meet in practice.

For prosecutors and Railway panel counsel, the case is a reminder that establishing "closed to road traffic" as a factual matter — not merely alleging it — is indispensable, and that damage to railway property must be proved through proper documentation (movement and damage certificates) rather than assumed from the fact of a collision. The judgment also usefully reaffirms that admissibility of a confession (not hit by Sections 25/26 because RPF officials are not police) is analytically distinct from its evidentiary reliability, which depends on compliance with the force's own internal safeguards — a distinction advocates should keep sharp when framing arguments around confessional statements recorded by RPF, GRP, or similar quasi-police forces.

Statutory cross-references

Section 160 of the Railways Act, 1989 remains unaffected by the 2023 criminal law recodification and continues to be tried, as here, before Special Railway Magistrates under the ordinary procedure — now the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023 in place of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, as reflected in the respondent's own application in this case seeking exemption under Section 528 BNSS (the successor provision permitting exemption from filing a certified copy, corresponding to the erstwhile Section 482 CrPC's residuary framework read with procedural exemption rules). The bar on police confessions under Sections 25 and 26 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 now finds its corresponding provision in Sections 23 and 24 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, carrying forward the same protection in substantially similar terms.
