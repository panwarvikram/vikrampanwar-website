Senior Intelligence Officer v. Mohammed Kamran: Supreme Court Says Chasing Arrest and Bail Remedies in Parallel Without Full Disclosure Is Abuse of Process (2026 INSC 1140)

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India (Criminal Appeal arising out of SLP (Crl) No. 7504 of 2026)
BenchJustice Dipankar Datta & Justice Sheel Nagu
Date16 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1140
Key SectionsCGST Act, 2017: Sections 16(2)(b), 132(1), 135; bail jurisdiction of the High Court (Section 439 CrPC / Section 483 BNSS)

What the case was about

A Senior Intelligence Officer of the GST intelligence directorate filed a complaint against Mohammed Kamran, proprietor of M/s MK Traders, alleging invoices issued without any supply of goods, fraudulent input tax credit and false information, with an alleged tax loss of about ₹43.08 crore. He was arrested on 16 September 2025. The trial court and the Sessions Court both declined bail.

On 22 December 2025 he also filed a writ petition in the Karnataka High Court, where interim release was refused. Four days later, on 26 December 2025, during the High Court’s winter vacation, a Single Judge granted him bail in a criminal petition. The department said its counsel could not appear because his adjournment request was refused. It appealed to the Supreme Court, arguing that the accused had hidden the failed remedies from the Single Judge.

What the Court held

The Bench found a lack of uberrima fides, the good faith that discretionary relief like bail demands. The accused had run parallel remedies — the Sessions Court bail route and a writ petition — and had not told the High Court that bail had been refused below and ad-interim relief denied in the writ. The Court called this suppression of facts and held that pursuing parallel remedies without adequate disclosure “clearly amounts to abuse of the process of law,” adding that forum shopping was evident.

It also disapproved of how the matter reached the Single Judge. The petition was got listed through a memo moved on 26 December 2025, in vacation, without proper notice to the department, and the Court described this as an attempt to steal a march by misleading the court. It observed that the petition should ordinarily have been listed after the court reopened.

Importantly, the Court did not cancel bail outright. It set aside only the part of the High Court order that disposed of the criminal petition, revived the petition on the High Court’s file, and kept Kamran on interim bail on the earlier terms in place of the final bail granted. The department must file a counter affidavit in two weeks and the accused a rejoinder in one week, and the Single Judge was asked to decide the matter preferably by the end of 2026.

The Bench added administrative guidance: where a time period such as two weeks ends during a vacation, the registry should ordinarily exclude the vacation days, and where a bench makes a matter returnable within a vacation or directs listing before the Vacation Bench, it should be listed accordingly, subject to High Court rules.

Why this matters for Criminal practitioners in Haryana

At district level in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, the bail ladder runs from the Magistrate to the Sessions Court and then to the Punjab & Haryana High Court, and clients often ask whether a quashing petition, a writ or a second bail plea can be filed alongside. This decision makes the duty of disclosure concrete. A petition should state every earlier application, its forum and its result, including interim refusals, and counsel should resist the temptation to leave out an unfavourable order.

It also fits with the High Court’s own recent position on repeated anticipatory bail pleas without changed circumstances, which this blog has covered, and it extends the same logic to overlapping remedies. For prosecuting agencies and the department, the case is a reminder that complaints about vacation listing and denied hearing are best raised promptly, with a clear record of what was said to the court.

Note also the Court’s remedy: it preferred a fresh, fair hearing on notice rather than punishing the accused with immediate custody. That balance is useful when arguing against cancellation or for restoration of a matter heard ex parte.

Statutory cross-references

The offences are under the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (false invoicing and input tax credit under Section 132(1) read with Section 16(2)(b), punishable under Section 135 in the form relevant to this complaint). The High Court’s special bail power, Section 439 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, corresponds to Section 483 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023; the general bail provisions in Sections 436–437 CrPC correspond to Sections 478–480 BNSS. Complaints about vacation listing are governed by each High Court’s own rules.
