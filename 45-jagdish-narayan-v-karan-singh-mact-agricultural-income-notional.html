Jagdish Narayan v. Karan Singh: Supreme Court Says Unproven Farm Income Must Be Assessed as a Labourer's Notional Income

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseJagdish Narayan v. Karan Singh & Ors.
BenchJustices N. Kotiswar Singh & K. Vinod Chandran
DateJudgment dated 7 July 2026
CitationReported by LawBeat, 21 July 2026 · neutral/SCC citation not yet available in open reporting
Key SectionsSs. 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

The appellant, a pillion rider on a motorcycle, suffered grievous injuries when a car collided with the bike, leaving him with 78% permanent disability. The car driver's negligence was established and the offending vehicle was insured, so liability itself was never seriously in dispute. The real fight was over one number: the claimant's monthly income, the figure that every other head of MACT compensation is built on.

The claimant asserted he earned Rs. 10,000 a month as a farmer, and produced revenue documents showing he owned agricultural land measuring 7.82 hectares. On the face of it, that looks like solid proof of a farming livelihood. But under cross-examination, two things came apart: he admitted he had given the land out to another person to actually cultivate, and the ownership documents themselves showed he held only a 1/16th share in that total extent of land — not the whole of it. The Motor Accident Claims Tribunal was not persuaded that this made him a working farmer earning Rs. 10,000 a month. It instead treated him as a labourer, fixed his notional monthly income at Rs. 3,000, and awarded total compensation of Rs. 4,00,000 — further cut by half after finding him 50% contributorily negligent. The High Court reversed the contributory negligence finding, enhanced the monthly income figure, and raised the award under various heads to Rs. 8,90,683. The matter reached the Supreme Court on further appeal.

What the court held

A Bench of Justices N. Kotiswar Singh and K. Vinod Chandran agreed with the Tribunal's original approach on income, not the claimant's. The Court held: "We cannot hence accept the plea of the appellant having earned Rs. 10,000 as a farmer" — because mere ownership of agricultural land, without proof that the claimant was personally carrying out farming operations and earning from them, cannot establish "farmer's income" for compensation purposes. Land given out to someone else to cultivate does not generate income for the owner in the sense the claim required; and a 1/16th ownership share undercut the claimed scale of the operation in any event.

Having rejected the farmer-income claim, the Court turned to what income figure should apply instead, and anchored its calculation in its own earlier ruling in Ramachandrappa v. Royal Sundaram Alliance Insurance Co. Ltd. (2011), which had fixed a coolie's/labourer's monthly income at Rs. 4,500 for the year 2004. Applying a standard notional escalation of Rs. 500 per year, the Court arrived at Rs. 8,000 per month as the appropriate labourer's income for 2011, the year relevant to this accident. It accepted the multiplier of 9 already applied by the Tribunal and High Court (the claimant being 57 years old), and upheld a 10% addition for future prospects. The final calculation for loss of earning capacity was: Rs. 8,000 × 12 × 9 × 110% × 78% = Rs. 7,41,312, with the other heads awarded by the High Court left undisturbed, and 8% interest ordered on the enhanced amount.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

Agricultural landowners are among the most common categories of claimants before Haryana's Motor Accident Claims Tribunals, including at Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri, and it is routine for such claimants to place jamabandi, fard, and khasra girdawari extracts on record and simply assert a monthly farming income based on the size of the holding. This ruling is a clear signal that ownership records alone will not do the job. A claimant asserting agricultural income must additionally show, through evidence, that they were personally engaged in cultivation — not merely a landowner who has leased or given out the land to a tenant or family member — and ideally support the claimed income with mandi arhtiya receipts, crop-sale records, bank credits, or agricultural income tax returns where available.

For claimants' counsel, the practical lesson is to build this evidence at the Tribunal stage itself, since it is far harder to repair a thin income record on appeal. For counsel appearing for insurers, this judgment is directly citable to challenge inflated farm-income claims that rest on land ownership documents alone, particularly where cross-examination can establish, as it did here, that the land was actually worked by someone else or that the claimant's real share in the property was far smaller than the headline acreage suggested. Where such proof is genuinely absent, this ruling confirms that the fallback is not a bare guess but a structured notional labourer income, escalated year-on-year from the Ramachandrappa benchmark, which gives both sides a predictable number to work with rather than an open-ended dispute.

Statutory cross-references

Sections 166 and 168 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, which govern the filing of claim petitions and the Tribunal's power to make an award, are untouched by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, since MACT proceedings are civil and quasi-judicial rather than criminal in character and continue to be governed by the Motor Vehicles Act read with the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 for procedural matters. Documentary evidence commonly relied on in such claims — jamabandi, fard, and khasra girdawari extracts — will now be assessed for genuineness and presumptive value under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which has replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872; the presumption attaching to certified copies of public documents, formerly under Section 79 of the Evidence Act, now sits at the corresponding provision of the BSA.
