S v. State of Odisha: Supreme Court Uses Article 142 to Dissolve a “Dead End” Marriage, Fixes ₹35 Lakh Permanent Alimony and Quashes 18 Cross-Cases

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — S v. State of Odisha & Ors.
BenchJustice B.V. Nagarathna and Justice R. Mahadevan
Date28 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1060
Key SectionsArt. 142, Constitution of India · S. 13B, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 · S. 498A IPC (S. 85 BNS) · Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005

What the case was about

The parties married on 4 December 2016 in Cuttack, Odisha, and have lived apart since 2022. In the few years since, the marriage turned into a thicket of litigation: a divorce petition, a police chargesheet in Cuttack, complaints under Section 498A IPC and under the Domestic Violence Act in Mumbai, a domestic violence case brought by the husband's mother, transfer petitions before the Orissa and Delhi High Courts, and further matters in Noida. In all, the Supreme Court counted 18 proceedings between the spouses and their families.

After an unsuccessful first attempt at mediation, the parties were sent to the Supreme Court Mediation Centre in February 2026, where a second round succeeded. The husband had begun by offering ₹15 lakh and the wife had asked for ₹30 lakh; they finally settled at ₹35 lakh as permanent alimony. About ₹2 lakh had already been paid, and the balance of ₹33 lakh was handed over in demand drafts in court.

What the court held

The bench, noting that the marriage had “reached a dead end” with no real chance of reconciliation, invoked Article 142 of the Constitution to dissolve the marriage by a decree of divorce by mutual consent. It then went a step further and quashed all the pending inter-se civil and criminal proceedings, and barred fresh litigation between the parties on the same matrimonial disputes. The transfer petitions were disposed of and the Registry was told to draw up the decree.

The legal reasoning rests on the line of decisions that begins with Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan (2023), where a Constitution Bench held that the Supreme Court can use Article 142 to dissolve a marriage that has irretrievably broken down, without making the parties wait out the six-month waiting period under Section 13B(2) of the Hindu Marriage Act. Here, the Court applied it where the parties had actually agreed, and used the same power to put an end to the criminal cases that had grown around the marriage. The important point is that the settlement was real: the money was paid in court, the wife consented, and mediation had produced the terms.

Why this matters for Matrimonial practitioners in Haryana

Anyone who has run matrimonial cases in Bhiwani or Charkhi Dadri knows the pattern: a petition under Section 13, a maintenance case, a domestic violence complaint, an FIR under Section 498A IPC (now Section 85 BNS) and perhaps a cheque or custody matter, all between the same two people and all in different courts. The ruling is a reminder that the quickest way out is a global settlement, and that the Supreme Court will convert it into a decree and wipe out the cross-cases in one sitting.

At the district court level, the usable parts are practical. First, a mediated settlement should say clearly how each pending case will end, and the alimony should be paid or secured when the settlement is recorded, because that is what gave the Supreme Court comfort here. Second, the six-month cooling-off period can be waived by a family court in an appropriate case, as the Supreme Court has held in Amardeep Singh (2017) and Shilpa Sailesh, so there is no need to wait for a Supreme Court order if the parties have truly settled. Third, the quashing of non-compoundable offences such as Section 498A cannot be done by a trial court; for that, the parties go to the Punjab and Haryana High Court under Section 528 BNSS (earlier Section 482 CrPC), and counsel should plan the sequence of orders with this in mind.

One caution: Article 142 is not available to district courts or the High Court, and the figure of ₹35 lakh is the product of that couple's own bargain, not a formula. Quantum depends on income, duration, assets and the needs of the spouse and children.

Statutory cross-references

Divorce by mutual consent is governed by Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, with the six-month period in sub-section (2); permanent alimony is dealt with under Section 25 of the same Act. Section 498A IPC corresponds to Section 85 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, and the power of the High Court to quash proceedings, earlier Section 482 CrPC, is now Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. Domestic violence proceedings continue under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and maintenance under Section 144 BNSS (formerly Section 125 CrPC).
