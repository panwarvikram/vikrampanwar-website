State of Haryana v. Arun & Ors.: P&H High Court Lays Down a Six-Fold Test for Disclosure Statements, Frees Four Men From Death Row

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseState of Haryana v. Arun & Ors.
BenchJustice Anoop Chitkara & Justice Ramesh Chander Dimri, Punjab & Haryana High Court
DateReported by LiveLaw, 31 August 2026 · The Tribune, 26 August 2026
CitationMRC-9-2023 & CRA-D-1522-2023
Key SectionsS. 27, Indian Evidence Act, 1872 · S. 6, POCSO Act · Ss. 376-DA & 302/34, IPC

What the case was about

In the early hours of 6 August 2021, two sisters aged 13 and 12 — referred to by the High Court by the affectionate names "Laado" and "Laadli" — were rushed to a Delhi hospital and died. Their mother initially told doctors and the police that both girls had been bitten by a snake. Autopsies conducted the next day found no snakebite marks at all. Instead, the post-mortem revealed injuries consistent with sexual assault, and toxicology found dark brown stomach fluid containing Pendimethalin, a herbicide.

Three days after the deaths, the mother lodged a fresh complaint naming four neighbours — tenants in the same building — accusing them of entering the room at night, raping both girls, forcibly administering poison, and threatening the family into silence. An FIR followed, and all four men were arrested the next day. At trial, they were convicted under Section 6 of the POCSO Act, Section 376-DA IPC (gang rape of a woman under sixteen), Section 302 read with Section 34 IPC, and other provisions, and sentenced to death by orders dated 18 and 24 November 2023. The reference for confirmation of the death sentences, along with the convicts' appeal, reached the Punjab and Haryana High Court.

The prosecution's case leaned heavily on two things: disclosure statements the four accused allegedly made in police custody, said to have led to the recovery of a cloth (a "parna") bearing semen stains and a bottle of poison, and the mother's accusatory account naming the four men. Both would come apart under the High Court's scrutiny.

What the court held

The Division Bench of Justice Anoop Chitkara and Justice Ramesh Chander Dimri set aside all four convictions and death sentences and ordered the men's immediate release. In doing so, the Court formulated what it called a "sextipartite test" — a six-part checklist that a disclosure statement must satisfy before it can be treated as admissible under Section 27 of the Evidence Act: the maker must already be an accused in the case; he must be in custody for that offence when he makes the disclosure; the disclosure must be voluntary, free of compulsion, coercion, threats, inducement or deception; the information disclosed must be relevant to the crime; it must be distinctly connected to the fact discovered; and — the requirement that decided this case — the fact discovered pursuant to the disclosure must not already have been discovered by the time the disclosure was made.

Applying that last limb, the Court found the prosecution's own paperwork defeated it. The crime-scene report, prepared by the forensic team on 11 August 2021, recorded that a metallic container of poisonous liquid had already been taken into police possession before the team even arrived at the scene. Yet the disclosure statements attributed to the accused, purportedly made the following day, described them leading police to a plastic bottle they claimed to have hidden. Once an object is already in police custody, the Court held, there can be no subsequent "discovery" of it at an accused's instance — the disclosure statements, and everything said to flow from them, fell outside Section 27 entirely. A similar contradiction dogged the recovery of the bloodstained cloth: only two of the four disclosure statements even mentioned it, yet the recovery memo showed all four men jointly pointing it out, from a spot different from where the statements said it had been concealed.

The ocular case fared no better. The mother, the sole surviving witness to the alleged intrusion, gave at least three materially inconsistent accounts — her initial snakebite story naming no one, her written complaint three days later naming all four accused with specific roles, and a Section 164 CrPC statement the very next day in which she said she did not know the names of any of the accused except one "Sattu," a name matching none of the four men (though it was, coincidentally, one man's father's name). At trial she resiled from everything, was declared hostile, denied making either the complaint or the Section 164 statement despite a fingerprint expert confirming her thumb impressions on both, and testified on oath that none of the four had wronged her daughters. The scribes of neither document were ever identified or examined. The Court held it would be "perilous" to selectively believe her one accusatory version while discarding both her first account and her sworn testimony — a bare FIR, after all, is not substantive evidence in itself.

DNA from the vaginal swabs of both victims and from the recovered cloth did not match any of the four accused. Medical examination of the men, six days after the incident, found only old scars — not fresh injuries — directly contradicting the trial court's finding that these were wounds from the victims' resistance. The Court also noted, without treating it as conclusive, the striking fact that the victims' parents and adult brothers had consistently refused to implicate any of the four men despite direct, leading questions from the prosecution. Weighing all of this together, the Bench concluded that while the commission of rape and death by poisoning stood established by the medical evidence, "the prosecution had failed to establish any legally admissible evidence connecting any of the four accused to the commission of any offence beyond a reasonable doubt."

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

The sextipartite test gives defence counsel in Haryana's district courts a compact, court-endorsed checklist to attack disclosure-based recoveries — a staple of prosecution evidence in NDPS, arms, and property-recovery cases as much as in cases of this gravity. The "already discovered" limb deserves particular attention: it is common in mofussil investigations for a crime-scene team's own report to record an object's seizure before any disclosure statement is drawn up, simply because paperwork gets sequenced loosely in the field. This judgment shows that discrepancy is not a technicality — it can be fatal to the entire recovery.

The ruling is also a pointed reminder about how courts must treat a hostile or inconsistent complainant's statements: a prosecutrix's or witness's later, more incriminating version cannot simply be cherry-picked over an earlier exculpatory account and a sworn denial at trial, without a coherent explanation for the shift. For prosecutors and investigating officers, the lesson runs the other way — sequencing of seizure memos, crime-scene reports, and disclosure statements must be internally consistent, or a conviction built on them, however serious the underlying crime, will not survive appellate scrutiny.

Statutory cross-references

Section 27 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, applied in this case, now corresponds to Section 23 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which carries forward the same "fact discovered" principle for offences investigated after 1 July 2024. Section 302 IPC (murder) corresponds to Section 103(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, while Section 376-DA IPC (gang rape of a woman under sixteen) has been subsumed, with an enhanced age threshold, into Section 70(2) BNS, which now covers gang rape of a woman under eighteen. The POCSO Act, 1989, is unaffected by the BNS/BNSS renumbering and continues to apply as before.
