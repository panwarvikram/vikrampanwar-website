Void and Voidable Marriages Under the Hindu Marriage Act: What Sections 11 and 12 Actually Do

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseYamunabai Anantrao Adhav v. Anantrao Shivram Adhav
BenchSupreme Court of India
Date27 January 1988
Citation1988 AIR 644 · (1988) 1 SCC 530
Key SectionsSs. 5, 11, 12 & 16, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955

What the topic is about

Most people who walk into a family court talk about "divorce" without pausing over whether their marriage was even valid to begin with. But a fair share of matrimonial disputes in Haryana's district courts — second marriages contracted while a first spouse is still alive, marriages rushed through without either side disclosing a pre-existing condition, marriages within a prohibited degree of relationship that nobody flagged until much later — are not really divorce cases at all. They are cases about whether a marriage existed in the eyes of law from day one, and the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 answers that question very differently depending on which of two provisions applies: Section 11, which deals with marriages that are void, and Section 12, which deals with marriages that are merely voidable.

The distinction is not academic. A void marriage is treated as though it never happened at all — it needs no court order to end it, though parties usually still seek a declaration for the sake of clarity and for use in other proceedings. A voidable marriage, by contrast, is a valid marriage right up until a court actually annuls it; until that decree is passed, the parties remain husband and wife with all the rights and obligations that come with it. Getting this wrong — treating a voidable marriage as if it were automatically void, or vice versa — can cost a client dearly in a maintenance claim, a succession dispute, or a bigamy prosecution.

What the legal position is

Section 11 lists three situations in which a Hindu marriage is void from its very inception: where either party has a living spouse at the time of the marriage (Section 5(i)); where the parties are within the degrees of "sapinda" relationship unless custom or usage permits it (Section 5(v)); and where the parties fall within "prohibited relationship" as defined under the Act, again unless custom permits it (Section 5(iv)). If any of these conditions existed at the time of the ceremony, the marriage is a nullity in law regardless of how many rituals were performed or how long the parties lived together afterward. The Supreme Court's ruling in Yamunabai Anantrao Adhav v. Anantrao Shivram Adhav illustrates just how strictly this is applied: a woman who married a man whose first wife was still alive was held not entitled to maintenance under Section 125 of the CrPC as his "wife," because in the eye of the law there was simply no valid marriage to found that claim on. The ruling has since been debated and partially cushioned by later decisions recognising maintenance in long-term domestic relationships on other grounds, but the core holding on the legal status of a bigamous marriage under Section 11 has not moved.

Section 12 covers a narrower and very different set of grounds, each of which renders a marriage voidable rather than void: the respondent's impotence rendering the marriage incapable of consummation; a contravention of the condition in Section 5(ii) relating to mental capacity to give valid consent or to consummate the marriage; consent to the marriage having been obtained by force or by fraud as to the nature of the ceremony or as to any material fact concerning the respondent; or the respondent being pregnant by someone other than the petitioner at the time of the marriage. None of these marriages is automatically void — each survives as a valid marriage unless and until a party actually files a petition and a court passes a decree of nullity. Section 12(2) then builds in its own limitation clock: a petition on the ground of force or fraud must ordinarily be filed within one year of the force ceasing or the fraud being discovered, and is barred altogether if the petitioner has voluntarily lived with the respondent as spouses after discovering the truth; a petition on the ground of pre-marital pregnancy by another must be filed within one year of the marriage, and only if the petitioner was unaware of the fact and did not consent to marital relations with knowledge of it.

One safeguard runs through both provisions and matters more than either might suggest at first glance: Section 16 of the Act protects the legitimacy of children born of a marriage that is later declared void or annulled as voidable, and, following the 1976 amendment, gives such children rights in their parents' property as if the marriage had been valid. Whatever happens to the marriage itself, the child's status and inheritance rights are ring-fenced from that outcome.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Bigamy allegations, undisclosed impotence, concealed illness, and marriages contracted within a prohibited relationship are not rare fact patterns in district court practice around Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, particularly in disputes involving second marriages entered into after a first wife's desertion but before any divorce decree was actually obtained. The first task in any such matter is classification: is this a Section 11 case, where the marriage was void from the start and no limitation period under Section 12(2) even applies, or is it a Section 12 case, where a missed one-year window can permanently foreclose the remedy of annulment regardless of how genuine the underlying grievance is? Advocates who file a Section 12 petition after the limitation clock has run, or who advise a client to simply "live separately" without filing anything at all, may find their client stuck with a marriage that remains legally valid for maintenance and inheritance purposes despite everything that has gone wrong.

The classification also decides forum strategy in related proceedings — a maintenance claim, a domestic violence complaint, or a criminal complaint of bigamy under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita all turn on whether the marriage in question was void, voidable but still unannulled, or voidable and already annulled. Getting the client's actual legal status right at the outset, rather than assuming "the marriage is over" covers the field, is often the difference between a claim that succeeds and one that collapses on a technical objection raised much later in the proceedings.

Statutory cross-references

Sections 5, 11, 12 and 16 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 remain the operative provisions and have not been altered by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 framework. What has changed is the criminal law overlay: a bigamous marriage that also amounts to the offence of bigamy is now prosecuted under Section 82 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (the successor to Section 494 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860), and a false marriage ceremony knowingly and fraudulently undergone without a lawful marriage is addressed under Section 83 BNS (successor to Section 495 IPC). Maintenance claims that intersect with a disputed marital status are now filed under Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, the successor to Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, which was the very provision at issue in the Yamunabai ruling.
