Permanent Alimony Under Section 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act: How Courts Decide Quantum and Duration

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing LawHindu Marriage Act, 1955
ForumFamily Court / District Court that passed the decree of divorce, judicial separation, restitution, or nullity
Leading CasesKalyan Dey Chowdhury v. Rita Dey Chowdhury Nee Nandy (2017) · Rajnesh v. Neha, (2021) 2 SCC 324
Key SectionsS. 25, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 · S. 144, BNSS, 2023 (parallel remedy)

What the topic is about

Section 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 is the provision that decides what happens to a spouse's finances after the marriage itself has ended, or been formally altered, by a decree. It is not confined to divorce: an order under Section 25 can follow a decree of judicial separation, restitution of conjugal rights, or even nullity, which is why the section speaks of "any decree" rather than divorce alone. Litigants and, frequently, junior counsel tend to conflate this with interim maintenance under Section 24 of the same Act or with maintenance under Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (the successor to Section 125 CrPC), but the three operate on different logic. Section 24 supports a spouse during the pendency of the very proceeding in which it is sought. Section 144 BNSS is a summary, forum-neutral remedy available regardless of religion or even the existence of a decree. Section 25 is the final word, passed at or after the decree, and is meant to be a more considered, evidence-based determination of what one spouse must pay the other going forward.

Either spouse can apply, and the court can act either on application or, under the section's own wording, even on its own motion at the time of passing the decree. The order can direct payment as a gross sum or as a periodical sum, secured or unsecured, for a term not exceeding the life of the applicant, having regard to the respondent's own income and property and to the applicant's income and property, conduct of the parties, and other circumstances of the case. That last phrase — "other circumstances" — is where most of the litigation actually happens, because it is broad enough to absorb everything from the standard of living enjoyed during the marriage to the age, health, and earning capacity of both spouses.

What the legal position is

Courts do not apply a rigid formula, but decades of case law have settled a fairly predictable set of factors: the status and social standing the parties enjoyed during the marriage; the reasonable needs of the applicant, including housing and, where relevant, the needs of children in the applicant's custody; the income, movable and immovable property, and earning capacity of both spouses, not merely the payer's disclosed salary; the number of dependents each party supports; whether the applicant gave up a career, education, or employment for the marriage; and the conduct of the parties leading up to and during the litigation. The Supreme Court's 2020 ruling in Rajnesh v. Neha went further and made this exercise mandatory rather than aspirational, directing every family court and district court to require both spouses to file a structured affidavit of assets, income, and liabilities before quantum is decided, precisely because alimony litigation was too often being resolved on guesswork or on whichever figure was shouted loudest in court. That judgment also settled a recurring practical dispute by holding that maintenance, once granted, should ordinarily run from the date the application was filed, not from the date of the final order, so that a payer cannot benefit from the law's delay.

On quantum itself, the Supreme Court's 2017 decision in Kalyan Dey Chowdhury v. Rita Dey Chowdhury Nee Nandy is the case most frequently cited before district courts, for its observation that maintenance pegged at roughly 25% of the husband's net monthly salary represents a just and reasonable benchmark in a straightforward case with no unusual complicating factors. It is important to be precise about what that case decided: it is a benchmark for a particular fact pattern, not a statutory ceiling or floor, and courts routinely depart from it upward or downward once property, a second income, joint family assets, or the needs of dependent children enter the picture. Courts have also made clear, including in rulings such as Shailja v. Khobbanna, that a wife's own modest income does not by itself disentitle her to maintenance calibrated to the marital standard of living, since the statute measures continuity of that standard, not bare subsistence.

An order under Section 25 is not carved in stone. Sub-section (2) lets either party apply to the same court for variation, modification, or rescission if the financial circumstances of either spouse change materially afterward — a job loss, a disabling illness, a windfall, or a significant salary increase have all been accepted grounds. Sub-section (3) goes further, permitting the court to cancel or reduce the order if the receiving party remarries, or if a wife is shown not to have remained chaste, or if a husband has had sexual relations outside the marriage; this last limb has drawn sustained academic and judicial criticism for its unequal, morality-laden framing and remains open to future scrutiny, but it stays on the statute book today.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Section 25 applications are filed in nearly every contested matrimonial matter that reaches a decree before the District Courts in Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri, and the most common drafting failure counsel encounter is treating the alimony prayer as an afterthought, supported by a bare, unparticularised claim of income or need. After Rajnesh v. Neha, that invites an adverse inference. Counsel on both sides should prepare the affidavit of assets, liabilities, and income the moment a Section 25 claim is anticipated, not wait for the court to direct it, and should build a documentary record — salary slips, land records, bank statements, dependent details — well before the quantum hearing rather than relying on oral assertions.

For clients of modest means, who make up the bulk of the practice in this region, the "25% of net salary" reference point from Kalyan Dey Chowdhury is a useful starting anchor for settlement talks, but it should never be presented as a guaranteed outcome; a landholding, a second income, or joint family assets change that calculation substantially, as does custody of minor children. Practitioners should also flag any parallel Section 144 BNSS or Domestic Violence Act maintenance order the client already holds, since courts routinely adjust a later Section 25 award to account for what has already been paid elsewhere, to avoid the same need being compensated twice.

Statutory cross-references

Section 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 is unaffected by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, since it is a civil, personal-law remedy rather than a criminal one. The parallel criminal-law maintenance route, formerly under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, is now Section 144 of the BNSS, 2023, and continues to be available independently of, and in addition to, any Section 25 order, subject to the adjustment principle noted above.
