M/s ASJ Finsolutions v. Vikram Bajaj (2026 INSC 1062): Supreme Court Says an E-Auction Notice Can Justify Forfeiting a Defaulting Bidder’s ₹6.39 Crore Even if the IBC Liquidation Regulations Are Silent

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — M/s ASJ Finsolutions Pvt. Ltd. v. Vikram Bajaj (Civil Appeal No. 13023 of 2025)
BenchJustice J.B. Pardiwala and Justice K. Vinod Chandran
Date28 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1062
Key SectionsIBC liquidation e-auction; IBBI (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, Sch. I; e-auction notice terms

What the case was about

A corporate debtor went into liquidation under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code after no resolution plan was filed. The Liquidator put its properties to e-auction by a notice dated 25 October 2021. Lot 5, land at Village Nangal Khurd, Tehsil Sonepat, Haryana, carried a reserve price of ₹25.56 crore and was sold “as is where is.” The notice disclosed a pending civil dispute over the sale deed for part of the land.

ASJ Finsolutions bid at the reserve price on 15 November 2021 and won. It paid ₹6.39 crore without protest: the ₹2.55 crore earnest money deposit (10% of the reserve price) and a further ₹3.84 crore, together 25% of the bid. The balance of ₹19.17 crore was due within 30 days, or within 90 days with 12% annual interest. The bidder wrote that it would pay by 14 February 2022 but never did. Instead it asked for the prior title deeds, a request the NCLT rejected in March 2023 and the NCLAT affirmed the next month.

The lot was re-auctioned for ₹31.10 crore, about ₹5.54 crore more than the first bid. The bidder then sought to annul the forfeiture and recover its ₹6.39 crore. The NCLT agreed, applying a “triple test”; the NCLAT reversed that order and upheld the forfeiture.

What the Court held

A bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran dismissed the appeal on 28 September 2026 and upheld forfeiture of the full ₹6.39 crore. Reports of the judgment (Verdictum and LawChakra) record the following reasoning.

Silence in the Regulations is not a bar. Schedule I of the IBBI (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 caps the EMD at 10% of the reserve price but says nothing expressly about forfeiture when the balance goes unpaid. The Court held that this silence did not stop the e-auction notice from providing for forfeiture, since the Regulations do not prohibit it. The bidder had accepted those terms by bidding and depositing money.

The triple test did not rescue the bidder. A bidder can avoid forfeiture where it acted with a hidden agenda to rig the auction, was not a genuine bidder with financial capacity, or was prevented by extraneous circumstances. None applied. Repeated assurances of willingness to pay are not proof of capacity; the bidder needs to put material before the tribunal.

Title objections come before bidding. The title dispute was disclosed in the notice and the sale was “as is where is.” The bidder did not ask to verify the title deeds before bidding or depositing. A challenge by a third company was treated as an afterthought, because that company filed one application and then withdrew it.

A higher resale price is not a set-off. The Court said the better price reflected the property’s inherent value and did not offset the costs of the failed sale. It also refused the alternative plea to limit forfeiture to 10% of the reserve price, relying on NCLAT decisions in Westcoast Infraprojects and Potens Transmission that allowed forfeiture of both EMD and other deposits under a notice clause. A discrimination argument raised late, only in written submissions, was not entertained.

The Court decided the case on its facts and on the terms of this notice, so it is not a licence to forfeit in every liquidation sale.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Land in Sonepat, Panipat, Jhajjar and neighbouring districts regularly comes up in liquidation and recovery auctions, and Haryana practitioners advise both bidders and liquidators. For bidders, the message is blunt: read the e-auction notice as a contract, because its forfeiture clause will be enforced even if the Regulations do not mention forfeiture. Complete title and encumbrance checks before the bid, not after, and keep documentary proof of funds ready; an email promising payment will not carry the day.

For liquidators and resolution professionals, the ruling supports drafting clear forfeiture and payment-schedule clauses, disclosing known disputes in the notice, and documenting the bidder’s unprotested payments. For lawyers acting for a defaulting bidder, the realistic arguments are those inside the triple test, supported by evidence of rigging, inability to pay because of something outside the bidder’s control, or similar. A bare claim of title uncertainty, once disclosed, is weak.

Statutory cross-references

This is an insolvency and contract matter, so no IPC/CrPC to BNS/BNSS mapping arises. The provisions engaged are the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (liquidation stage), Schedule I of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 (EMD cap and auction conduct), and the terms of the e-auction notice itself. The reports reviewed do not state how IBC Section 35 or Regulation 33 were applied, so they are not relied on here.
