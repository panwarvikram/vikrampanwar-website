K Rashik v. National Insurance Co. Ltd.: Supreme Court Says an Insurer's Business Address Alone Cannot Fix a MACT's Territorial Jurisdiction

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / CaseK Rashik v. National Insurance Company Ltd. & Anr., Supreme Court of India
BenchJustices Ujjal Bhuyan & Atul S. Chandurkar
Date16 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 996
Key SectionsS. 166(2), Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

A claimant who had suffered loss in a motor accident filed his compensation petition before the Motor Accidents Claims Tribunal at Chennai. Neither the accident nor the claimant's own residence or place of business fell within Chennai's jurisdiction. What did fall within Chennai was the insurer's place of business — National Insurance Company Ltd. had an office there. On that single thread, the claimant sought to anchor the Tribunal's jurisdiction.

The insurer objected. The Madras High Court agreed with the insurer, set aside the Chennai Tribunal's assumption of jurisdiction, and directed that the claim be tried instead by the MACT at Chittoor, Andhra Pradesh — the place actually connected to the claimant under the statutory scheme. The claimant carried the fight to the Supreme Court, arguing that a claim petition could validly be filed wherever any defendant, including the insurer, carries on business.

The question for the Supreme Court was narrow but practically important: does Section 166(2) of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, which lists the places where a compensation claim may be filed, extend to the place where the insurance company — as opposed to the claimant — carries on business?

What the court held

The Bench of Justices Ujjal Bhuyan and Atul S. Chandurkar read Section 166(2) closely and found the answer in what Parliament chose to include, and what it chose to leave out. The provision gives a claimant a menu of forums: the place where the accident occurred, the place where the claimant himself resides or carries on business, or the place where the defendant resides. What the section does not say is that a claim may be filed where the defendant carries on business. That omission, the Court held, was deliberate and cannot be read back into the statute by implication.

The Court observed that while a claimant is given the added convenience of suing where he himself carries on business — a legislative concession meant to ease the burden of litigation on an accident victim or their dependents — no equivalent concession was extended in respect of an insurer's or any other defendant's place of business. Extending Section 166(2) to cover an insurer's business address, the Court reasoned, would let a claimant forum-shop by simply choosing whichever city happened to house a branch office of the insurance company being sued, regardless of any real connection between that place and the accident, the claimant, or the driver. That would defeat the very purpose of a territorial jurisdiction clause, which is to tie litigation to a place genuinely connected with the dispute.

Applying this reading, the Supreme Court upheld the Madras High Court's order and directed that the claim be tried by the MACT at Chittoor — the forum actually connected to the claimant under the statute, rather than the one connected only to the insurer's corporate presence.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

Every national insurer — National Insurance, Oriental, United India, New India Assurance, and the larger private players — maintains regional or branch offices in Chandigarh, Panchkula, and other hub cities that serve claims arising across Haryana, including Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani. It can be tempting, particularly where a claimant's own residence or the accident site sits in a less convenient forum, to anchor a claim petition to the insurer's nearest branch instead. This judgment forecloses that shortcut in unambiguous terms.

The practical lesson for drafting a claim petition under Section 166 is to anchor jurisdiction only to one of the three statutorily sanctioned pegs: where the accident occurred, where the claimant resides or carries on business, or where the owner or driver — the actual tortfeasor-defendants, as opposed to the insurer joined under Section 149 — resides or carries on business. An insurer's office address, however convenient, cannot do that work on its own. A petition filed on that basis risks a jurisdictional objection at the threshold, eating into the claimant's time and, given how long compensation proceedings already take, compounding the very delay the summary MACT procedure was designed to avoid. Practitioners in Haryana would do well to plead and, where necessary, lead evidence on the claimant's actual residence or place of business at the outset, rather than relying on the insurer's presence as a fallback.

The ruling also offers a useful template for resisting an opposite party's attempt at forum-shopping. Where an insurer or vehicle owner is sued in a distant Tribunal on no stronger ground than a branch office or a registered address in that city, this judgment gives Haryana practitioners solid authority to seek return or transfer of the claim to the Tribunal genuinely connected with the accident or the claimant.

Statutory cross-references

Section 166(2) of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 is a civil, procedural provision and has no counterpart under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. The closest procedural analogy for civil practitioners is the return-of-plaint mechanism under Order VII Rule 10 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, which similarly polices the forum in which a suit may proceed once a genuine territorial defect is shown. The insurer's own liability in a motor accident claim continues to be governed by Section 149 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, which is unaffected by this ruling; only the question of where the claim itself may be tried has been settled here.
