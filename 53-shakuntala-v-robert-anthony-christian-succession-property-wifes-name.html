Shakuntala v. Robert Anthony: Supreme Court Says a Wife's Name on the Sale Deed Means the Property Is Hers, Not Her Husband's Estate

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseShakuntala & Ors. v. Robert Anthony & Ors.
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & N. Kotiswar Singh
Date30 July 2026
CitationCivil Appeal arising out of SLP(C) No. 9449 of 2020 · reported by LiveLaw & Law Trend, 30 July 2026
Key SectionsSs. 25, 33, 35 & 38, Indian Succession Act, 1925

What the case was about

In 1959, a man named Mattus Anthony bought a small plot of land in Bilaspur, Chhattisgarh — roughly 1,776 square feet, paid for out of his own pocket — but had the sale deed executed in the names of his two wives, Filomina and Shyam Bai. That single choice, made decades before anyone could have anticipated the litigation it would spawn, is what the Supreme Court had to untangle sixty-seven years later.

Filomina had three children and died in 1985. Shyam Bai had one son, John Anthony, who predeceased her, also in 1985. Mattus Anthony himself died in 1991, and Shyam Bai followed in 2000. In 2002, Shyam Bai's grandchildren — John Anthony's legal heirs — sold half the property to a third party. Filomina's children objected, filed a civil suit in Bilaspur, and asked the court to declare that sale invalid and to recognise their own share.

The case bounced through three tiers before reaching the Supreme Court. The trial court sided with Filomina's children, holding the sale void for want of consent from co-owners. The first appellate court reversed that, holding the land belonged to the legal heirs of both wives in their own right, since Mattus Anthony had bought it in their names using his own money. The Chhattisgarh High Court took a third view: leaning on Section 33 of the Indian Succession Act, which governs how a deceased man's property devolves when he leaves a widow and lineal descendants, it treated the entire property as Mattus Anthony's own estate, split it between his two "widows" collectively and his descendants, and excluded John Anthony's branch entirely by holding that a son from a possibly non-monogamous second union did not count as a "lineal descendant." All three courts, from different angles, wrestled with the same underlying question: whose property was this to begin with, and once that is settled, whose law of succession decides who inherits it?

What the court held

The Supreme Court's answer began with a point the lower courts had talked past: Section 33 only comes into play when a man dies intestate and leaves property that was actually his. Here, the sale deed itself said otherwise — the land was bought and registered in the names of Filomina and Shyam Bai, not Mattus Anthony. Whatever his motive, the property in law belonged to his wives from the moment of purchase. Applying Section 33, which distributes a deceased husband's estate between his widow and descendants, to property that never legally sat in the husband's name was simply the wrong provision.

Once that was settled, the Court worked out each wife's share on its own footing. Filomina died first, in 1985, while Mattus Anthony was still alive. Her half therefore passed under Section 35, which gives a surviving husband the same rights over a predeceased wife's property that a widow has over her husband's — one-third of Filomina's half went to Mattus Anthony, the remaining two-thirds to her own children directly. The one-third Mattus Anthony received then became his own property, and on his death in 1991 it devolved equally among all his children across both wives. Shyam Bai's own half remained entirely her separate property throughout, and on her death in 2000 without a surviving child, it passed under Section 38 to her surviving grandchildren — the very people who had sold half the land in 2002.

The result was a far more finely calibrated division than either the trial court's all-or-nothing approach or the High Court's exclusion of John Anthony's line. The Court also brushed aside the argument that John Anthony was not Mattus Anthony's "lineal descendant" — nobody had pressed a case that the marriage to Shyam Bai was invalid or the property benami, so the Court proceeded on the relationships as they stood.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Haryana's district courts see relatively few disputes governed by the Indian Succession Act in its full rigour — most intestate succession litigation here runs through the Hindu Succession Act, 1956. But the 1925 Act still governs the state's Christian residents and many Special Marriage Act unions, and it surfaces by analogy whenever a court reasons about how separate property should devolve. Shakuntala is a reminder that applies well beyond Christian succession: before reaching for any inheritance formula, first nail down who actually owned the asset. A sale deed or registered title in a spouse's or child's name is not a formality to be brushed aside once the nominal owner dies — it decides which Act even applies.

This has direct value in Haryana's common benami-adjacent disputes: land bought by one family member but registered in another's name, only to become the subject of a partition or succession fight a generation later. Practitioners drafting a partition suit or succession petition should lead with clear documentary proof of ownership — sale deed, mutation, and revenue entries — before layering succession arguments on top. Getting the ownership question wrong at the pleading stage, as happened at more than one level here, invites exactly the kind of prolonged, multi-tier litigation this family endured for over two decades. The judgment is also a caution against assuming "widow" always means one person entitled to one undivided share; where a man is survived by more than one wife, courts will examine whether each held property independently before assuming a joint entitlement.

Statutory cross-references

The Indian Succession Act, 1925 is a civil enactment and is unaffected by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which replaced the Indian Penal Code and the Code of Criminal Procedure respectively; no BNS/BNSS cross-reference arises here. For contrast, Haryana practitioners should note that Hindu, Sikh, Jain and Buddhist intestate succession is governed instead by the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, particularly Sections 8 to 13 for males and Sections 15 to 16 for females dying intestate — a materially different scheme from the widow-and-lineal-descendant formula in Sections 33 and 35 of the Indian Succession Act discussed in this case. Property acquired under a Special Marriage Act, 1954 marriage where the parties are governed by different personal laws typically falls back on the Indian Succession Act's default rules under Section 24 read with the relevant succession chapters.
