Grounds for Divorce Under Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955: A Complete Guide

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Governing LawHindu Marriage Act, 1955
ForumFamily Court / District Court of the district where the marriage was solemnised, the parties last resided together, or the respondent resides
Leading CasesNaveen Kohli v. Neelu Kohli, (2006) 4 SCC 558 · Samar Ghosh v. Jaya Ghosh, (2007) 4 SCC 511 · Rakesh Raman v. Kavita, (2023) 1 SCC 483
Key SectionsSs. 13(1) & 13(2), Hindu Marriage Act, 1955

What the topic is about

Every divorce petition filed under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 in the Family Courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani has to be anchored to one or more of the specific grounds Parliament has laid down in Section 13. Unlike some jurisdictions, Indian law does not permit divorce simply because a marriage has broken down or the parties no longer wish to remain married — the petitioner must plead and prove a recognised fault ground, or, far more commonly today, must satisfy a court that the facts amount to "cruelty" or "desertion" as courts have come to read those terms. Understanding exactly what each ground requires, and which grounds are available to a wife alone, is usually the first real conversation an advocate has with a client walking in wanting "a divorce."

Section 13(1) sets out grounds available equally to husband and wife; Section 13(1A) adds two grounds tied to earlier judicial or legal separation; and Section 13(2) creates four additional grounds that only a wife may invoke. A petition can rely on more than one ground simultaneously, and in practice most contested petitions in Haryana are fought principally on cruelty, with desertion and, less often, adultery pleaded in the alternative.

What the legal position is

Section 13(1) lists nine grounds common to both spouses. Adultery requires the respondent to have had voluntary sexual intercourse with a person other than the spouse after the marriage; since the Joseph Shine judgment decriminalised adultery under the erstwhile Section 497 IPC, it survives purely as a civil ground for divorce, provable on the civil standard of preponderance of probability rather than beyond reasonable doubt. Cruelty is the ground most petitions in Haryana are actually decided on, and it covers both physical cruelty and mental cruelty — the Supreme Court in Samar Ghosh v. Jaya Ghosh, (2007) 4 SCC 511, laid down an illustrative (not exhaustive) list of what may constitute mental cruelty, including sustained unjustified accusations, persistent denial of conjugal relations, and conduct causing such mental pain that living together becomes impossible; the test is not any single incident but the cumulative effect of conduct on the petitioner's mind. Desertion requires the respondent to have abandoned the petitioner without reasonable cause and without consent for a continuous period of at least two years immediately before the petition, and requires proof of both the factum of separation and the deserting spouse's intention to permanently end cohabitation (animus deserendi). Conversion to another religion, unsoundness of mind of an incurable nature or a kind that makes cohabitation reasonably impossible, virulent and incurable leprosy (retained in the text though now of limited practical relevance given modern treatment), venereal disease in a communicable form, and renunciation of the world by entering a religious order round out the remaining Section 13(1) grounds. Section 13(1A) adds two further grounds available to either spouse: no resumption of cohabitation for one year or more after a decree of judicial separation, and no restitution of conjugal rights for one year or more after a decree under Section 9 — grounds that convert an earlier, lesser matrimonial remedy into a route to full divorce.

Section 13(2) gives a wife four grounds her husband cannot invoke. She may seek divorce if her husband has another wife living from a marriage solemnised before the 1955 Act (a bigamy-adjacent ground addressing pre-Act polygamous marriages), if the husband has since the marriage been guilty of rape, sodomy, or bestiality, if a decree or order for maintenance has been passed against the husband under Section 125 CrPC (now Section 144 BNSS) or Section 18 of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act and cohabitation has not resumed for a year, or if she was married before attaining the age of fifteen and repudiates the marriage after turning fifteen but before eighteen (the "option of puberty").

Two further threads run through how these grounds are actually applied. First, Section 13-B provides an entirely separate route — divorce by mutual consent, requiring the parties to have lived apart for a year and to jointly move a first and, after a cooling-off period (which the Supreme Court in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, (2017) 8 SCC 746, held is not mandatory and can be waived in appropriate cases), a second motion; this avoids the need to establish fault at all. Second, in genuinely irretrievably broken marriages that do not neatly fit a Section 13 ground but where continued litigation serves no purpose, the Supreme Court has on occasion invoked its extraordinary power under Article 142 of the Constitution to grant a decree of divorce directly, as it did in Naveen Kohli v. Neelu Kohli, (2006) 4 SCC 558, though this power is exercised sparingly and is not available to the Family Courts that hear the overwhelming majority of matrimonial petitions.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Pleading strategy at the outset often determines the life of the case. Cruelty pleadings that recite generic grievances — "quarrelsome nature," "not adjusting" — routinely fail; Family Courts in Haryana, following Samar Ghosh and the Supreme Court's more recent restatement in Rakesh Raman v. Kavita, (2023) 1 SCC 483 (which held that prolonged separation itself can be treated as evidence of mental cruelty, since forcing parties to remain formally married after the relationship has effectively ended serves no one), expect specific incidents, dates, and, where available, corroboration. Where desertion is pleaded, counsel should be alert to the two-year clock running from the date the parties actually stopped cohabiting, not from an earlier quarrel, and should anticipate the respondent's likely defence that the separation had "reasonable cause" or the petitioner's own conduct drove them out (which, if made out, defeats desertion but may itself support a cruelty plea by the other side).

Because a Section 13(2) ground is available only to the wife, advocates representing husbands should scrutinise whether a wife's petition improperly conflates a general cruelty allegation with, say, an unresolved maintenance dispute better addressed as a distinct 13(2)(ii) ground with its own one-year non-cohabitation requirement. Given how often a DV Act complaint, a maintenance petition under Section 144 BNSS, and a Section 13 divorce petition run in parallel in the same family, it is worth advising clients early about how findings in one proceeding (say, on cruelty) can be relied upon, though not treated as conclusive, in the other.

Statutory cross-references

The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 is a personal-law statute and has not been renumbered or amended by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, or Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. Where a Section 13 petition is accompanied by a maintenance claim, the relevant procedural provision is now Section 144 of the BNSS, 2023 (replacing Section 125 of the CrPC, 1973, referenced directly in Section 13(2)(iii)). Where cruelty is also alleged as a criminal offence rather than only a matrimonial ground, the applicable provision is Section 85 (with Section 86 defining cruelty) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, corresponding to the erstwhile Section 498-A IPC. The erstwhile Section 497 IPC (adultery as a criminal offence) stood struck down in Joseph Shine v. Union of India, (2019) 3 SCC 39, and has no BNS equivalent; adultery survives only as a civil ground under Section 13(1)(i) of the HMA.
