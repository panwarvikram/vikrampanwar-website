Senior Citizens Act Proceedings Die With the Senior Citizen: P&H High Court Sends Family Settlement Disputes Back to Civil Court

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — proceedings under the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007, where the senior citizen-applicant died during pendency
BenchJustice Kirti Singh
DateReported 15 July 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 235
Key SectionsMaintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007

What the case was about

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 gives elderly parents a fast, summary remedy: they can approach a Maintenance Tribunal to secure maintenance from children or relatives, and — importantly for property disputes — they can seek eviction of children or relatives from a house if the senior citizen's right to reside there is being denied. Because these proceedings are designed to be quick and protective, they are frequently used not just for straightforward maintenance claims but as a lever in larger family property disputes, including disputes over the validity of a family settlement or an arbitral award dividing ancestral or self-acquired property.

That is exactly what happened in the matter before the Punjab & Haryana High Court. Proceedings had been initiated under the 2007 Act, and as part of that dispute, questions arose about the validity of a family settlement — or, on some readings, an arbitral award — governing how family property was to be divided. Before those proceedings could run their course, the senior citizen at whose instance they had been initiated passed away. The question before the High Court was whether the case could continue in some form, with the deceased's legal representatives stepping in, or whether it necessarily came to an end.

What the court held

Justice Kirti Singh held that proceedings under the Senior Citizens Act do not survive the death of the senior citizen who initiated them. The Court's reasoning was rooted in the personal and welfare-oriented character of the remedy itself: "Proceedings under the Act are intended to secure the welfare of senior citizens during their lifetime. Once the senior citizen, at whose instance such proceedings were initiated, ceases to survive, the very substratum of the proceedings stands extinguished." In other words, the right being enforced — the senior citizen's own right to maintenance and residence during their lifetime — is personal to them, and once they are no longer alive, there is nothing left for the Tribunal or the High Court to protect.

Crucially, the Court rejected the argument that the character of the proceedings changes simply because a family settlement or arbitral award happened to be under scrutiny along the way. As the judgment put it, "the mere circumstance that the proceedings incidentally involved the validity of a family settlement or an arbitral award does not alter their essential character." The Senior Citizens Act route was always, at its core, a summary welfare mechanism — not a forum for adjudicating title, contractual validity, or the enforceability of an arbitral award. Those are civil law questions requiring full-fledged civil adjudication, with all the procedural safeguards — pleadings, evidence, cross-examination — that a summary tribunal proceeding does not offer.

The practical result: the proceedings abated on the senior citizen's death, and the surviving parties — whether children, other relatives, or legal heirs disputing the family settlement — were left to pursue whatever grievance they had about the settlement or award before a competent civil forum, through a properly instituted civil suit or other appropriate civil remedy.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

This ruling has direct, everyday relevance to district court practice. Senior Citizens Tribunals across Haryana routinely see applications where the underlying dispute is really about property — who gets to live in the house, who inherits what, whether an earlier family settlement was validly executed — dressed up as a maintenance or eviction claim because that route is faster and does not require court fees on the scale of a civil suit. Practitioners advising elderly clients need to be candid about this limitation: the Senior Citizens Act can secure a quick roof over an elderly client's head or an order for maintenance while they are alive, but it cannot be relied upon to finally settle title or contractual disputes, and it offers no guarantee of continuity if the client passes away before the matter concludes.

For lawyers representing children or relatives resisting such applications, this judgment is useful ammunition where the senior citizen has died mid-proceeding — the correct response is to have the Tribunal or writ proceedings declared abated, not to continue litigating the property question in that forum through legal representatives. At the same time, families should not treat this as a loophole: the underlying dispute over the family settlement or arbitral award does not disappear, it simply has to be pursued afresh — and within limitation — before a civil court, which may mean fresh court fees, fresh pleadings, and a materially slower process than the tribunal route offered.

Advocates drafting family settlements involving elderly parents should also take this ruling as a reminder to build in independent enforceability — through registered documents, clear consideration recitals, and where possible contemporaneous civil suits for declaration — rather than relying on the Senior Citizens Act as a backstop enforcement mechanism, since its protective character is tied strictly to the senior citizen's own lifetime.

Statutory cross-references

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 is a standalone civil welfare statute and has no BNS/BNSS equivalent, since it does not create criminal offences in the ordinary sense (though it does provide penal consequences for abandonment of senior citizens under Section 24 of the Act, which remains unaffected by the 2023 criminal law reforms). Where a family settlement or arbitral award is challenged independently, the applicable civil remedy lies through a suit for declaration under the Specific Relief Act, 1963, read with the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 — both of which remain in force unchanged by the recent overhaul of the criminal statutes.
