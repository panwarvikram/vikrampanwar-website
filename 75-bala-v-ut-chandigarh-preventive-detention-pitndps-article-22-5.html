Bala v. UT Chandigarh & Ors.: P&H High Court Quashes a PITNDPS Detention Over a 45-Day Delay in Telling the Detenue She Could Petition the Centre

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court · Bala v. UT Chandigarh & Ors.
BenchJustice Virinder Aggarwal
DateOrder dated 24 August 2026; reported 26 August 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 295 · 2026:PHHC:115366
Key SectionsArt. 22(5), Constitution of India · PITNDPS Act, 1988 · S. 21, NDPS Act, 1985

What the case was about

Preventive detention is one of the few tools the State has that lets it lock a person up without a trial, on the mere apprehension that she might do something prejudicial in the future. Because it sidesteps the ordinary safeguards of a criminal prosecution, the Constitution builds a set of tight procedural guarantees into Article 22(5): the detenue must be told, at the earliest opportunity, the grounds of her detention, and she must be given the earliest possible chance to make a representation against it. This case turned on how seriously the administration is expected to take that second guarantee.

The petitioner had been detained once before under the Prevention of Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1988 (the PITNDPS Act), and that first detention order was set aside by the Advisory Board in July 2025. She was released. Soon after, an FIR was registered against her and twenty-one others under Section 21 of the NDPS Act, allegedly on secret information about narcotics — though, on her account, no search was conducted, nothing was recovered, and nobody was arrested pursuant to it. Roughly three months after that FIR, on 1 November 2025, the Home Secretary, Chandigarh Administration, passed a fresh detention order against her, substantially built on the same material that had already failed to hold up before the Advisory Board once, with the new FIR added in as the fresh circumstance.

She was told on 3 November 2025 of her right to represent to the Detaining Authority, the Chief Secretary, and the Advisory Board, and she did so on 26 November 2025. But her right to make a separate representation to the Central Government — a distinct constitutional safeguard under Article 22(5) — was communicated to her only on 16 December 2025, some 45 days after the detention order. She represented to the Centre on 24 December 2025. What followed was a chain of further delay: the Chandigarh Administration did not forward her representation to the Centre until 3 February 2026; it reached the PITNDPS Division only on 16 March 2026; comments were called for on 18 March 2026 and furnished only on 24 May 2026; and the representation was finally rejected on 2 June 2026 — roughly five months after it was made.

What the court held

Justice Virinder Aggarwal found no satisfactory explanation for either the 45-day delay in informing her of the right to approach the Centre or the months-long delay that followed at every subsequent stage — forwarding, seeking comments, and deciding. The Court was blunt about it: "There is delay at every step i.e. informing about rights, forwarding of representations, furnishing of comments and decision of representations." Relying on the Supreme Court's rulings in Alfiya A. v. State of Kerala, Jaseela Shaji v. Union of India, and Sarabjeet Singh Mokha v. District Magistrate, Jabalpur, the Court reiterated that the right to represent to the Central Government stands independent of the right to represent to the state authorities, and that failing to communicate it promptly leaves the detenue's opportunity to make an effective representation incomplete from the outset.

The Court also rejected the State's implicit suggestion that delay, so long as it can be rationalised, does not vitiate detention. Here, it held, there was no explanation commensurate with the scale of the lapses. Quoting the Supreme Court's observation in Jaseela Shaji that "each day's delay matters" where personal liberty is at stake, the Court held that authorities administering preventive detention owe the detenue the highest degree of diligence and promptitude, not a bureaucratic best-effort. The belated communication of her right, compounded by the drawn-out consideration of her eventual representation, was found to be a substantive denial of a constitutional safeguard rather than a mere procedural slip. On that basis, the Court allowed the habeas corpus petition, quashed the detention order dated 1 November 2025, and set the detenue free.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Preventive detention cases under special statutes — PITNDPS, the National Security Act, the Haryana Prevention of Anti-Social Activities Act, and similar laws that the Chandigarh Administration and Haryana government both use against narcotics networks — turn far more often on procedural compliance than on the underlying allegations. This judgment is a working checklist for a defence lawyer handling a habeas corpus petition against a detention order: pull the file dates for every stage of the representation pipeline — when the detenue was informed of each forum she could approach, when she actually represented, when the state forwarded it, when comments were sought and received, and when the decision came. A gap at any one link in that chain, if unexplained, is enough to unravel the detention regardless of how serious the underlying narcotics allegation is. For practitioners in Haryana's district courts who occasionally get instructed on connected bail or quashing matters feeding into a larger detention case, tracking the detaining authority's own paper trail is often more productive than re-arguing the merits of the FIR.

Statutory cross-references

Article 22(5) of the Constitution remains untouched by the 2023 recodification and continues to govern preventive detention regardless of which special Act authorises it. The underlying FIR in this case was registered under Section 21 of the NDPS Act, 1985, itself a standalone special statute that was not renumbered when the Indian Penal Code and the Code of Criminal Procedure were replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023. General arrest safeguards that would apply to an ordinary NDPS arrest, such as the obligation to communicate grounds of arrest, now sit in Section 47 and related provisions of the BNSS, though this case turned specifically on the separate and more stringent Article 22(5) framework applicable to preventive detention rather than ordinary arrest.
