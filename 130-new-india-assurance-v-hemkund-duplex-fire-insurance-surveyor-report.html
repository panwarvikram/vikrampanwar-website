New India Assurance Co. Ltd. v. Hemkund Duplex and Board Pvt. Ltd.: Supreme Court Says a Consumer Forum Cannot Brush Aside a Surveyor's Report to Award a Fire Insurance Claim

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — New India Assurance Co. Ltd. v. Hemkund Duplex and Board Pvt. Ltd.
BenchJustices Sanjay Kumar & Sanjeev Sachdeva
Date21 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1023 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 969
Key SectionsConsumer Protection Act, 2019 · Contract of Insurance (Uberrimae Fidei) · S. 39, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023

What the case was about

Hemkund Duplex and Board Pvt. Ltd., a paper-board manufacturer, held two fire policies from New India Assurance together worth roughly Rs 27 crore. In May 2007, fire broke out in the company's waste-paper storage yard. The company put in a claim before the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC) for Rs 7.31 crore with 18% interest.

The insurer sent a preliminary surveyor first, who flagged the fire as needing careful investigation before any deliberate cause could be ruled out. A second, final surveyor was then appointed, and assessed the actual net loss at only Rs 46.09 lakh — a fraction of what was claimed — after finding that the company had manipulated its books to inflate the numbers. The surveyor's report also caught a direct contradiction: the company's Vice-President insisted the shed held valuable, usable raw material, while the site supervisors and workers who were actually present said it held nothing but waste paper. On top of that sat a set of circumstances the insurer found hard to explain away innocently — the fire brigade wasn't called for fifty minutes despite an eleven-minute response capability nearby, firefighting efforts at the site were minimal, the fire's spread pattern pointed toward a deliberately set blaze rather than an accidental one, and the company's production records showed irregular yield and consumption figures consistent with inflating the stock said to be destroyed.

On the strength of the final surveyor's findings, the insurer repudiated the claim entirely, citing a false declaration about what was actually stored in the shed and a consequent breach of policy conditions. The NCDRC disagreed and ruled for the company: since the fire's exact cause remained formally undetermined, it held, the insurer could not treat it as proven arson, and it awarded Rs 2.4 crore plus interest, along with Rs 4 lakh in damages and costs.

What the court held

The Supreme Court reversed the NCDRC and restored the insurer's repudiation in full. Its central holding was about how much weight a surveyor's report deserves: while such a report is not automatically binding on a consumer forum, a forum cannot simply brush it aside without pointing to a specific deficiency in it. The NCDRC, the Bench found, had done exactly that — it rejected a detailed, evidence-backed surveyor assessment without engaging with any of its actual findings, and treated the absence of a court-proven arson finding as though it were proof the insured had done nothing wrong.

The Court also addressed when it is legitimate to call in a second surveyor at all: doing so needs cogent reasons, and here those reasons were self-evident, because the first surveyor had expressly recommended a more thorough investigation before any figure could be finalised. Taken together with the false declaration about the shed's contents and the unexplained delay in alerting the fire brigade, the Court held the insured had breached the fundamental condition of an insurance contract — the duty of utmost good faith — and that the insurer was entitled to repudiate the claim outright rather than merely reduce the payout.

The ruling leaves the Rs 2.4 crore NCDRC award, along with the Rs 4 lakh in damages and costs, set aside in its entirety.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Fire and burglary claims against insurers are a recurring line of civil and consumer work for advocates handling shopkeepers', transporters', and small manufacturers' disputes in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, and this judgment hands both sides of that litigation a clear evidentiary yardstick. For counsel representing an insurer, the message is that a well-documented surveyor's report, especially one flagging specific factual contradictions between what a claimant's management says and what its own staff say on the ground, is not something a district forum or commission can wave away with a general finding that the cause of fire is "unproven." A repudiation built on such a report needs to be defended by walking the court through the surveyor's actual findings point by point, not by resting on the report's existence alone.

For counsel representing a claimant, the lesson cuts the other way: a surveyor's report can be challenged, but only by identifying concrete deficiencies in its methodology or its conclusions — vague submissions that the cause of the fire was never conclusively established will not get past a report that is otherwise coherent and internally consistent. Practitioners drafting or opposing insurance claims before District Consumer Commissions in Haryana, where fire and theft claims from small industrial units are common, should treat this ruling as the benchmark for what a "reasoned rejection of a surveyor's report" now has to look like, and should insist that any tribunal doing so spell out precisely where the report falls short.

Statutory cross-references

The NCDRC's jurisdiction and the appeal route to the Supreme Court continue to run under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, which replaced the 1986 Act as the governing statute for such disputes. The evidentiary weight given to a surveyor's report as expert opinion is assessed under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 — Section 39 of the BSA now occupies the space that Section 45 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (opinions of experts) used to hold, and the underlying principle that a court must engage with an expert's reasoning rather than accept or reject it wholesale is carried forward unchanged. The insurer's right to repudiate for a false declaration rests on the general contractual doctrine of uberrimae fidei (utmost good faith) that governs every contract of insurance under the Indian Contract Act, 1872 read with the Insurance Act, 1938.
