A v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Restores a Six-Year-Old Girl’s Interim Custody to Her Mother, Says Maternal Care Is Vital and Habeas Corpus Can Be Used in the Child’s Interest

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab and Haryana High Court — A v. State of Punjab & Ors. (habeas corpus)
BenchJustice Sumeet Goel (single judge)
DateReported 22 October 2026 (date of order not stated in reports)
CitationCRWP-279-2026 (no official citation yet)
Key SectionsArticle 226; BNSS Section 528; Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956

What the case was about

A mother approached the Punjab and Haryana High Court with a habeas corpus petition for her daughter, born in March 2020. She had married in December 2014; her husband had moved to Kuwait for work and settled there, while she lived with her in-laws. She alleged that a relative in the matrimonial family sent her inappropriate messages, made unwanted advances and once tried to molest her, and that her in-laws told her to stay quiet.

On 4 October 2025 she left the matrimonial home with the child. According to her, the in-laws took the girl from her on the way, and she reached her parental home alone. A police complaint produced no effective action, a panchayat called by her parents failed to secure the child’s return, and mediation at the High Court’s Mediation and Conciliation Centre also failed. The private respondents answered that she had left voluntarily, that the grandparents were caring for the child properly, and that a police inquiry had found her allegations against the relative unsubstantiated. The petition was filed under Article 226 of the Constitution read with Section 528 BNSS.

What the court held

Justice Sumeet Goel disposed of the petition by holding that, for the time being, custody “ought to be with the mother.” The key strands of reasoning, as reported, are these.

  • Tender age and maternal care. A child of this age ordinarily needs her mother’s care, and nothing was shown to suggest the mother could not provide it. She showed no economic deprivation, and the respondents did not say she was unfit.
  • Welfare, not status. Custody cannot turn on being a parent or relative alone. The court looks at the child’s safety, the custodian’s relationship with her, and the ability to offer a stable and nurturing environment, acting as parens patriae.
  • Allegations not proved, but not ignorable. The court did not find the allegations against the in-laws established, yet held that, together with the circumstances of the separation, they raised a concern that could not be brushed aside at that stage.
  • Habeas corpus in custody cases. The usual requirement of showing that the child’s custody is demonstrably illegal can be relaxed where the child’s interest demands it, and an alternative remedy does not by itself bar the High Court’s writ jurisdiction. The court relied on its earlier decision in Veerpal Kaur v. State of Punjab (2025:PHHC:113490).
  • Interim in nature. The writ is not a substitute for guardianship proceedings. Permanent custody needs a fuller assessment by the competent court.
  • Prima facie proof of relationship. The birth certificate was accepted as establishing the mother’s relationship with the child at this stage.

The reports do not reproduce the operative directions in detail, and the exact date of the order is not stated; both should be checked against the judgment.

Why this matters for Matrimonial practitioners in Haryana

Custody fights in Haryana frequently begin with one side taking or keeping the child and the other rushing to the High Court. This order is useful when the facts favour a mother: it shows that a very young child removed from the mother’s care by relatives, with the father abroad, can lead to an immediate interim handover without waiting for a full guardianship trial.

It should be read alongside the High Court’s recent decision in Amit Kumar v. State of Punjab, discussed on this blog, where being the biological father alone did not justify habeas corpus while a guardianship case was pending. Taken together, the two rulings show that the writ remains available but is not automatic: the petitioner must show real welfare concerns, and the court will still send long-term custody to the family or guardian court.

Practically, a petitioner should place on record the child’s age and birth certificate, how and when the child was removed, the steps already taken with the police and mediation, and the respondents’ actual capacity to care for the child. A respondent should show concrete evidence of the child’s settled, safe care, not merely deny the allegations. Both sides should expect the court to treat the order as temporary and to push the dispute to the Guardians and Wards Act or the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act forum for a final decision.

Statutory cross-references

The petition invoked Article 226 and Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which corresponds to the inherent powers once found in Section 482 CrPC. Counsel relied on the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, Section 13 of which makes the child’s welfare the paramount consideration. The reports do not say the court decided the matter on the Act itself. The Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 remains the usual route for a full custody determination.
