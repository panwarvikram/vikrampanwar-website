₹236 Crore Bank Fraud: P&H High Court Denies PMLA Bail, Says Abuse of Fiduciary Structures Demands Greater Circumspection

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Sumeet Goel
Date3 September 2026
CitationCRM-M-22257-2026 (O&M)
Key SectionsS. 45, Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 · S. 3 & 4, PMLA · Ss. 120-B, 420 & 467/471, IPC (Ss. 61, 318 & 336/338, BNS, 2023)

What the case was about

The Enforcement Directorate registered an ECIR after the CBI booked a scheduled offence alleging criminal conspiracy, cheating and forgery in the diversion of bank funds by M/s Richa Industries Limited (RIL), a Gurugram-based textile and pre-engineered building company whose loan accounts had been declared fraudulent by its lender bank. The ED's case is that RIL's funds and business assets, generating alleged proceeds of crime of roughly ₹236 crore, were systematically routed through a cluster of shell entities — among them Saariga Constructions Pvt. Ltd., Subhash Gupta & Sons Pvt. Ltd., Riyana Infratech Pvt. Ltd. and Richa Krishna Constructions Pvt. Ltd. — using fabricated accounts, accommodation entries, corporate guarantees and related-party transactions designed to make tainted money look like legitimate business income.

The petitioner before the High Court was RIL's former Managing Director. The ED's case treats him as the de facto controlling authority behind Saariga Constructions even though he held no formal directorship there, and alleges he continued steering RIL's projects — including recommending vendor payments to Saariga — even after RIL entered Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process. He was arrested on 20 January 2026, and the Enforcement Directorate's prosecution complaint, naming 25 witnesses, was filed on 19 March 2026. A Special Court had already rejected his first bail application, finding prima facie material pointing to his active role in RIL's financial affairs. He then approached the High Court seeking regular bail, roughly seven months into custody.

What the court held

The petitioner's case rested on familiar ground for a long-pending economic offence: about seven months already spent in custody; investigation against him complete, with the prosecution complaint on record; evidence that was overwhelmingly documentary and already in the ED's possession, which he argued removed any real risk of tampering; no custody in the predicate CBI offence; deep roots in society and no flight risk; and a trial that, given 1,245 documents running over 28,000 pages and 187 witnesses in the scheduled offence alone, was still at the pre-cognizance stage and unlikely to conclude soon — which he framed as a breach of his Article 21 right to a speedy trial.

Justice Goel rejected the plea, but not by reciting the twin conditions under Section 45 PMLA mechanically. The order instead builds a considered distinction between offences of "passion, impulse or sudden provocation" and economic offences, which "may, indubitably depending upon its factual matrix, involve a preconceived course of conduct, preparation, manipulation of records or transactions, diversion or layering of funds, creation of fictitious documentation, misuse of corporate or fiduciary structures, concealment of the proceeds or coordinated participation of several persons." The absence of physical violence, the Court held, cannot by itself dilute how seriously such offences and their consequences should be treated.

Crucially, the Court did not treat this as a blanket rule against bail in every economic offence. It held that the distinction "assumes particular significance" where the material against an accused is not vague or omnibus but consists of specific, direct evidence — documentary trails, communications, corporate records, witness statements — attributing a definite and active role. Only in such cases must the Court approach bail "with greater circumspection." On the facts, Justice Goel found exactly that kind of specific material: prima facie evidence of the petitioner's effective control over RIL and its group concerns, witness statements, WhatsApp communications connecting him to Saariga Constructions, and his alleged direct involvement in RIL's affairs during the insolvency process. With 25 ED witnesses yet to be examined, the Court held it could not, at this stage, find reasonable grounds to believe he was not guilty, nor assess the evidentiary weight of the material without prejudicing the trial — effectively holding that neither limb of the Section 45 twin test was satisfied.

On the speedy-trial argument, the Court was equally direct: seven months in custody, without more, could not be treated as sufficient "at least for the nonce" to justify bail, given the present stage of the trial and the gravity of the allegations. The petition was dismissed "for the nonce" — a phrase the Court used deliberately, clarifying that its observations touched only the bail stage and would not bind the trial court's eventual assessment on merits, which was directed to proceed uninfluenced by the order.

Why this matters for criminal law practitioners in Haryana

Economic offence and PMLA litigation is no longer confined to metro commercial courts; Haryana's own industrial belt — Gurugram, Faridabad, Panipat — regularly throws up bank-fraud, GST-fraud and Ponzi-style ECIR registrations that eventually land before the Special Court and, on bail, before this High Court. This order is a useful template for how a Section 45 PMLA bail plea actually gets decided once the prosecution complaint is filed and witness examination has not begun: the length of custody standing alone rarely moves the needle; what moves it is whether the material against a specific accused is generic and omnibus, or specific enough to show a "definite and active role." Defence counsel drafting a bail application in a diversion-of-funds or shell-company case would do well to attack the specificity of the material head-on, rather than leaning primarily on custody period and documentary-evidence arguments, both of which this order treats as insufficient on their own once the ED shows a concrete, attributable role.

The order is equally instructive for prosecutors and complainant-side counsel in cheating and criminal-breach-of-trust matters that eventually attract a PMLA angle: framing the chargesheet and prosecution complaint around specific communications and documented control (rather than a generalised corporate-structure narrative) is what lets a court find "particular significance" justifying a more circumspect approach to bail. For practitioners advising promoters, directors or de facto controllers of companies under CIRP, the order is also a reminder that continued involvement in a corporate debtor's operational decisions during insolvency — even informally, even without a directorship — can itself become a central plank of a money-laundering case once dressed up as "effective control."

Statutory cross-references

The predicate CBI offence in this case was framed under the erstwhile Indian Penal Code — conspiracy under Section 120-B, cheating under Section 420, and forgery/using a forged document as genuine under Sections 467 and 471. For offences registered on or after 1 July 2024, the corresponding provisions under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 are Section 61 (criminal conspiracy), Section 318 (cheating), and Sections 336 and 338 (forgery and using a forged document as genuine) respectively. The Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 itself is unaffected by the BNS/BNSS transition; Section 45's twin conditions for bail, and Sections 3 and 4 defining and penalising the offence of money laundering, continue to apply exactly as before.
