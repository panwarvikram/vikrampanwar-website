Abhishek v. State of Rajasthan: Supreme Court Says Injuries to Non-Vital Body Parts Cannot, By Themselves, Defeat a Section 307 IPC Attempt-to-Murder Charge

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Abhishek v. State of Rajasthan & Ors.
BenchJustice Aravind Kumar and Justice Vipul M. Pancholi (judgment authored by Justice Pancholi)
Date7 October 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1093 · Criminal Appeal No. 4767 of 2026
Key SectionsS. 307, 308 IPC · S. 216, 227, 228 CrPC

What the case was about

The complaint arose out of a violent quarrel in an agricultural field in Rajasthan. According to the prosecution, one accused drove a tractor at speed towards the complainant, Abhishek, and his family and hit his mother. Others then attacked the family with sharp-edged farm implements. The complainant and his father suffered deep incised wounds reaching muscle and bone, while the mother had an abrasion. The police filed a charge-sheet under Sections 307, 326, 324, 323 and 447 read with Section 34 IPC.

The Additional Sessions Judge framed a charge of attempt to murder under Section 307 against the accused who allegedly drove the tractor. In revision, the Rajasthan High Court quashed that charge. It noted that the wounds were on the arms and wrists and the mother’s injury was minor, and concluded that Section 308 at most could apply. The complainant appealed.

What the Supreme Court held

On 7 October 2026 the Supreme Court allowed the appeal and restored the Section 307 charge. The central message is that Section 307 asks about intention or knowledge, not about the outcome. The offence is complete if the act was done with such intention or knowledge, and in such circumstances, that death would have made the accused guilty of murder. The injury actually caused need not be life-threatening, and a wound on a non-vital part does not by itself rule the section out.

Intention, the Bench said, is gathered from the whole setting: the weapon used, how persistently the assault was pressed, any earlier animosity, and the manner of attack. The prosecution case was that the victims had raised their arms to protect their heads from axe blows, which explained why the wounds were on the limbs. The High Court had instead looked mainly at where the injuries fell and made what the Supreme Court called a summary, technical assessment.

The Court also stressed the limited role of the court at the charge stage. Under Sections 227 and 228 CrPC the judge only asks whether the material discloses a prima facie case or raises grave suspicion; it is not a mini-trial and evidence is not weighed as at final judgment. Finally, the Bench pointed to Section 216 CrPC as the built-in safeguard: if trial evidence falls short of attempt to murder, the trial court can alter the charge to Section 308 or another lesser offence at any time before judgment. That made early interference by the High Court unnecessary. The Court clarified that its observations do not bind the trial on merits.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Section 307 charges are very common in our Sessions Courts, usually in village and land disputes where several members of each side are injured. Defence counsel often argue at the charge stage that limb injuries or simple injuries mean Section 307 cannot be made out. After this ruling that argument cannot stand on injury location alone. Counsel must engage with the weapon, the number and force of blows, the pre-existing enmity and the medical opinion on what a blow to that area could have caused.

For the prosecution and for complainants, the decision is helpful when a Sessions Judge has framed a Section 307 charge and a revision petition is filed. It supports the position that the High Court should not trim the charge on a surface reading of the MLR. For the defence the practical route is the one the Court itself describes: lead evidence at trial, test the intention evidence in cross-examination, and ask the trial court to alter the charge under Section 216 once the evidence is in. Practitioners should also note that the trial court has the same power to correct a charge that was pitched too high.

Statutory cross-references

Section 307 IPC (attempt to murder) corresponds to Section 109 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, and Section 308 IPC (attempt to commit culpable homicide) to Section 110 BNS. Section 326 IPC (grievous hurt by dangerous weapons) is now Section 118(2) BNS; Sections 323 and 324 IPC are covered by Section 115(2) BNS; Section 447 IPC (criminal trespass) is Section 329(3) BNS; and Section 34 IPC is Section 3(5) BNS. On procedure, Sections 227 and 228 CrPC (discharge and framing of charge) correspond to Sections 250 and 251 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, and Section 216 CrPC (power to alter charge) to Section 239 BNSS.
