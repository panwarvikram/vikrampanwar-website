Shiv Kumar v. State of Haryana: P&H High Court Cuts Rape Sentence to Time Served, Applies Juvenile Justice Act 2000 Retrospectively — 2026 LiveLaw (PH) 220

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana · Published 10 July 2026

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court, Chandigarh
BenchJustice Subhas Mehla
Date of Judgment1 July 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 220
Key SectionsSections 363, 366-A, 376 IPC; Section 2(k), Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000

What the case was about

In May 2001, a trial court in Yamuna Nagar convicted Shiv Kumar for kidnapping and raping a woman, an offence dated to 19 June 1999. He was sentenced under Sections 363 (kidnapping), 366-A (procuring a minor for illicit intercourse), and 376 (rape) of the Indian Penal Code, drawing a cumulative 15 years of rigorous imprisonment, with 8 years attributable to the rape conviction alone. His co-accused died before the case reached judgment.

No juvenility plea was raised at the original 2001 trial. Decades later, Kumar's counsel — advocate Harparteek Singh Sandhu, appointed amicus curiae — approached the High Court arguing that Kumar was actually a juvenile on the date of the offence. The complication: under the Juvenile Justice Act, 1986, which governed in 1999, the cut-off age for boys was 16 — a threshold Kumar had technically crossed. But the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000 raised that cut-off to 18, and the Supreme Court has held in Dharambir v. State (NCT of Delhi) and Hansraj v. State of UP that this higher age applies retrospectively — even to convictions finalised years before the 2000 Act came into force.

In September 2025, the High Court directed the District and Sessions Judge, Yamuna Nagar, to conduct a factual inquiry into Kumar's real age. That inquiry, submitted in December 2025, relied on the original admission-withdrawal register of S.D. Modern School, Saharanpur, produced by the school's principal. The register recorded Kumar's date of birth as 2 January 1983 and showed he studied there from 1992 to 1993 — meaning he was 16 years, 6 months and 17 days old on the date of the offence, a minor by nearly two years under the 2000 Act's standard. The State did not contest this finding.

What the court held

Justice Subhas Mehla accepted the district inquiry report on age without hesitation, finding no reason to disbelieve the school records produced. The ratio breaks down into three parts:

  • Conviction upheld, sentence modified: The Court did not disturb the 2001 finding of guilt. What fell was the sentence — because Kumar, being a juvenile at the time of the offence, could never have lawfully received an adult custodial term for it.
  • Statutory cap on juvenile punishment applies retrospectively: Under the Juvenile Justice Act framework, punishment for a juvenile offender is capped at three years, regardless of the gravity of the underlying offence. This cap displaced the original 8-year term for the rape conviction, even though it was imposed decades before juvenility was raised.
  • No remand to the Juvenile Justice Board: Ordinarily, a juvenility finding would send the matter back to the JJB for sentencing under the juvenile framework. Here, since Kumar had already served 2 years, 8 months and 16 days in custody — more than the 3-year cap would meaningfully add — the Court held a remand would serve no purpose and directly ordered his release on time served.

Why this matters for Criminal practitioners in Haryana

This judgment is a live reminder that a juvenility plea has no limitation period — it can be raised at any stage, including decades after conviction and sentence, and even in cases never argued at trial. For counsel handling old sessions matters or long-pending revisions before the District Courts in Bhiwani, Charkhi Dadri, or elsewhere in Haryana, this means client files with vintage FIRs should be revisited specifically on the age question, especially where the accused was between 16 and 18 at the time of offence and the incident predates the 2000 Act.

Practically, the judgment also underscores the evidentiary weight given to school admission-withdrawal registers over other forms of age proof. Where a school certificate exists and is unrebutted by the State, courts are inclined to accept it without demanding elaborate corroboration — a useful precedent when arguing age determination applications under the current juvenile justice regime as well.

For district court litigators, the case is also a template for procedure: rather than filing a fresh writ or approaching the Juvenile Justice Board cold, the High Court itself directed a fact-finding inquiry through the Sessions Judge concerned — a route worth citing when a juvenility claim surfaces at the appellate or revisional stage and the trial record is silent on age.

Statutory cross-references

The 1999 offence was tried under the old Indian Penal Code framework — Sections 363, 366-A, and 376 IPC. Practitioners handling matters filed after 1 July 2024 should note the corresponding provisions now sit under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023: kidnapping under Section 137 BNS, procuration of a minor under Section 96 BNS, and rape under Section 63 BNS. Procedurally, an equivalent age-determination inquiry today would be conducted under the machinery of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023 read with the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, which has since replaced the 2000 Act but retains its 18-year threshold and forward-looking sentencing caps for juveniles.

This is one of the more significant Punjab Haryana High Court judgments on juvenile justice retrospectivity to come out in 2026, and adds to a growing line of authority — alongside Dharambir and Hansraj — that district courts in Haryana will need to apply when old matters resurface with a juvenility question.
