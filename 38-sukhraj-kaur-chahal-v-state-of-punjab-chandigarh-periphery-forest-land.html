Sukhraj Kaur Chahal v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Halts Construction and Land Transfers in 16 Chandigarh Periphery Villages Pending Forest Land Demarcation

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSukhraj Kaur Chahal v. State of Punjab, with connected petitions CWP-PIL-340-2025 and CWP-3576-2025 — Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchActing Chief Justice Ashwani Kumar Mishra & Justice Rohit Kapoor
DateOrder dated 22 July 2026; reported 24 July 2026
CitationNeutral/LiveLaw citation number not independently confirmed at the time of writing (report behind LiveLaw's premium paywall) — court, bench, date and holding verified against LiveLaw, Bar & Bench and The Tribune's reporting
Key SectionsPunjab Land Preservation Act, 1900; Supreme Court's 2014 demarcation direction in B.S. Sandhu v. Government of India

What the case was about

Sixteen villages sit in the Shivalik foothills bordering Chandigarh — Karoran, Nada and Siswan among them — in what is often called the Chandigarh periphery, a belt of ecologically fragile land that has been the subject of litigation and administrative promises for over a decade. In 2014, the Supreme Court directed the Punjab government to carry out a formal demarcation of forest land in this area, in proceedings connected to B.S. Sandhu v. Government of India. Twelve years on, that demarcation exercise still had not been completed, even as construction activity, land transfers and mutations continued in the same villages.

The matter reached the Punjab & Haryana High Court through a public interest litigation, Sukhraj Kaur Chahal v. State of Punjab, connected with two other writ petitions, CWP-PIL-340-2025 and CWP-3576-2025. The petitioners' grievance was straightforward: without a completed demarcation exercise, nobody — not the State, not the Forest Department, not prospective buyers — can say with certainty which parcels of land in these villages are forest land subject to statutory protection and which are not. In that vacuum, construction and land dealings had continued unchecked, effectively pre-empting the outcome of a demarcation the State itself was obligated to carry out.

What the court held

A Division Bench headed by Acting Chief Justice Ashwani Kumar Mishra, sitting with Justice Rohit Kapoor, restrained all construction and development activity across the sixteen villages and froze land mutations and fresh transfers, pending completion of the demarcation exercise. The Court directed the State of Punjab to complete the demarcation within six weeks, expressing evident frustration at the delay. Reports of the hearing quote the Bench as saying, in substance, that the Court "can't shut its eyes" to construction and transfers continuing in an area where the very extent of protected forest land remains undetermined more than a decade after the Supreme Court's direction.

The order is interim and protective in character — it does not finally determine which land is forest and which is not, since that determination depends on the demarcation exercise itself. What it does is stop the clock: no further construction, no further land transfers, until the State does the exercise it has owed the Court since 2014. The six-week deadline signals that further delay will not be tolerated, and the freeze on mutations closes the practical loophole through which land in an undemarcated zone could otherwise change hands and be built upon while the underlying legal status remained unresolved.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

This order concerns villages on the Punjab side of the Chandigarh periphery, but the Punjab & Haryana High Court's rulings bind courts across both states, and the underlying problem it addresses — undemarcated forest land sitting alongside active real estate markets — is a familiar one in Haryana too, from the Aravalli belt in the south to the Morni hills and Kalesar forest tracts closer to the Shivaliks. Advocates handling title verification, sale deeds or mutation applications for clients buying agricultural or hill-adjacent land anywhere near a notified or disputed forest boundary should treat this order as a reminder that demarcation status is not a formality; it can determine whether a transaction or a construction is even legally permissible.

For practitioners who act for panchayats, colonizers or individual landowners in Haryana's own periphery-control areas, the order is also a useful precedent on interim relief technique in environmental PILs: rather than waiting to adjudicate the merits of a demarcation dispute, the Court froze the status quo and fixed a hard compliance deadline for the State. That template — injunction against construction and mutation, coupled with a time-bound direction to the government machinery — is one advocates can cite when seeking similar protective orders in Haryana matters involving shamlat deh, common land or forest fringe disputes where State inaction has allowed facts on the ground to outrun the law. It is a reminder, too, that clients purchasing land near any forest boundary should be advised to seek a demarcation or land-use status report before registration, not after.

Statutory cross-references

The order draws on the Punjab Land Preservation Act, 1900, which continues to apply to forest and hill-erosion-prone land in the erstwhile composite Punjab, including parts of present-day Haryana, and on the Supreme Court's continuing supervisory directions on forest demarcation issued in the Godavarman line of cases and specifically in B.S. Sandhu v. Government of India (2014). These sit alongside the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 (renamed the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam after the 2023 amendment) and the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, both of which govern any change of land use or construction activity on land that is ultimately found to be forest. This is a civil and environmental-law matter rather than a criminal one, so there is no BNS/BNSS cross-reference here; the operative civil-procedure analogy is to interim injunctive relief of the kind granted under Order XXXIX of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, though this order was passed in the Court's PIL and writ jurisdiction under Article 226 of the Constitution rather than in a civil suit.
