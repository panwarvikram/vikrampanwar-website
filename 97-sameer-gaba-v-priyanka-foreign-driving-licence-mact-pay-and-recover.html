Sameer Gaba v. Priyanka & Ors.: P&H High Court Says a Foreign Driving Licence Without an International Permit Won't Defeat MACT Liability

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSameer Gaba v. Priyanka and Others
BenchJustice Deepak Gupta, Punjab & Haryana High Court
Date8 September 2026
CitationReported by LiveLaw, Bar & Bench & Law Trend, 8 September 2026
Key SectionsSs. 3, 149, 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

On 31 August 2015, a car allegedly overtaking on a public road struck a motorcycle from behind, and a nine-month-old child, Jivika, died in the accident. The Motor Accident Claims Tribunal awarded her family ₹2,49,500 with 9% interest, holding the driver and the vehicle's owner jointly and severally liable. But the Tribunal added an important qualification: since the driver did not possess a valid and effective licence to drive in India, the insurer was directed to satisfy the award first, with liberty to recover the amount from the driver and owner afterward — the standard "pay and recover" arrangement under the Motor Vehicles Act.

The driver appealed to the High Court on two fronts. First, he argued he was not driving the vehicle at all, relying on additional evidence that the child's father, who had identified him as the driver before the Tribunal, later gave inconsistent testimony in the parallel criminal trial and said he could not identify the driver — testimony that led to the appellant's acquittal in the criminal case. Second, he argued that even if he had been driving, he held a valid Australian driving licence, issued in Victoria and valid until April 2017, and that the Tribunal was wrong to disregard it merely because it was not an Indian licence. The insurer resisted both grounds, pointing out that Tribunal findings rest on the civil standard of preponderance of probabilities rather than the criminal standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt, and that the appellant had admitted in cross-examination that he held no Indian licence and could produce no international driving permit or Indian authorisation for the Australian one.

What the court held

Justice Deepak Gupta rejected both grounds and dismissed the appeal. On the identity of the driver, the Court held that a criminal acquittal "does not ipso facto exonerate the appellant from civil liability arising out of the same occurrence," precisely because the two proceedings operate on different standards of proof. Weighed against the father's later, inconsistent statement in the criminal trial were the contemporaneous FIR naming the appellant as the driver, the police charge-sheet, and the fact that the appellant himself had been arrested and charge-sheeted without ever alleging false implication to any authority at the time. On that fuller picture, the Tribunal's finding that the appellant was driving the vehicle was not rendered perverse.

On the licence question, the Court held that Section 3 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 requires an effective driving licence authorising the holder to drive in India, and that a foreign licence's validity in its country of issue does not automatically confer a right to drive here. The appellant had not shown that his Australian licence met the statutory requirements applicable to a visiting foreign national, and had not produced any accompanying international driving permit or Indian endorsement. Relying on the Court's own earlier ruling in Dilpreet Singh v. United India Insurance Co. (2015), where a Canadian licence without Indian endorsement was held similarly insufficient, and on the Supreme Court's decisions in National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Swaran Singh (2004) and Shamanna v. Oriental Insurance Co. (2018), the Court held that once it stands established that a vehicle was driven without a valid licence, the insurer is entitled to avoid its contractual indemnity obligation towards the insured — but remains bound by its statutory duty to first satisfy the award in favour of the third-party claimant, and only then recover from the driver and owner. The dismissal of the appeal, the Court clarified, would not disturb the claimant family's right to receive and retain the compensation already awarded.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

Haryana's border districts, including Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani, have a significant NRI and returnee population, and it is common to encounter drivers who hold only a foreign licence — from the Gulf, Australia, Canada, the UK or the United States — without ever having converted it to an Indian licence or obtained the international driving permit that Indian law requires for it to be valid here. This judgment is a clear, current reminder that such a licence, however genuine in its country of origin, does not by itself satisfy Section 3 of the Motor Vehicles Act, and that driving without statutory compliance exposes both the driver and, through the pay-and-recover mechanism, ultimately the driver's own pocket to liability the insurer would otherwise have covered.

For claimants' counsel, the case is a useful template for structuring a claim where the driver's licence status is in doubt: plead the absence of a valid Indian licence or international permit clearly, and seek a pay-and-recover direction so that the victim's compensation is not held hostage to a prolonged dispute over the driver's licence, insurer liability, or a parallel criminal trial. For counsel defending drivers or owners, the case is equally a caution that an acquittal in a criminal trial arising from the same accident carries only limited weight before the Tribunal, and cannot substitute for actually establishing that the licence held meets the statutory bar.

Statutory cross-references

The compensation claim itself proceeds under Sections 166 and 168 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, while Section 3 fixes the licensing requirement and Section 149 supplies the pay-and-recover mechanism that protects a third-party claimant even where the insurer could otherwise have avoided liability. The parallel criminal trial referred to in the judgment, arising from the same accident, would today be prosecuted under Section 106, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (causing death by negligence, corresponding to the erstwhile Section 304-A, IPC), read with the relevant provisions of the Motor Vehicles Act; that criminal track remains legally distinct from, and largely irrelevant to, the compensation determination made by the Claims Tribunal.
