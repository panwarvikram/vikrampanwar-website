Rajjan Lal @ Rajanu v. State of Uttar Pradesh: Supreme Court Says Mere Presence in an FIR Cannot Justify Summoning Additional Accused Under Section 319

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Rajjan Lal @ Rajanu v. State of Uttar Pradesh & Ors.
BenchJustices Ahsanuddin Amanullah & K. Vinod Chandran
Date9 December 2025
Citation2025 INSC 1421 : 2026(1) RCR (Criminal) 99
Key SectionsSection 319, Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973

What the case was about

The complainant, riding a bicycle with his son on the back, alleged that a jeep carrying five named persons deliberately rammed into them, injuring both father and son. The FIR named all five occupants of the jeep. Yet when the police completed investigation, the chargesheet was filed against only one of them — the driver. The other four were let off after investigation found no evidence connecting them to the incident beyond simply being named in the FIR as passengers in the vehicle.

The complainant applied under Section 319 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, asking the trial court to exercise its power to summon the four uncharged persons as additional accused once the trial had begun. Section 319 allows a court, if it appears from the evidence that a person not currently an accused has committed an offence, to proceed against that person as well — even mid-trial. The Additional Sessions Court and, on appeal, the High Court both declined to summon the four. The complainant carried the matter to the Supreme Court, arguing that all five had been seen sitting in the vehicle and that their guilt or innocence could only fairly be determined through a full trial rather than screened out at the investigation stage.

Notably, the Supreme Court itself had earlier flagged concern, in the course of these proceedings, about a chargesheet naming only one accused out of five FIR-named persons, and had directed the State to conduct an independent, higher-level review of the investigation. That review, too, confirmed that only the driver's involvement could be established.

What the court held

By the time the matter reached the Supreme Court, the trial itself had progressed considerably — the complainant and his son had already been examined as PW-1 and PW-2. Critically, their sworn testimony in court went only so far as to say that the sole chargesheeted accused had driven the vehicle. Neither witness's deposition, nor even their original statements to police, asserted that any of the other four were inside the jeep at the actual moment of the collision, or that they did anything at all to bring about the incident.

The Court drew a sharp distinction between being named in an FIR as having been seen in a vehicle at some earlier point, and being shown, through evidence led at trial, to have participated in or intended the offence. As the Bench put it, even assuming the four others had at some point been inside the vehicle, "there is nothing to suggest that there was a criminal intent on the part of the four persons travelling in the vehicle." Mere presence, without more, does not establish complicity.

The judgment also underscored the procedural posture that undercut the complainant's case: he had made no effort to file a protest petition against the chargesheet naming only the driver, despite being fully aware at that stage that the other four had been excluded. Having allowed the trial to proceed on that basis and having watched it advance to an evidentiary stage that supported only the driver's culpability, the complainant could not now expect the court to exercise its suo motu Section 319 power on the strength of accusations that the evidentiary record itself did not bear out. The Court held plainly: "Section 319 of the Cr.P.C. is a suo motu power conferred on the Court, which though could be invoked by the complainant, it would depend upon the satisfaction of the Court based on the evidence led at the time of trial" — and that power "cannot be lightly invoked on the mere accusation made by the complainant." The Special Leave Petition was dismissed, with the Court expressly clarifying that its observations would not bind the trial court's final disposal of the matter against the one accused actually facing trial.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Multi-accused FIRs where the police ultimately chargesheet only some of the named persons are extremely common in Haryana's district courts, particularly in road accident, assault, and group-crime cases where families or rival groups name every person present at a gathering. This judgment is a useful, recent reaffirmation of the evidentiary threshold for Section 319 applications: a complainant cannot simply point back to the FIR and demand that everyone named in it be put on trial. The application must be anchored in what the witnesses actually say on oath during the trial itself.

For complainants' counsel, the practical lesson is procedural discipline at the earliest stage — if the chargesheet omits persons you believe are genuinely involved, the correct and timely remedy is a protest petition against the chargesheet, not a wait-and-watch approach followed by a belated Section 319 application once trial evidence has already crystallised against a narrower set of facts. For defence counsel resisting Section 319 applications, this ruling is directly citable authority that mere presence at a scene, without evidence of an overt act or criminal intent brought out through examination-in-chief or cross-examination, falls well short of the threshold Section 319 demands.

Statutory cross-references

Section 319 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 has been substantially reproduced as Section 358 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS), preserving the court's power to proceed against any person appearing from the evidence to have committed an offence, though not put on trial as an accused. The evidentiary threshold discussed in this judgment — requiring more than a mere FIR-level accusation, and something approaching a strong prima facie case emerging from trial evidence — continues to apply under the renumbered BNSS provision, since the substance of the power and its constitutional safeguards for the newly summoned person remain unchanged by the 2023 recodification.
