Shishu Pal @ Shish Ram v. Surjeet: Supreme Court Creates a New "Loss of Domestic Care" Head, Values a Homemaker's Work at ₹30,000 a Month

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseShishu Pal @ Shish Ram & Ors. v. Surjeet & Ors.
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol & N. Kotiswar Singh
Date11 June 2026
Citation2026 INSC 634 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 617
Key SectionsSs. 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

On 25 November 2001, a woman travelling from Sirsa to Fatehabad was killed in a road accident caused by the rash and negligent driving of the offending vehicle. The driver's negligence was never seriously disputed. What was disputed, for the next twenty-five years, was how much her life — lived entirely as a homemaker, with no proven independent income — was worth in law.

The Motor Accident Claims Tribunal, Sirsa, awarded her legal heirs a mere ₹2.42 lakh in December 2003. The family appealed to the Punjab & Haryana High Court, where the matter sat for nearly two decades, partly because thousands of case records were destroyed in a 2011 fire in the High Court's record room. The High Court finally enhanced the award to ₹8.43 lakh with 7.5% interest in December 2024. Still dissatisfied, the claimants moved the Supreme Court. Along the way, they had argued the deceased earned about ₹3,000 a month from knitting and stitching, but were unable to prove this with evidence, so the Supreme Court proceeded on the footing that she was a homemaker with no independently proven income.

What the court held / the legal position

A Bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and N. Kotiswar Singh used the appeal to do two things: fix the compensation in this specific case, and lay down a structured, forward-looking method for valuing a homemaker's unpaid labour in every future motor accident claim.

The Court held that treating a homemaker as merely "dependent" on the earning members of a household inverts reality. As the Bench put it, "it is ironic to describe a homemaker as dependant on earning members, when, in reality the household's functioning depends substantially on the homemaker." It noted that unpaid domestic and care work accounts for roughly 16 billion hours globally every day, and that women's unpaid caregiving work is estimated to contribute between 15% and 17% of India's GDP despite going entirely unrecognised in economic terms. The existing practice of compensating a homemaker's death mainly through the "loss of consortium" head was found inadequate, since consortium compensates emotional loss and does not capture the managerial and economic value of running a household.

To fix this, the Court created a new, distinct head of compensation: "Loss of Domestic Care." It identified three components within this head — loss of household management (the day-to-day domestic labour that has real economic value even though unpaid), loss of maternal support (the guidance, supervision and developmental support children lose, beyond emotional affection), and loss of spousal support (the assistance and partnership a spouse loses in running family life). Where all three factors are present, the Court directed that ₹30,000 per month be treated as the basic minimum "stand-in" monthly income for a homemaker with no independently proven earnings, to be revised upward by 10% cumulatively every three years, and to be added on top of any independently proven income if the homemaker was also earning.

Applying this to the facts, the Court computed ₹30,000/month as annual income of ₹3,60,000, added 40% for future prospects to reach ₹5,04,000, applied a multiplier of 16 to arrive at ₹80,64,000, deducted 25% for personal expenses leaving ₹60,48,000, and added conventional heads — loss of consortium (₹1,93,600), loss of estate and funeral expenses (₹18,150 each) — for a final award of ₹62,77,900, against the ₹8.43 lakh the High Court had granted.

The Bench also used the occasion to flag the chronic delay plaguing MACT litigation, observing that appeals under the Motor Vehicles Act — a beneficial legislation meant to deliver timely relief — ordinarily should not remain pending before a High Court for more than four years, whereas the average pendency nationally runs to roughly eight years before High Courts and six years before Tribunals. It directed Chief Justices of all High Courts to list long-pending MACT appeals by age, give priority to matters pending beyond four years, and assess whether additional MACT benches are needed. Finally, in the judgment's most quoted line, the Court expressed the hope that "the word housewife/homemaker is, in future, in recognition of the contributions of the lady of the house, now used as 'Nation Builder.'"

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

This ruling changes how every homemaker-death MACT claim should be pleaded and argued going forward, including matters before the district Tribunals at Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani. Claimants no longer need to strain to prove a small, hard-to-document income from tailoring, dairy work or piece-rate labour just to avoid a token notional-income award; the ₹30,000/month "Loss of Domestic Care" figure now operates as a floor, applicable regardless of whether any such income is separately proved, and stacks on top of it where it is.

Practitioners handling pending appeals or fresh claims involving a deceased or disabled homemaker should now plead "Loss of Domestic Care" as a distinct head, separate from and in addition to loss of consortium, loss of estate and funeral expenses, and should recompute quantum using the multiplier method the Court has now formalised. Given how frequently insurers and even Tribunals in Haryana have historically anchored homemaker income to a bare, dated notional figure, this judgment is likely to become the standard citation in every enhancement appeal from now on. Equally, the Court's delay directions give practitioners fresh ground to seek priority listing for old MACT appeals gathering dust before the High Court — a real and recurring problem in this jurisdiction.

Statutory cross-references

The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 is a special statute and is unaffected by the transition from the IPC/CrPC to the BNS/BNSS, so no criminal-law cross-mapping applies here. The judgment operates within the existing structured-formula framework under Sections 166 and 168 of the Motor Vehicles Act, and builds on the multiplier methodology previously settled in Sarla Verma v. DTC and National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Pranay Sethi, adding the new "Loss of Domestic Care" head to the conventional heads (loss of consortium, loss of estate, funeral expenses) already recognised in that line of authority.
