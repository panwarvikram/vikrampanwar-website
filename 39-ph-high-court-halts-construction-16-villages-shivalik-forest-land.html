"Can't Shut Our Eyes": P&H High Court Halts Construction and Land Transfers in 16 Shivalik Villages Near Chandigarh

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSuo Motu Cognizance (2004) & connected PILs/writ petition re: Karoran & 15 other villages, Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchActing Chief Justice Ashwani Kumar Mishra and Justice Rohit Kapoor
DateOrder reported 24 July 2026
CitationNeutral citation not yet available in open reporting at the time of writing — see disclaimer
Key SectionsPunjab Land Preservation Act, 1900 · Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 · principle from B.S. Sandhu v. Government of India (Supreme Court, 2014 directions)

What the case was about

This litigation traces back more than two decades, to a 22 January 2004 newspaper report alleging that a club in Karoran village — then in Ropar district, now in Mohali — had been built on forest land in violation of law. The High Court took suo motu cognizance in 2004, examined the land under the Punjab Land Preservation Act, 1900 (the "PLP Act"), and found that the entire village land, roughly 3,700 acres, was shown in revenue records as forest land, directing that it be preserved as such.

That order did not survive appeal. The Supreme Court, in B.S. Sandhu v. Government of India, held that mere inclusion of an area within the PLP Act's coverage did not automatically mean the land was "forest land" in the legal sense. What mattered was the actual nature of the land as it stood on the date the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 came into force — a factual question requiring a proper demarcation exercise. The Supreme Court accordingly directed the State of Punjab, back in 2014, to carry out that demarcation. Twelve years on, according to the High Court's own findings, it still had not been done.

Meanwhile, a bunch of public interest litigations and a writ petition brought the ground reality back before the High Court: while a few restaurants operating in the area had been shut down, a large number of other commercial activities were allowed to flourish, with what the petitioners called "massive constructions" being permitted across sixteen villages in the ecologically fragile Shivalik foothills ringing Chandigarh — Karoran, Nada, Parch, Sunk, Majrian, Choti Bari Nagal, Parol, Siswan, Pallanpur, Saini Majra, Dulwan, Burana, Gochar, Mirzapur, Tarapur and Sultanpur. The petitioners went further, alleging collusion by state officials, some of whom, it was contended, had themselves secured prime farmland in the area.

What the court held

The Bench of Acting Chief Justice Ashwani Kumar Mishra and Justice Rohit Kapoor did not mince words. Revenue and other state records, the Court observed, prima facie showed land across all sixteen villages as forest land, and the demarcation exercise the Supreme Court had ordered in 2014 remained undone. Holding that the manner in which the area was being commercialised "not only violated the Punjab Land Preservation Act, 1900, but also reflected failure on the part of the state to preserve the environment," the Court said it "could not shut its eyes to the grievances" raised before it.

The operative directions were sweeping. First, all construction and development activity in the sixteen villages stands restrained. Second, the transfer or alienation of land in the area is prohibited, and no mutation entries are to be recorded, for a period of six weeks or until the forest land identification exercise is completed, whichever is later. Third, the Punjab Chief Secretary was directed to constitute a dedicated team to determine the extent of land that qualified as forest as on the date the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 came into force — reviving, in effect, the very exercise the Supreme Court had ordered over a decade earlier. Fourth, the entire revenue record of the sixteen villages was ordered into the safe custody of two senior officers heading that exercise, with photocopies to be deposited with the High Court's Registrar-General within a week. The Deputy Commissioner, Mohali, was directed to personally appear with the records at the next hearing. And critically, the Court warned that any violation of its directions against land transfer would be treated as contempt of court.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Though this order concerns villages on the Punjab side of the tri-city, the legal question it revives — how "forest land" is identified under the Punjab Land Preservation Act, 1900 and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, and what a revenue entry as "forest" or "gair mumkin pahar" means for marketability of title — is directly relevant across the Shivalik belts running through Panchkula, Yamunanagar and parts of Ambala in Haryana. The PLP Act applies to notified areas in both states, and the B.S. Sandhu principle, that a PLP Act notification does not by itself establish forest status, is the governing precedent any Haryana practitioner would invoke in a similar dispute.

For civil litigators handling sale, partition or mutation matters involving land anywhere near a PLP Act-notified belt, this order is a reminder to check three things before advising a client: whether the specific khasra numbers fall within land notified under the PLP Act; whether any court-ordered demarcation of forest land under the 1980 Act has actually been completed for that revenue estate, since an incomplete demarcation can freeze transactions indefinitely, as it now has for these sixteen villages; and whether the property has any history of suo motu or PIL litigation attached to it, since such orders bind subsequent purchasers and can render a seemingly clean sale deed vulnerable to a contempt petition down the line.

The order is also a useful precedent for environmental and public-interest practice generally: it shows a constitutional court willing to revive and enforce a decade-old Supreme Court direction that state machinery had allowed to lapse, using a construction and transfer freeze across an entire cluster of villages rather than village-by-village litigation. That template — freeze first, demarcate second — could plausibly be replicated in Haryana's own forest-fringe belts if similar non-compliance with pending demarcation directions comes to light.

Statutory cross-references

This matter arises entirely under special and central land/forest legislation rather than the criminal codes, so there is no BNS/BNSS cross-reference applicable here. The two operative statutes remain the Punjab Land Preservation Act, 1900 (a pre-independence enactment still in force, applicable to notified areas in both Punjab and Haryana following the 1966 reorganisation) and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, a central enactment that governs the diversion of forest land for non-forest use and remains unchanged since the recent 2023 amendments, which primarily affected definitions of "forest land" for certain categories of pre-1980 land use rather than the demarcation exercise at issue here.
