R v. E & Anr.: Supreme Court Frames Nationwide Safeguards Against POCSO Misuse in Custody Battles

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseR v. E & Anr. and connected matters
BenchJustices J.B. Pardiwala & K.V. Viswanathan
Date24–25 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1049 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 982
Key SectionsS. 10, POCSO Act, 2012 · S. 35, BNSS, 2023 · Art. 21, Constitution of India

What the case was about

The parties, referred to in the judgment only as R (the father) and E (the mother) to protect the identity of their minor daughter M, born in 2015, separated after their marriage broke down. Custody proceedings dragged on for years and, by 2023, had been decided in the father's favour twice over — first by the family court and then, on appeal, by the jurisdictional High Court. Soon after the child was physically handed over to the father's custody, the mother lodged an FIR accusing him and members of his household of sexually assaulting M, invoking Section 10 of the POCSO Act, 2012 along with other penal provisions.

Given the gravity of the allegation set against the backdrop of a bitterly contested custody dispute, the Supreme Court did not leave the investigation to the local police. It first directed that a Senior Woman Police Officer handle the probe, and later transferred the matter entirely to the CBI. The CBI, in turn, roped in a specialised evaluation team that included clinicians from NIMHANS to assess the child. That evaluation became central to how the case ultimately turned: the experts found the child's statements to be inconsistent across retellings, unsupported by objective material such as CCTV footage from the relevant period, and — critically — lacking the trauma markers ordinarily associated with genuine sexual abuse. The Court's own reading of the record traced the allegation instead to the friction of a long, unresolved parental conflict rather than to any actual assault.

What the court held

The Bench of Justices Pardiwala and Viswanathan quashed the FIR in its entirety, confirmed the father's custody of the child, and ordered a structured, supervised process to rebuild the mother-child relationship going forward, guided by the same expert committee that had examined the case. It also closed out a related complaint the father had filed against the mother years earlier, treating the whole dispute as one that needed to end rather than spawn further litigation.

What makes the order significant beyond its own facts is the set of safeguards the Court laid down for future cases presenting the same fact pattern — a POCSO allegation levelled against a parent or a co-resident family member, arising in the middle of a matrimonial or custody dispute. The Court was careful not to disturb the POCSO Act's victim-protective architecture at large; the safeguards apply only where both conditions are met together. Within that narrow band, the Court held that registration of an FIR under the Act cannot, by itself, trigger an automatic or mechanical arrest — investigating officers retain their ordinary discretion not to arrest under Section 35 of the BNSS, and that discretion must actually be exercised, not treated as a formality. Before an arrest is affected in the ordinary course, the child should be evaluated by a qualified child psychiatrist or psychologist, ideally through a District Child Protection Unit or a comparable institutional set-up; a departure from this requirement is permissible only for recorded, exceptional reasons. Crucially, the Court did not turn the expert's opinion into a veto over the investigation — the report is meant to inform the investigating officer's judgment, not substitute for it, and the same expert-evaluation step applies equally where a Special Court is examining whether to take cognizance of a private complaint. Underlying all of this was the Court's reminder that the mere fact of a POCSO registration cannot by itself found an inference of guilt, and that both investigators and courts owe the accused an independent assessment of whether the allegation, on the whole record, looks genuine or looks like a weapon picked up mid-custody-fight.

Why this matters for Matrimonial Law practitioners in Haryana

Custody litigation before the district courts in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani follows a familiar arc: once one parent senses that custody is slipping away, allegations of a criminal character sometimes surface for the first time, and POCSO complaints against a father or his relatives are no exception. R v. E gives practitioners on both sides of that fight a fresh, citable authority to work with. For an advocate defending a parent accused mid-custody-dispute, the judgment supports an immediate application seeking pre-arrest expert evaluation of the child, and a submission that arrest cannot proceed as a matter of course merely because an FIR under Section 10 POCSO has been registered. It also gives counsel a basis to argue, at the custody-hearing stage itself, that a pending POCSO case should not automatically tip the welfare-of-the-child balance against their client without a closer look at how the allegation arose.

At the same time, this is not a blanket shield, and it should not be sold to clients as one. The safeguards are expressly confined to cases where the accused is a parent or someone sharing the child's household and a matrimonial or custody dispute is genuinely in progress; they do nothing to soften the law for an unrelated or stranger-perpetrated offence. Counsel appearing for a mother or a child in a matrimonial dispute where abuse allegations are real should be equally ready to show that the threshold conditions do not neatly excuse the accused — that corroborative material exists independent of the custody dispute, and that the expert evaluation, once conducted, supports rather than undermines the complaint. Practically, both sides should expect trial and family courts in this district to start asking, more consistently than before, whether a child-psychiatric evaluation has been obtained before an arrest was made or before cognizance was taken on a private complaint.

Statutory cross-references

The POCSO Act, 2012 is a special enactment and was not touched by the 2023 replacement of the Indian Penal Code and the Code of Criminal Procedure with the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita; Section 10, which deals with aggravated sexual assault on a child, continues in its original form. Where the judgment does interact with the renumbered criminal code is on the question of arrest discretion: the erstwhile Sections 41 and 41-A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, which preserved an investigating officer's discretion against automatic arrest and required documented reasons for any arrest in cases with a lower prescribed sentence, now correspond to Section 35 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which the Court expressly invoked in framing these safeguards. Practitioners drafting applications in Haryana's district courts should cite Section 35, BNSS rather than the old CrPC numbering when relying on this judgment.
