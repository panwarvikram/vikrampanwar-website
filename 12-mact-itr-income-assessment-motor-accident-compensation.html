Rashmirekha Tripathy v. Sriram General Insurance: Supreme Court Lays Down the ITR Rule for Motor Accident Compensation

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Rashmirekha Tripathy & Anr. v. The Branch Manager (Legal Claims), Sriram General Insurance Co. Ltd. & Ors.
BenchJustices Sanjay Karol and Nongmeikapam Kotiswar Singh
Date1 July 2026
Citation2026 INSC 661 · also reported as 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 654
Key SectionsSection 166, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988; Income Tax Returns as evidence of income

What the case was about

The claimant was the widow of a man who ran a construction business and died in a motor accident. In the two assessment years before his death, his filed Income Tax Returns showed annual incomes of roughly Rs 11.6 lakh and Rs 15.06 lakh — figures that moved upward year on year, consistent with a growing business. The Motor Accident Claims Tribunal and later the High Court had to decide, as every MACT case eventually must, what "annual income" to plug into the multiplier formula under Section 166 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988. The High Court had settled on a figure that produced an award of about Rs 1.87 crore. The claimant appealed to the Supreme Court arguing that the income assessment undervalued her husband's earning trajectory.

This is a problem every MACT practitioner in Haryana runs into constantly: claimants rarely carry a neat, single salary slip. Truck operators, shopkeepers, small contractors and farmers with allied businesses file ITRs that fluctuate year to year, and Tribunals across the country had been applying inconsistent methods — some simply averaging the last three years without asking whether the business was growing, shrinking, or seasonal.

What the court held

The Supreme Court used this appeal to lay down a structured, two-track rule for using ITRs to assess income in motor accident compensation claims. For salaried persons, the Court held that the last available ITR ordinarily suffices, since a salaried income is typically stable and verifiable from a single year's return. For self-employed persons and business owners, however, the Court held that an average of the ITRs for up to the preceding three years should ordinarily serve as the reference point — but with an important qualification.

The Bench was clear that mechanical, unthinking averaging is itself an error. Where the ITRs show a discernible growth trajectory — as they did here, with income rising in successive years — a Tribunal cannot simply average the numbers and ignore the direction they are moving in. Doing so would understate what the deceased or injured claimant would likely have earned going forward, which defeats the "just compensation" standard under Section 166. Applying this to the facts, the Court fixed the deceased's annual income at Rs 14 lakh, considering the nature and growth potential of the construction business, and enhanced the compensation from Rs 1.87 crore to Rs 1.97 crore, with interest continuing to run at 6 per cent per annum.

The judgment also touches, without dwelling excessively, on the relationship between tax law and compensation law: an ITR is evidence of income for compensation purposes, not a concession on tax liability, and Tribunals should not be shy of relying on filed returns merely because they were self-assessed.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani see a heavy volume of MACT claims arising out of highway accidents on NH-148 and the state highway network, and a large share of claimants are self-employed — dairy farmers, transport operators, shopkeepers, and small contractors. Until now, Tribunals in this belt have taken varying approaches to averaging ITR income, and insurers have frequently pushed for a flat three-year average regardless of trend, which tends to depress awards for claimants whose income was rising.

Rashmirekha Tripathy gives claimant-side counsel a clear, citable basis to argue against flat averaging wherever the ITRs show growth: the argument is no longer just equitable, it is now supported by a reasoned Supreme Court ruling on the point. Conversely, insurer-side counsel can rely on the same judgment to resist inflated income claims where the ITRs are erratic or declining, since the "growth trajectory" test cuts both ways. For salaried claimants, the ruling also simplifies matters at the evidence stage — a single year's ITR, properly proved, should not routinely be challenged as insufficient on its own.

Practically, this means income-tax returns should be filed as primary exhibits in every MACT case from the outset, with counsel prepared to lead evidence on the nature and trend of the claimant's business where self-employment income is in dispute, rather than leaving the Tribunal to do rough-and-ready averaging on its own.

Statutory cross-references

This ruling arises entirely under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 and general principles of evidence relating to income tax returns; it does not touch the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, since MACT proceedings under Section 166 are civil compensation claims, not criminal prosecutions. Where a parallel criminal case arises out of the same accident (for instance under Section 106 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, corresponding to the erstwhile Section 304A of the Indian Penal Code, for causing death by rash or negligent driving), that prosecution proceeds independently of the compensation claim and does not affect the Tribunal's income assessment under this ruling.
