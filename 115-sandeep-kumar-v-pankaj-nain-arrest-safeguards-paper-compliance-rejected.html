Sandeep Kumar v. Pankaj Nain, IPS & Ors.: P&H High Court Calls Arrest-Safeguard Affidavits a "Farce Exercise," Demands Actual Police Station Records

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court, Chandigarh · Sandeep Kumar v. Pankaj Nain, IPS & Ors. (contempt petitions, batch)
BenchJustice Sudeepti Sharma
Date18 September 2026 (matter adjourned to 29 September 2026)
CitationReported by LiveLaw & The Tribune, 18–21 September 2026
Key SectionsS. 41-A, CrPC, 1973 (S. 35(3), BNSS, 2023) · Satender Kumar Antil v. CBI, 2022 AIR SC 3386 · Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar, (2014) 8 SCC 273

What the case was about

A week earlier, on 9 September 2026, the same Bench hearing this batch of contempt petitions had ordered the Directors General of Police and Chief Secretaries of Punjab, Haryana and the Union Territory of Chandigarh to file affidavits explaining, in concrete terms, how they had implemented the Supreme Court's four-year-old mandate in Satender Kumar Antil v. CBI — the direction requiring every State to put a standing order in place governing how a Section 41-A notice is issued before police arrest someone in a case where arrest is not automatic. That earlier order asked for something specific: not just the standard operating procedures already sitting on file, but the actual proforma used for the notice and its acknowledgment.

When the matter came back before Justice Sudeepti Sharma, the affidavits had arrived — but according to amicus curiae Ankur Mittal, they simply reattached the same SOPs already on the court's record, dressed up as a fresh response. The Punjab Chief Secretary's affidavit fared worse still: its annexures were, in the Court's words, "not even legible." The amicus went further, pointing to the very contempt petitions before the Court as live proof that the safeguards were failing in practice — notices not in the prescribed format, no acknowledgment obtained, and in at least one instance a notice sent over WhatsApp rather than served in any verifiable manner.

What the court held

Justice Sharma was unsparing. She observed that "despite the detailed directions issued time and again by the Courts, much compliances have been made on paper, and affidavits after affidavits have been filed, but minimal has been actually done on ground," and described the pattern of re-filing SOPs as "a farce exercise which looks rosy on surface but is far from the actual realities." The Punjab Chief Secretary's affidavit was rejected outright and a fresh one directed to be filed. The Court held that what it had asked for on 9 September was "substantially more" than production of an SOP that was, in any event, already on record.

To force the question past paperwork, the Court ordered the Station House Officers of three specific police stations — Phase-I, Mohali (S.A.S. Nagar); Sector 11, Chandigarh; and Sector 5, Panchkula — to personally appear with their actual operational records for the period after each jurisdiction's SOP was issued. The list is granular and worth reproducing in full, because it now effectively functions as a template for what "compliance" with Section 41-A is supposed to look like on the ground: an indexed booklet of serially numbered Section 41-A notices in triplicate carbon-copy format; the carbon copies of notices actually issued, retained with the relevant case diary; proof of acknowledgment in the prescribed format; a year-wise register maintained at the police station recording notices issued by each investigating officer; proof of actual service of every notice; a separate statement of every case where an arrest was made despite a notice having been issued and complied with, together with the recorded reasons and the material placed before the Magistrate; and the case files of every instance where an arrest was made without any notice at all, along with the justification offered for skipping it. The three DGPs were directed to ensure the SHOs' appearance and to produce the rules governing preservation, retrieval and eventual destruction of these booklets, and the Court insisted that the officers appearing "must be well versed with the actual procedure/compliances being made in their respective police stations" — not simply sent to read from a file. The matter now stands adjourned to 29 September 2026.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

For advocates handling anticipatory bail or regular bail applications, or petitions under Section 528 BNSS seeking to quash an FIR or arrest on procedural grounds, this order effectively hands practitioners a checklist for testing whether a Section 41-A notice was genuine or cosmetic. Until now, arguing that a notice was defective often meant pointing to its absence or its form in isolation. This order tells you what else to ask for: was the notice drawn from a serially numbered, triplicate carbon-copy booklet, was an acknowledgment actually obtained and preserved, and does the police station's own year-wise register show the notice was logged at the time, not reconstructed afterward. A notice that cannot be traced through that paper trail is now harder for the prosecution to defend as genuine compliance, and easier for the defence to characterise as exactly the "paper compliance" the High Court has just rejected.

It is also a reminder that the burden here runs both ways. Where the police did arrest a client despite an apparently valid Section 41-A notice, the order requires the State itself to maintain a separate record of the reasons for that arrest and the material shown to the Magistrate — material a defence advocate is entitled to demand and scrutinise at the bail stage. Practitioners appearing before the district courts of Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani should watch for whether Haryana's own affidavit, once refiled, discloses a functioning proforma and register system; if it does not, that gap becomes independent ammunition in any case where an arrest is challenged as having bypassed Section 41-A altogether.

Statutory cross-references

Section 41-A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 — the notice-of-appearance safeguard at the heart of this litigation — is now incorporated as Section 35(3) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which requires police to issue a notice directing appearance rather than effecting arrest in cases where the maximum punishment does not exceed seven years, subject to the exceptions recognised in that provision. The final report under the erstwhile Section 173(2) CrPC, referenced in the Court's directions on record preservation, corresponds to Section 193(3) of the BNSS.
