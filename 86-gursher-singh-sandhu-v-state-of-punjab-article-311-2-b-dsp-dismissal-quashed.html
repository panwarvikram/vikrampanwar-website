Gursher Singh Sandhu v. State of Punjab: P&H High Court Quashes a DSP's Dismissal Over the Lawrence Bishnoi Jail Interview, Says Non-Cooperation Alone Can't Skip a Departmental Inquiry

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseGursher Singh Sandhu v. State of Punjab & Ors., Punjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Namit Kumar
DateJudgment dated 1 September 2026
CitationReported by Bar & Bench & The Tribune, 2–3 September 2026 (neutral citation not yet available at time of writing)
Key SectionsArt. 311(2)(b), Constitution of India

What the case was about

Gursher Singh Sandhu was appointed a Deputy Superintendent of Police in the Punjab Police in 2016 and was later posted as DSP (Detective), Mohali. The case against him traces back to the intervening night of 3–4 September 2022, when gangster Lawrence Bishnoi — an accused in the Sidhu Moosewala murder case — was lodged in the custody of CIA Staff, Kharar. A television interview of Bishnoi recorded during that custody was broadcast by ABP Sanjha in March 2023, triggering a controversy over how a man in police custody managed to speak to a journalist on camera.

The department's case was that Sandhu, posted in the area at the relevant time, had facilitated journalist Jagwinder Patial's access to Bishnoi and was present at the CIA Staff premises when the interview was recorded. He denied this, pointing out that he was neither investigating Bishnoi nor overseeing his custody, which was under the charge of the Anti-Gangster Task Force.

What followed was a fairly standard disciplinary sequence that then took an unusual turn. A show-cause notice went out on 19 September 2024, to which Sandhu replied within the week. A second, more detailed notice followed on 14 October 2024; he sought documents and filed an interim reply. On 25 October 2024 he was suspended and simultaneously chargesheeted — but he maintained that only the covering letter was pasted at an old Chandigarh address, even though the department knew his current posting and permanent address. Rather than proceeding with the inquiry already underway before retired Justice Rajiv Narain Raina, the State closed it and dismissed Sandhu outright on 2 January 2025, invoking Article 311(2)(b) of the Constitution — the provision letting the State dismiss an employee without a regular inquiry where holding one is "not reasonably practicable." Sandhu challenged the dismissal before the High Court.

What the court held

Justice Namit Kumar drew a sharp line between the gravity of the allegations against an officer and the far more demanding threshold for dispensing with an inquiry altogether. The judgment reiterates settled Supreme Court law that Article 311(2)(b) has two ingredients, both of which must be satisfied: first, a factual situation must actually exist that makes holding an inquiry not reasonably practicable, and second, the disciplinary authority must record its reasons for that satisfaction in writing. The test, the Court stressed, is not whether an inquiry would be inconvenient or difficult to conduct — it is whether the circumstances made it genuinely impracticable.

On the facts, the Court found that Sandhu had not gone incommunicado. He had answered the first show-cause notice, replied to the second, and actively sought documents to mount an effective defence before he was ever suspended. "The record does not show any instance where the petitioner had disappeared from the proceedings or had rendered himself completely unavailable to the department," the Court observed, adding that if he had genuinely failed to receive the full chargesheet or declined to participate, the department's own remedy was to proceed ex parte — not to skip the inquiry entirely. Crucially, there was no material before the Court to show that Sandhu had threatened or intimidated any witness, tampered with evidence, or interfered with the inquiry officer's functioning — the kind of conduct that has, in past cases, been held to genuinely justify dispensing with an inquiry. Mere non-cooperation or absence, the Court held, "cannot be elevated to a circumstance rendering the holding of an inquiry constitutionally impracticable."

Finding that the competent authority's satisfaction fell short of the constitutional standard, the Court set aside the 2 January 2025 dismissal order and directed Sandhu's reinstatement with all consequential benefits. It did not, however, shut the door on the underlying allegations: the State remains free to resume the departmental inquiry that was already pending before Justice Rajiv Narain Raina against Sandhu and other implicated officers. The ruling is, in that sense, a procedural correction rather than an exoneration on the merits of what actually happened at CIA Staff, Kharar.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

Article 311(2)(b) disputes recur often in Haryana's service litigation, whether the client is a police officer, a Class III or IV government employee, or a panchayat functionary facing summary removal. This judgment usefully restates a principle advocates often argue from memory of older Supreme Court authority: dismissal under Article 311(2)(b) requires the State to show, with a demonstrable and proximate nexus, why an inquiry could not be held — not merely to recite the statutory language and treat non-cooperation as a talisman excusing the safeguard altogether.

For employees' counsel, the case is a template for building a record that defeats a 311(2)(b) dismissal: a client who answers every notice, requests documents, and does not evade service builds precisely the picture the Court relied on here. For departments and their counsel, the converse lesson matters equally — a dismissal order reciting "non-cooperation" without contemporaneous evidence of witness intimidation, evidence tampering, or genuine unavailability risks being set aside years later, with liability for back wages and seniority. The judgment also reminds that quashing a dismissal on procedural grounds does not extinguish the underlying proceeding; departments retain the right to resume a properly conducted inquiry, and employees should not mistake a procedural win for final vindication.

Statutory cross-references

Article 311(2)(b) is a constitutional safeguard for civil servants and is unaffected by the 2023 recodification of the criminal law — the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam have no bearing on it. Practitioners handling parallel criminal proceedings arising from the same facts — for instance, any prosecution connected with facilitating a jail interview or breach of custody protocol — should note that offences earlier charged under IPC provisions dealing with public servants disobeying the law (Ss. 166, 217, IPC) now correspond to Ss. 198 and 236 of the BNS, 2023, while the procedural safeguards on arrest and departmental action against public servants continue under Ss. 197–199, CrPC and their BNSS, 2023 counterparts under Ss. 218–220.
