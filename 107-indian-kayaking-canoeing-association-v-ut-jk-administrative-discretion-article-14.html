Indian Kayaking & Canoeing Association v. UT of J&K: Supreme Court Says a Government's "Discretion" to Grant Permission Is Still Answerable to Article 14

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / CaseSupreme Court of India · Indian Kayaking and Canoeing Association v. Union Territory of J&K & Ors.
BenchJustices Aravind Kumar & Vipul M. Pancholi
Date10 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 981
Key ProvisionsArt. 14, Constitution of India · Art. 226/227 writ jurisdiction

What the case was about

Bilquis Mir is a government employee — a Physical Education Teacher in the Department of Youth Services and Sports, Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir. She was selected by the Indian Kayaking and Canoeing Association, a recognised National Sports Federation, for a coaching assignment with the Indian national team ahead of the Asian Games, 2026, and separately as an International Technical Official for the Canoe Sprint discipline and as Chief Finish Line Judge at the 2026 ICF Canoe Sprint World Cup in Hungary, scheduled for 8–10 May 2026.

For any of this to happen, her employer — the J&K Government — had to relieve her and issue a No Objection Certificate. The Association wrote to the competent authorities on 3 February 2026 asking that her services be made available for the national coaching camp. The request sat undecided. With the World Cup dates approaching, the Association moved the Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh High Court seeking a writ of mandamus directing the government to grant the permission, relieving order and NOC. A single judge granted provisional permission for both the World Cup and the ongoing national coaching assignment. But by the time the government actually responded — issuing an order that treated the matter as an ordinary service question and flagged that the teacher was under a pending departmental inquiry — the World Cup had already come and gone, and the opportunity to officiate there was lost. The Division Bench of the High Court, while directing the Union Government to frame a policy for deputing outstanding sportsperson-employees to national federations, held that once the government had finally passed its order, the delay grievance had become academic. The Association appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The Supreme Court agreed with the High Court on one point: neither a government employee nor the organisation seeking to borrow her services can claim an absolute, indefeasible right to deputation or foreign travel. An employer is entitled to weigh service exigencies, applicable rules, and pending disciplinary proceedings before deciding whether to relieve an employee. A pending inquiry, in particular, is a legitimate factor a government can take into account.

But the Court went further, and it is this part of the ruling that gives the case its wider significance. It held that the absence of a right to a favourable outcome is not the same thing as the absence of a right to fair, timely and non-arbitrary consideration of the request. In the Court's own words, "administrative discretion is not placed beyond judicial review merely because the ultimate relief sought lies within the discretion of the executive. Article 14 of the Indian Constitution operates upon the exercise of administrative discretion as much as it does upon a statutory decision." Where an authority holds discretionary power, it must apply its mind to relevant considerations, exclude irrelevant ones, engage with the actual nature of the request, and decide within a reasonable time.

Measured against that standard, the government's conduct failed on more than one front. The request concerned a recognised National Sports Federation's need for a coach ahead of an identified international event — not an indefinite private arrangement — and had to be considered in that light. The teacher's selection for the World Cup, scheduled for 8–10 May 2026, was known well in advance, yet the government's order refusing permission came only on 19 June 2026, more than a month after the event had already concluded. That delay, the Court held, had by then produced an irreversible consequence: an opportunity that could never be recovered. The Court held that the appeal succeeded on this "broader and independent" ground of failure of timely consideration and non-application of mind to the true nature of a national sporting assignment, distinct from any consideration of the pending inquiry's merits. It set aside the government order to the extent it denied permission, and directed the J&K Government and the Director General of Youth Services and Sports to immediately issue the relieving order, NOC and permission so that the teacher could join and complete her remaining assignment as Coach/Chief Coach ahead of the Asian Games, 2026.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

The specific facts — a sports coaching assignment in Jammu & Kashmir — are unlikely to recur often in a Haryana district court practice. What will recur, regularly, is the underlying principle, and it is a genuinely useful one to have in hand. A very large share of writ practice before the Punjab & Haryana High Court involves government employees seeking some form of discretionary permission from the State: leave to pursue higher studies, no-objection certificates for private employment or foreign travel, deputation to another department or a Central agency, permission to contest an election, or clearance to accept an outside assignment or award. In each of these, the department's stock defence is that the grant of permission is a matter of pure administrative discretion and therefore not something a court can compel.

This ruling sharpens the counter to that defence. It does not disturb the settled position that courts cannot direct a favourable outcome on a matter genuinely left to executive discretion. But it makes clear that the process by which that discretion is exercised is always open to challenge under Article 14 — for unreasonable delay, for failure to apply the mind to the specific and time-sensitive nature of what is being sought, and for treating a request mechanically as routine paperwork when its context demands something more considered. A writ petition seeking to compel a decision (as opposed to a particular decision) therefore remains maintainable even where the underlying grant is discretionary, and unexplained delay that causes an opportunity to lapse irreversibly is, on its own, a distinct and independent ground for relief. Practitioners drafting such petitions for clients in government service in Haryana — teachers, police personnel, health department staff, and others routinely seeking NOCs or deputation orders — now have Supreme Court authority directly on point for framing the delay itself, and not merely the ultimate refusal, as the actionable wrong.

Statutory cross-references

The ruling rests on Article 14 of the Constitution of India (equality before law, read here as guaranteeing fair, reasonable and non-arbitrary exercise of administrative discretion) and the writ jurisdiction exercised by High Courts under Articles 226 and 227. No corresponding Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 cross-reference arises, as the case involves constitutional and service law rather than criminal procedure.
