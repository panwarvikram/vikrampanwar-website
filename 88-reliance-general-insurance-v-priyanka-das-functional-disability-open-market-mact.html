Reliance General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Priyanka Das: Supreme Court Says a Job Saved by Employer Compassion Doesn't Reduce 100% Functional Disability

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtSupreme Court of India
BenchJustices S.V.N. Bhatti & N.V. Anjaria
Date3 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 950 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 897
Key SectionsSs. 166 & 168, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

What the case was about

On 10 April 2011, a motorcycle carrying 33-year-old Sushant Prabhakaran and his colleague Priyanka Das was run over by a heavy goods vehicle near the Brijghat Bridge. Prabhakaran died on the spot. Das survived, but with injuries that would reshape the rest of her life — pelvic fractures, severe degloving injuries, third-degree burns, permanent bilateral blindness from a head injury, and a bowel repair at AIIMS that failed, leaving her with a colostomy for life.

Two claims followed before the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal, Gurugram. Das claimed as Prabhakaran's widow for his death, and separately claimed for her own injuries. On the first claim, the Tribunal found no documentary proof she was his legally wedded wife — her own employment records had listed him as her fiancé — and awarded ₹82.56 lakh to his parents, with a smaller share to Das. That finding was never disturbed through the High Court or the Supreme Court, and it is worth flagging for practitioners: courts will not infer a marriage from cohabitation or a relationship's seriousness alone when the claimant's own contemporaneous records point the other way.

The harder question sat in her personal injury claim. Das kept her job as Deputy Group Manager at IBM Daksh, and her salary even rose from ₹16 lakh to ₹19 lakh CTC after the accident — but only because her employer built a role around her: specialised software, flexible hours, and accommodations that amounted, in the Court's words, to "corporate compassion." The Tribunal treated this continued employment as proof she hadn't really lost earning capacity, and fixed her functional disability at 60%, despite a 100% physical impairment. The Punjab and Haryana High Court raised that to 80% on appeal. Both the insurer and Das went further, to the Supreme Court.

What the court held

The Bench's central holding cuts against a familiar insurer argument in MACT litigation — that a claimant who is still drawing a salary, or even getting raises, cannot really be "disabled" in any way that matters for compensation. The Court rejected that framing outright:

"The calculation of functional disability depends on assessing the victim's earning capacity in the open, competitive market, rather than on retention in a specific, highly accommodated role."

Relying on an independent Medical Board report from Vardhman Mahavir Medical College & Safdarjung Hospital — constituted on the Supreme Court's own direction during the appeal — which certified Das with 100% Permanent Physical Impairment of the whole body, the Bench held that her functional disability, for compensation purposes, had to be assessed at 100%, not the High Court's 80%. The distinction between physical impairment and functional (earning-capacity) disability is not new to motor accident law, but this judgment sharpens exactly how that gap should be read when an employer, out of goodwill, keeps an injured employee on the payroll. The Court noted Das had lost real prospects — promotion to General Manager, Director, or Vice-President — and that her present job survived only because of extraordinary accommodation, not because the open market would have hired or retained her on those terms.

On the connected death claim, the Court left the ₹82.56 lakh award and its apportionment untouched, reaffirming through Sarla Verma, Munna Lal Jain, Pranay Sethi, and Sube Singh that the multiplier in a fatal accident case is fixed by the age of the deceased, not the age of the dependents — a point the Bench called no longer res integra.

On quantum for the injury claim, the Bench recalculated the heads from the ground up: loss of future earning capacity at 100% disability with 50% future prospects (₹2,42,08,416), 24×7 attendant charges for three attendants (₹28,80,000), ₹20,00,000 for loss of marriage prospects, ₹15,00,000 for pain and suffering, and other admissible heads — taking her total compensation to ₹3,77,84,297, with 7.5% interest per annum from the date of the claim petition.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

A large share of injury claims filed before Tribunals in Charkhi Dadri, Bhiwani, and across Haryana involve claimants who, for financial survival, return to some form of work after a serious accident — whether a government job with a sympathetic department, a family business role scaled down to their capacity, or, increasingly, white-collar employment where an employer simply adjusts duties. Insurers routinely use continued employment, or even a stable salary, to argue against a high disability percentage. This judgment gives claimant counsel a clear, citable answer: retention in an accommodated role is not the same as retained earning capacity in the open market, and a Tribunal cannot use one to discount the other.

Equally, the judgment is a reminder to be rigorous about the underlying proof. Das's case succeeded because there was an independent, court-directed Medical Board report certifying 100% PPI — not just a disability certificate from a treating doctor or a general assessment. Practitioners drafting evidence in serious injury claims should, wherever the disability is disputed, push for exactly this kind of independent, above-reproach medical assessment early, since it is what let the Supreme Court override two levels of lower findings.

The judgment's silent lesson on the death claim is also worth carrying into practice: a claim of marital status that isn't backed by documentary proof, and is contradicted by the claimant's own records (like an employment form naming the deceased as a "fiancé" rather than a "husband"), will not survive scrutiny, however sympathetic the facts. Pleadings and supporting documents for dependents claiming a matrimonial relationship must be internally consistent from the very first filing.

Statutory cross-references

The judgment operates entirely under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 — Sections 166 and 168, governing the filing of claim petitions and the Tribunal's power to determine "just compensation." These provisions have no BNS/BNSS equivalent, since motor accident compensation is a civil remedy under a separate special statute untouched by the 2023 criminal law reforms. Where a criminal prosecution runs parallel to a MACT claim (as the FIR at PS Gadmukteshwar did here, against the truck driver), that FIR would today be registered and investigated under the BNSS, 2023, but the compensation proceeding itself remains governed by the Motor Vehicles Act framework discussed in this judgment.
