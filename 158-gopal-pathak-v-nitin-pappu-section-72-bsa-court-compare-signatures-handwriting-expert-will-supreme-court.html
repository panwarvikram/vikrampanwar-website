Gopal Pathak v. Nitin @ Pappu: Supreme Court Says Courts Must Compare Disputed Signatures Themselves Under Section 72 BSA and Cannot Routinely Call a Handwriting Expert (2026 LiveLaw (SC) 1018)

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Gopal Pathak v. Nitin alias Pappu & Anr. (Civil Appeal arising from SLP (C) No. 513 of 2026)
BenchJustice Ahsanuddin Amanullah and Justice Manmohan
Date18 September 2026 (reported 6 October 2026)
Citation2026 LiveLaw (SC) 1018
Key SectionsBharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023: Sections 39, 72 (earlier Evidence Act, 1872: Sections 45, 73)

What the case was about

The dispute began as an eviction case before a civil judge in Gwalior, Madhya Pradesh. The landlord side relied on a Will, and the other side questioned whether the signatures on it were genuine. Years into the trial, which had started in 2020, the respondents applied for the Will to be sent to a handwriting expert. The trial court rejected that request in October 2025, noting among other things that both sides had already closed their evidence.

The Madhya Pradesh High Court (Gwalior Bench) reversed that order in a petition filed under its supervisory jurisdiction. In its view, a court is not itself an expert, so an expert’s opinion was essential before the genuineness of the Will could be decided. The appellant, Gopal Pathak, challenged that view in the Supreme Court. He argued that the court can compare signatures on its own, and that the application was a late attempt to reopen the evidence. The respondents leaned on Thiruvengada Pillai v. Navaneethammal (2008) 4 SCC 530 to say that disputed writing should go to an expert.

What the Supreme Court held

The Bench of Justice Ahsanuddin Amanullah and Justice Manmohan allowed the appeal, set aside the High Court order and restored the trial court’s order. The central point is that the duty to compare disputed writing lies with the court. Section 72 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (the successor of Section 73 of the Evidence Act) lets a court compare a disputed signature, writing or seal with one that is admitted or proved, and reach its own conclusion. A court cannot step away from that task by saying it has no expertise in handwriting.

The Court also reminded that an expert opinion under Section 39 BSA is only opinion evidence. It is not binding, and it is not a mandatory step in proving a document. The High Court was therefore wrong to treat expert evidence as essential to test the Will. As reported, Thiruvengada Pillai was distinguished because it dealt with disputed finger impressions, not handwriting or signatures, and it does not lay down that every disputed writing must go to an expert.

The ruling does not close the door on experts altogether. Reports of the judgment record that a court may still seek expert help where it has a genuine doubt, or where the writing or impression is smudged, vague or very light. The eviction matter was sent back with a direction to expedite it and to dispose of all pending applications.

Why this matters for Civil practitioners in Haryana

Will, sale deed and receipt disputes are routine in the civil courts of Bhiwani and Charkhi Dadri, and an application to send a document to a handwriting expert is one of the most common delaying steps. Such applications are frequently filed after evidence is closed, and they stall a suit for months while the report is awaited. This judgment gives trial courts a clear basis to refuse a routine request and to ask first whether comparison by the court itself will do.

For the side opposing the application, the arguments are now sharper: the court must itself compare under Section 72, expert opinion is not binding, and the stage of the case matters. For the side that wants an expert, the application should do more than assert that the signature is forged. It should point to a concrete reason why the court cannot decide on its own, such as a faint or smudged impression, and it should be filed early. Admitted specimen signatures should be placed on record in time, because the court’s comparison depends on having reliable admitted writing.

Practitioners should also remember that comparison by the court is a matter of caution and not a licence for guesswork. Commentary on the judgment points out that judicial eyesight has limits, so a court that decides on comparison should record its reasons carefully, and a prudent counsel should give it other corroborating material, such as attesting witness evidence, to rest the finding on.

Statutory cross-references

Section 72 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 corresponds to Section 73 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (comparison of signature, writing or seal), and Section 39 BSA corresponds to Section 45 of the Evidence Act (opinions of experts, including on handwriting). The proof of a Will itself continues to be governed by the Indian Succession Act, 1925 and the rules on attestation and execution of documents.
