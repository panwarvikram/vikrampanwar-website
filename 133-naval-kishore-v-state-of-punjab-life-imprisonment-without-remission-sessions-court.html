Naval Kishore & Anr. v. State of Punjab (2026 INSC 1048): Supreme Court Says a Sessions Court Cannot Order a Murder Convict to Stay in Prison for Life, Fixes 25 Years Without Remission

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Naval Kishore & Anr. v. State of Punjab
BenchJustices Aravind Kumar and Vipul M. Pancholi
Date24 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1048
Key SectionsS. 302 read with S. 34 IPC; Ss. 432–435 CrPC (remission and commutation); Swamy Shraddananda (2008); V. Sriharan (2016)

What the case was about

On the night of 27-28 December 2009, a woman and her two minor daughters, aged eight and five, were killed with sharp-edged weapons near railway tracks in Ludhiana. The prosecution case was that the woman’s husband suspected her character and doubted the paternity of the children, and conspired with his brother and a friend, Naval Kishore, to eliminate them. Insurance money was said to be part of the motive, with Naval Kishore promised ₹50,000.

The Sessions Court at Ludhiana convicted all three men on 26 April 2014 for murder with common intention. It did not stop at the ordinary life sentence. It directed that the convicts “remain in prison till the rest of their life”. The Punjab and Haryana High Court dismissed the appeal on 12 December 2022 and left both the conviction and that direction untouched, without examining whether a trial court could add such a rider at all. Two of the three men took the matter to the Supreme Court, which was asked to decide a narrow but important question of sentencing power.

What the Supreme Court held

The bench of Justices Aravind Kumar and Vipul M. Pancholi affirmed the conviction but set aside the “rest of life” direction. The reasoning rests on a distinction that Haryana practitioners will recognise from the Swamy Shraddananda line of cases. An ordinary sentence of life imprisonment is a sentence that the appropriate government may remit or commute under the statutory scheme, subject to the fourteen-year floor in the restriction provision. A fixed term without remission, or a term for the whole of natural life, is a different “special category” sentence that sits between that ordinary life term and the death penalty.

Relying on the Constitution Bench in Union of India v. V. Sriharan (2016), the Court reiterated that only the High Courts and the Supreme Court can impose such a special category sentence. A Sessions Court, however grave the crime, has no power to curtail the remission machinery by its own direction. The bench also drew support from recent decisions including Kiran v. State of Karnataka (2025), Ravinder Singh v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2024) and Navas alias Mulanavas v. State of Kerala (2024).

The Court added a legislative point. When Parliament wanted life to mean the remainder of natural life, it said so expressly, for example in the 2018 amendments for aggravated rape offences. The murder provision was not amended in that way, so a court cannot read that meaning into it.

Having corrected the error, the bench did not simply restore an ordinary life sentence. It weighed the aggravating features, namely a premeditated killing of three persons including two young children by a brutal method, against the mitigating ones: the appellants had already served over twelve and eleven-and-a-half years respectively, and their jail conduct was reported as satisfactory. It held that ordinary life with remission would be inadequate, while confinement for natural life would be disproportionate. The sentence was modified to rigorous imprisonment for 25 years without statutory remission, and the appeal was allowed only to that extent.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

Trial courts in Haryana and Punjab sometimes append “till natural life” or “without remission” to life sentences in heinous cases. After this ruling, that is a ground of appeal in itself. Where a client has received such a direction from a Sessions Court, the appeal memo should challenge it expressly, because, as this case shows, the High Court may affirm the sentence without noticing the defect.

The ruling also shapes how counsel argue sentence before the High Court. The Constitutional Courts do have the power to fix a non-remittable term, and this judgment shows the Supreme Court exercising it with a structured balancing of aggravating and mitigating factors. Time already spent in custody and a clean jail record carried real weight in fixing the number at 25 years rather than something longer. Defence counsel should therefore assemble custody certificates and conduct reports early. Prosecution counsel, on the other hand, should be ready to seek an enhanced special-category term in the High Court instead of relying on a Sessions Court rider that will not survive.

For convicts and families, the practical message is that a murder conviction with a standard life sentence still keeps the remission route open, and the sentencing court cannot close it unilaterally.

Statutory cross-references

Section 302 read with Section 34 IPC corresponds to Sections 103(1) and 3(5) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. The remission and commutation provisions of Sections 432 to 435 CrPC (including the 14-year restriction in Section 433A) now appear as Sections 473 to 477 BNSS, 2023. This case arose under the IPC and CrPC because the offence dates from 2009; the principle on special category sentences applies equally to sentences imposed under the new codes.
