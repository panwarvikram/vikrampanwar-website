Sucha Singh v. State of Haryana: P&H High Court Says Criminal Antecedents Alone Cannot Justify Refusing Bail

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CasePunjab & Haryana High Court — Sucha Singh v. State of Haryana (FIR No. 91 dated 27.03.2022, PS Sadar Ratia, District Fatehabad)
BenchJustice Rupinderjit Chahal
Date11 November 2025
Citation2026(1) RCR (Criminal) 105 : 2025 NCPHHC 155741
Key SectionsSection 483, BNSS · Ss. 307, 34, 427 & 506, IPC · S. 25, Arms Act, 1959

What the case was about

The petitioner was named in an FIR registered at Police Station Sadar Ratia, District Fatehabad, alleging that he and co-accused chased down the complainant and fired gunshots at him with intent to kill, giving rise to charges under Section 25 of the Arms Act, 1959 and Sections 307 (attempt to murder), 34 (common intention), 427 (mischief causing damage) and 506 (criminal intimidation) of the Indian Penal Code, 1860. He sought regular bail under Section 483 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.

His counsel argued that he had been falsely implicated, that he was unarmed and was only alleged to have been riding the motorcycle used in the incident, and that it was a co-accused who actually fired the shot, driven by pre-existing enmity with the complainant. By the time the bail plea was heard, the motorcycle used in the alleged offence had already been recovered from him with nothing further to recover, investigation was complete, the chargesheet had been presented, charges had been framed, and the complainant — the key witness — had already been examined at trial, reducing any risk that the petitioner could influence the evidence. A co-accused on a similar footing had already been granted bail by a coordinate Bench of the same High Court. The State opposed bail, describing the offence as serious, alleging that the petitioner had played an active role, and pointing out that he was involved in multiple other criminal cases, effectively branding him a habitual offender.

What the court held

Justice Rupinderjit Chahal granted bail, anchoring the decision on two distinct strands of reasoning. First, on the question of prolonged incarceration, the Court noted that the petitioner had already spent a considerable period in custody, that investigation was complete, charges were framed, and that the trial was likely to take a long time to conclude. Keeping him in further detention without any realistic prospect of the trial concluding soon, the Court held, would violate his rights under Article 21 of the Constitution. The order relied on the Supreme Court's decision in Dataram Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh, which held that keeping a person behind bars for an indefinite period before guilt is proved amounts to an infringement of the right to life and liberty, and reaffirmed the now-familiar principle that "bail is the rule and jail is the exception."

Second, and more significant for everyday bail practice, the Court squarely addressed the State's argument that the petitioner's involvement in other criminal cases should count against him. Relying on the Supreme Court's ruling in Maulana Mohd. Amir Rashadi v. State of U.P., the Bench held that a bail application cannot be rejected solely on the ground that the accused has criminal antecedents or is facing other cases. As the Supreme Court had put it in that decision, and as this Court reiterated, "it is the duty of the Court to find out the role of the accused in the case in which he has been charged and other circumstances such as possibility of fleeing away from the jurisdiction of the Court" — rather than treating a rap sheet as an automatic bar to liberty. On weighing these factors together with the completed investigation, the framing of charges, the examination of the material witness, and the grant of bail to a similarly placed co-accused, the Court found no useful purpose would be served by continued detention and allowed the petition, while expressly clarifying that nothing in the order should be read as an opinion on the merits of the case.

Why this matters for Criminal Law practitioners in Haryana

This is a Haryana district court matter in substance and origin — the FIR itself is from Fatehabad — and it speaks directly to a pattern seen routinely across Haryana's district courts: the State opposing bail chiefly on the strength of an accused's criminal history rather than the specific facts of the case at hand. This order gives defence counsel a clean, citable line of argument: antecedents are a relevant factor, but they cannot be the sole or decisive one, and the court must independently examine the accused's role in the present FIR, the stage of the trial, the risk of flight or witness tampering, and considerations of parity with similarly placed co-accused who have already secured bail.

For counsel drafting or opposing bail applications in serious IPC/BNS matters involving firearms and multiple accused, the order also usefully illustrates how courts weigh delay: a completed investigation, framed charges, an already-examined complainant, and a realistically distant trial date together build a strong Article 21 case for bail, independent of and in addition to any argument on the merits of the allegation itself.

Statutory cross-references

Section 439 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, under which such bail applications were formerly filed, now corresponds to Section 483 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, the provision actually invoked in this petition. On the substantive side, Section 307 IPC (attempt to murder) corresponds to Section 109 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023; Section 34 IPC (acts done in furtherance of common intention) corresponds to Section 3(5) BNS; Section 506 IPC (criminal intimidation) corresponds to Section 351 BNS; and Section 427 IPC (mischief causing damage) now falls within Section 324 BNS, which consolidates and restructures the mischief provisions formerly spread across Sections 425 to 440 of the IPC. The Arms Act, 1959 remains a standalone special statute unaffected by the 2023 recodification of the general criminal law.
