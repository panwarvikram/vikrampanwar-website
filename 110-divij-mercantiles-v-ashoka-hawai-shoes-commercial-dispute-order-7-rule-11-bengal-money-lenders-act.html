Divij Mercantiles v. Ashoka Hawai and Shoes: Calcutta High Court Refuses to Reject a Loan-Recovery Plaint at the Threshold, Even With a Money-Lenders Act Objection Pending

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / CaseDivij Mercantiles Pvt. Ltd. v. Ashoka Hawai and Shoes Pvt. Ltd., High Court at Calcutta, Commercial Division (Original Side)
BenchJustice Aniruddha Roy
Date7 September 2026
CitationCS-COM/354/2024 [Old CS/41/2022]; IA No. GA/1/2022, GA-COM/7/2024, GA-COM/11/2025
Key SectionsSs. 2(c) & 2(f), Commercial Courts Act, 2015 · S. 13, Bengal Money-Lenders Act, 1940 · O. VII R. 11, CPC, 1908

What the case was about

Divij Mercantiles Pvt. Ltd. sued Ashoka Hawai and Shoes Pvt. Ltd. for recovery of a sum it says it lent in 2020. According to the plaint, the defendant approached the plaintiff for funds in January 2020, and the plaintiff agreed to advance money repayable with interest at 12% per annum, with monthly interest payments and a right to recall the loan on any default. Between 18 January and 18 September 2020, the plaintiff transferred Rs. 65,00,000 to the defendant from its HDFC Bank account, backed by a ledger and bank statements annexed to the plaint. The defendant made one interest part-payment of Rs. 1,37,650 in October 2020, and separately paid TDS on the interest to the government through 2021 — but did not pay the balance interest to the plaintiff itself. A legal notice went unanswered, and the suit followed.

The defendant did not, at this stage, contest the underlying facts. Instead, it applied under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 for outright rejection of the plaint, on two grounds. First, that the dispute was not a "commercial dispute" within the meaning of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, since the plaint disclosed no mercantile document or written loan agreement — only bank transfers and a ledger. Second, that the suit was barred under the Bengal Money-Lenders Act, 1940, because the plaint nowhere declared that the plaintiff held a valid money-lender's licence, without which, the defendant argued, no such suit could be maintained at all.

What the court held

Justice Aniruddha Roy declined to reject the plaint on either ground, and the reasoning on each is worth separating out.

On the commercial-dispute question, the Court read Section 2(c) of the Commercial Courts Act — which defines what counts as a commercial dispute — alongside Section 2(f), which defines "document" for the Act's purposes. Neither provision, the Court found, confines a commercial dispute to one arising from a written contract; the reference to "mercantile documents" is illustrative, not an exhaustive checklist that excludes a lending relationship evidenced only by bank transfers, a ledger, and TDS filings. Whether this particular transaction between Divij Mercantiles and Ashoka Hawai actually qualifies as commercial in nature, the Court held, is not something that can be settled by reading the plaint alone; it calls for a proper fact-finding trial. The Court distinguished the two rulings the defendant leaned on — Prime Hitech Textiles LLP v. Manish Kumar and Meena Vohra v. Master Hosts Pvt. Ltd. — on the ground that in both of those cases, the plaint itself disclosed an "unimpeachable" case for rejection; here, by contrast, the plaint set out an arguable, triable case that could not be dismissed as ex facie non-commercial.

On the Bengal Money-Lenders Act point, the Court turned to Section 13 of that Act, which does not impose an absolute bar on an unlicensed lender's suit. Sub-section (3) allows a court to give a defaulting money-lender an opportunity to pay a penalty within a time the court fixes, and only if that opportunity is not availed of does the power to dismiss the suit arise. Following its own recent ruling in Dutta Vinimay Pvt. Ltd. v. Dinesh Singh, the Court held that this built-in curing mechanism means a suit cannot be thrown out at the very threshold merely because the plaint is silent on a licence — the plaintiff must first be given the chance the statute itself contemplates. The Court also set aside the defendant's reliance on a Supreme Court order in Raj Kumar Santoshi v. Prashant Malik, noting that the order arose in a criminal proceeding (an SLP against a criminal matter) and had nothing to do with the maintainability of a civil recovery suit.

Summing up, the Court held that Order VII Rule 11 CPC is not a mandate to reject every plaint that raises a debatable point; it is triggered only where the plaint, on a meaningful reading, is ex facie barred by law. Where an arguable and triable case is pleaded, the plaint must go to trial rather than be summarily thrown out. The defendant's application for rejection of the plaint was accordingly dismissed, without costs, and the Court made clear it had expressed no opinion on the merits — the defendant remains free to press both the commercial-dispute and Money-Lenders Act objections as issues at trial.

Why this matters for Civil Law practitioners in Haryana

The Commercial Courts Act, 2015 applies wherever a commercial dispute of the specified value threshold arises, and Haryana's district courts — including at Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani — routinely see commercial division suits over unpaid loans, inter-corporate deposits, and trade advances that are documented only by bank transfers, ledgers, and correspondence rather than a signed loan agreement. Defendants in such matters often reach first for an Order VII Rule 11 application, hoping to end the case before discovery even begins. This ruling is a useful reminder, on both sides of the bar, of how high that bar actually sits: a plaint is rejected at the threshold only where it discloses an unimpeachable, ex facie bar, not merely because the opposing side thinks the claim will eventually fail on the facts or the law.

The point on curable statutory bars is equally practical. Wherever a special statute — whether the Bengal Money-Lenders Act in West Bengal or an analogous licensing or registration requirement elsewhere — builds in a mechanism for a plaintiff to cure a defect (here, by paying a penalty), that curing provision defeats an argument that the suit is barred outright at the pleading stage. Haryana practitioners defending or prosecuting recovery suits against non-institutional lenders should check any applicable licensing statute for a similar saving clause before either resisting or pressing a threshold rejection application on that ground.

Statutory cross-references

Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 and Sections 2(c) and 2(f) of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 are unaffected by the 2023 criminal law recodification, since they are civil and commercial procedural provisions with no counterpart in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita or Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita. The Bengal Money-Lenders Act, 1940 remains a West Bengal-specific enactment; Haryana practitioners dealing with an unlicensed-lender objection locally would instead need to check the Punjab Registration of Money-lenders Act, 1938 (as applicable to Haryana) and its own penalty and curing provisions, which may differ in structure from Section 13 of the Bengal statute discussed in this judgment.
