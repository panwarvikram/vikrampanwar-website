Jagga Ram v. Neelam Rani: P&H High Court Says a Wife's Subsisting First Marriage Doesn't, By Itself, Defeat Her Maintenance Claim

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

CourtPunjab & Haryana High Court
BenchJustice Mandeep Pannu
DateOrder dated 6 August 2026; reported 12 August 2026
Citation2026 LiveLaw (PH) 272
Key SectionsS. 125, CrPC, 1973 (S. 144, BNSS, 2023)

What the case was about

The parties married on 12 December 2015 according to Hindu rites, and a son was born from the relationship. The wife's case before the Family Court at Fatehabad was a familiar one in matrimonial litigation: she alleged cruelty, harassment and repeated demands for dowry that eventually forced her out of the matrimonial home, and that her husband, despite having sufficient means, had refused to maintain her or their child. She sought ₹30,000 a month.

The husband's defence took a different, more technical route. Rather than contesting the facts of cruelty or his own financial capacity in any serious way, he argued that the marriage itself was void from the start — the wife, he said, already had a subsisting first marriage on the date she married him, and no decree of divorce had ever dissolved it. He also pointed out that she had earlier filed a maintenance petition on the same cause of action which had been dismissed for non-prosecution, a fact he claimed she had concealed, and separately noted that he had filed his own petition under Section 11 of the Hindu Marriage Act seeking a declaration that the marriage was a nullity, which was still pending. He additionally argued that since the wife had worked as a schoolteacher, she was capable of maintaining herself and needed nothing from him.

The Family Court was not persuaded. On 27 January 2026, it directed the husband to pay ₹5,000 a month to the wife and ₹2,500 a month to the minor son until he attains majority. The husband challenged this order in a criminal revision before the Punjab & Haryana High Court.

What the court held

Justice Mandeep Pannu dismissed the revision and upheld the maintenance order in full. The core of the ruling turns on how the "void marriage" defence interacts with a claim under Section 125 CrPC, and the Court answered it squarely: an objection about a subsisting earlier marriage cannot, on its own, non-suit a woman seeking maintenance, particularly where the parties had actually lived together as husband and wife for a considerable period and a child had been born of the relationship.

The Court leaned heavily on the Supreme Court's more recent word on the subject in Smt. N. Usha Rani and Another v. Moodududla Srinivas (2025), which held that a woman can claim maintenance from a second husband under Section 125 even where her first marriage has not been formally dissolved by a decree, provided she is de facto separated from the first husband, is not drawing any benefit or right from that first marriage, and the second husband was aware of the situation when he entered into the relationship regardless. The husband in this case had relied on the older decision in Savitaben Somabhai Bhatiya v. State of Gujarat, (2005) 3 SCC 636, where the Supreme Court had rejected a second wife's maintenance claim. Justice Pannu held that Savitaben turned on its own facts and did not survive as an absolute bar once N. Usha Rani had traced and resolved the divergence in judicial opinion on the point, including the broader reading of "wife" endorsed in Chanmuniya v. Virendra Kumar Singh Kushwaha, (2011) 1 SCC 141, which cautioned against letting a man enjoy the benefits of a de facto marital relationship while using legal technicalities to escape its obligations.

The judgment also disposes of two smaller but practically important points. First, on the earlier maintenance petition dismissed in default: the Court held that a dismissal for non-prosecution is fundamentally different from a decision on the merits, and since the husband could not show that the earlier proceeding had actually adjudicated and rejected the wife's claim, its dismissal in default was no bar to her filing afresh. Second, on the argument that the wife's employment as a teacher disentitled her to maintenance, the Court held that entitlement under Section 125 has to be assessed on the actual means and circumstances of both parties and the statutory parameters of the provision — being employed does not, by itself, wipe out a maintenance claim, especially when weighed against what the husband himself was shown to be earning. Section 125, the Court emphasised, is a measure of social justice, and "its application cannot be defeated by technicalities where the factual circumstances otherwise justify the grant of maintenance."

Why this matters for Matrimonial practitioners in Haryana

Second-marriage disputes of exactly this kind are routine in the Family Courts across Haryana's districts, and the "void marriage, so no maintenance" defence is one of the first things a husband's counsel reaches for once bigamy or an undissolved first marriage comes to light. This judgment narrows that defence considerably. Counsel defending a maintenance claim on behalf of a woman in a second marriage now have a direct, reasoned P&H High Court authority applying N. Usha Rani to hold that cohabitation for a substantial period, the birth of a child, and the husband's knowledge of the circumstances at the time of marriage can together outweigh the bare fact of an undissolved first marriage. Conversely, counsel appearing for husbands need to understand that simply pleading a subsisting first marriage, without more, is no longer a reliable route to defeating a Section 125 claim outright — the actual "nature and duration of the relationship," in the Court's words, will be scrutinised.

The ruling on dismissal-in-default is equally useful in everyday practice. Maintenance petitions in busy Family Courts are sometimes dismissed for non-prosecution owing to absence of counsel, procedural lapses, or a party's inability to appear on a given date, and husbands frequently try to use that earlier dismissal as if it were a final rejection on merits, hoping it will bar a fresh petition. This judgment is a clean, citable authority that a default dismissal carries no such preclusive effect, and a wife remains free to file again.

The point on the wife's own employment is also worth flagging for Haryana's Family Courts, where it is common for husbands to argue that an educated or working wife needs no support at all. The Court's reasoning confirms that employment is only one factor in the means-and-circumstances analysis under Section 125, not an automatic disqualifier, and that the comparative financial position of both spouses still has to be weighed on the actual evidence.

Statutory cross-references

Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, under which this maintenance claim was filed and decided, now corresponds to Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, for proceedings instituted on or after 1 July 2024. The substantive test applied by the courts — whether the claimant has sufficient means, and whether the person from whom maintenance is sought has "sufficient means" and has neglected or refused to maintain her — remains unchanged under the renumbered provision.
