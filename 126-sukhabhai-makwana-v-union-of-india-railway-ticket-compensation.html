Sukhabhai Nanjibhai Makwana v. Union of India: Supreme Court Says a Missing Train Ticket Can't Defeat a Railway Accident Compensation Claim

By Adv. Vikram Panwar · District Courts, Charkhi Dadri & Bhiwani, Haryana

Court / Leading CaseSupreme Court of India — Sukhabhai Nanjibhai Makwana & Anr. v. Union of India
BenchJustices Ujjal Bhuyan & Atul S. Chandurkar
Date25 September 2026
Citation2026 INSC 1056 · 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 994
Key SectionsSs. 123(c)(2) & 124-A, Railways Act, 1989

What the case/topic was about

In September 2017, the appellants' son, Maheshbhai, was travelling in the general compartment of a passenger train in Gujarat when a sudden jerk threw him off balance. He fell from the moving train and sustained multiple grievous injuries. He was hospitalised for over a month, fighting for his life, before he succumbed to those injuries in October 2017. His parents approached the Railway Claims Tribunal seeking compensation under the Railways Act, 1989 — a statute that treats a fall from a running train, causing death or injury to a bona fide passenger, as an "untoward incident" compensable without any need to prove negligence on the Railways' part.

The Tribunal accepted the parents' case and awarded ₹8 lakh, with 9% annual interest running from the date of the incident. The Railways appealed, and a Single Judge of the Gujarat High Court reversed the award on two grounds: that there was insufficient evidence Maheshbhai had actually fallen from the train at all, and that the absence of any recovered ticket suggested he may have been travelling without one — which, if true, would take his death outside the Act's no-fault compensation scheme entirely. The parents carried the matter to the Supreme Court.

What the court held / the legal position

A Bench of Justices Ujjal Bhuyan and Atul S. Chandurkar restored the Tribunal's award in full. The father had deposed on oath that his son always purchased a ticket before travelling, and that the ticket — along with his other personal belongings — was lost during the five-plus weeks he spent in hospital before he died. The Court found this entirely credible, observing that it was "quite unlikely" a ticket would survive over a month of emergency medical treatment, transfers, and eventual death. Once the claimant discharges this initial burden through consistent, plausible sworn testimony, the Court held, the onus shifts to the Railways to affirmatively prove the deceased was not a bona fide passenger. The Railways failed that test comprehensively: its own witnesses admitted, under cross-examination, that nobody had even searched Maheshbhai's pockets or belongings for a ticket at the time of the incident. Compounding this, the Railways' internal investigation report was prepared a full seven months after the incident, well outside the timeline its own procedure contemplates — a delay the Court treated as further evidence undermining the department's case rather than a neutral gap in the record.

The Bench reiterated the settled position that "mere absence of a ticket" can never, standing alone, defeat an otherwise credible passenger-status claim, and restored the ₹8 lakh award along with the Tribunal's original 9% interest, directing the Railways to pay within thirty days.

Why this matters for MACT practitioners in Haryana

District court advocates in Charkhi Dadri and Bhiwani rarely handle Railway Claims Tribunal matters directly, but the reasoning in this judgment travels straight into everyday Motor Accident Claims Tribunal practice, where an almost identical fact pattern recurs constantly: a rider or passenger is killed or grievously injured, and in the chaos of the accident and the hospitalisation that follows, a driving licence, a fare receipt, a helmet, or an eyewitness's contact details simply go missing. This judgment is a ready citation for exactly that situation. It confirms, first, that a claimant's own sworn affidavit — if internally consistent and inherently plausible on its facts — is enough to discharge the initial burden of proving victim or passenger status, even in the total absence of documentary corroboration. Second, once that burden shifts, an insurer or opposite party that made no effort to investigate at the time — no site inspection, no search of the body or the wreckage, no contemporaneous report — cannot manufacture doubt years later through cross-examination alone. Third, and perhaps most usefully for claimants' counsel, a delayed investigation report is treated as evidence against the party that produced it, not as a neutral or forgivable administrative lapse.

For counsel resisting a "no valid document" defence before a MACT — whether the missing paper is a driving licence, a fare ticket, a registration certificate, or proof of ownership — this judgment supplies the Supreme Court's own template for how much weight a documentary gap should carry against a plausible, unrebutted sworn account, and how much weight it should not.

Statutory cross-references

The Railways Act, 1989 was left untouched by the 2023 recodification of the general criminal law, so there is no BNS or BNSS mapping required here — Sections 123(c)(2), defining an "untoward incident," and 124-A, the no-fault strict-liability compensation provision, continue to operate exactly as they did before. For Haryana practitioners the closer statutory cousin is the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988: Section 140 codifies a similar no-fault liability principle for motor accident deaths and injuries, while Sections 166 and 168 govern the claim petition and award process before the MACT, much as Section 124-A does before the Railway Claims Tribunal. The burden-shifting logic applied here — a claimant's plausible affidavit discharges the initial onus, after which the burden shifts to the party best placed to have investigated at the time — sits comfortably alongside the settled MACT practice of treating an unrebutted claimant affidavit on income, dependency, and circumstances of the accident as sufficient proof absent a genuine, timely challenge from the other side.
